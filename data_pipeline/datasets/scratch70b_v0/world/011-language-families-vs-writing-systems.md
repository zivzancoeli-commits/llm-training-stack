A language family is a claim about **ancestry**. A writing system is a
claim about **technology in use**. They travel by different routes, at
different speeds, and confusing them produces most popular
misconceptions about language.

## What a family means

Languages belong to the same family if they descend from a shared
ancestor language, the way species share a common ancestor. The evidence
is not "these words look similar" — borrowing produces that too. The
real evidence is **regular sound correspondences**: systematic,
exceptionless-ish patterns across large vocabularies.

For example, where Latin has a *p*, English often has an *f*: *pater* /
father, *piscis* / fish, *pes* / foot. That pattern holds across many
words, which is far harder to explain by borrowing than by shared
descent with a later sound change (part of what is called Grimm's Law).
Shared irregular morphology counts too: if two languages conjugate "to
be" with the same strange stem changes, coincidence is implausible.

Some large families:

- **Indo-European**: English, German, Russian, Greek, Hindi, Bengali,
  Persian, Spanish, Irish, Armenian.
- **Sino-Tibetan**: Mandarin and other Chinese varieties, Burmese,
  Tibetan.
- **Afro-Asiatic**: Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Hausa, ancient Egyptian.
- **Atlantic-Congo / Niger-Congo**: Yoruba, Igbo, Zulu, Swahili and the
  wider Bantu group.
- **Austronesian**: Malay/Indonesian, Tagalog, Javanese, Malagasy,
  Māori — a spectacular maritime spread from Madagascar to Polynesia.
- **Dravidian**: Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam.
- **Turkic**, **Uralic** (Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian), **Japonic**,
  **Koreanic**.

Note two entries above. Hindi and Persian are Indo-European — related
to English — while Tamil, spoken in the same country as Hindi, belongs
to an entirely separate family. Finnish and Hungarian are related to
each other but not to their Indo-European neighbours. Relatedness does
not track geography.

## Writing spreads by contact, not descent

A script is a tool that can be borrowed by anyone. It usually travels
with religion, conquest, trade, or a deliberate state decision — which
means the script map looks nothing like the family map.

- The **Latin alphabet** writes English and Spanish (Indo-European),
  Turkish (Turkic), Finnish (Uralic), Vietnamese (Austroasiatic),
  Indonesian (Austronesian), and Swahili (Atlantic-Congo). Six families,
  one script.
- The **Arabic script** writes Arabic (Afro-Asiatic), Persian and Urdu
  (Indo-European), and Uyghur (Turkic).
- **Cyrillic** writes Russian and Bulgarian (Indo-European), Kazakh and
  Kyrgyz (Turkic), and Mongolian in Mongolia.
- **Chinese characters** were adopted for Japanese, a language from a
  different family with completely different grammar, requiring the
  invention of extra syllabic scripts to make it work.

The reverse also happens: one language written in two scripts. Hindi and
Urdu share a spoken core so closely that ordinary conversation is
mutually intelligible, but Hindi uses Devanagari and Urdu uses a
Perso-Arabic script, and their formal vocabularies pull toward Sanskrit
and Persian/Arabic respectively. Serbian is written in both Cyrillic and
Latin. Turkish switched from Arabic to Latin script in 1928 by
government decree — the language did not change, its clothing did.

## Language vs dialect is partly political

Where you draw a boundary between "two dialects" and "two languages" is
not settled by linguistics alone. Mutual intelligibility is the natural
criterion, but it is gradient, sometimes asymmetric, and it forms
**dialect continua** where each village understands the next but the
ends do not understand each other.

Politics fills the gap. Serbian, Croatian, and Bosnian are largely
mutually intelligible and are counted separately for national reasons.
The Chinese "dialects" — Mandarin, Cantonese, Hokkien — are not mutually
intelligible in speech and would be called separate languages by the
usual test, but are grouped as one for reasons of shared script,
literature, and state. Hence the old quip that a language is a dialect
with an army and a navy.

## Practical upshot

When you meet an unfamiliar language, three questions are independent
and all worth asking: *what family is it in* (tells you about grammar
and likely relatives), *what script does it use* (tells you about its
religious and political history), and *who counts it as a language*
(tells you about its politics). Global counts hover around 7,000
languages, but the number depends entirely on where you draw the
dialect line, so treat it as an estimate rather than a fact.
