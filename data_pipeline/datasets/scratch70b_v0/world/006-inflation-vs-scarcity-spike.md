"Inflation" is used for two things that behave differently and call for
different responses. Keeping them apart is most of the skill.

## Definition first

Inflation is a **sustained rise in the general price level** — the
average of prices across the economy, measured by an index such as a
consumer price index built from a fixed basket of goods and services. It
is a *rate*, usually quoted per year.

A **relative price change** is one good getting more expensive compared
to others. If tomatoes double while everything else holds still, the
price *level* barely moves, because tomatoes are a small slice of the
basket.

The confusion arises because a big enough relative price change in a
big enough category — energy, food, housing — shows up in the index and
looks like inflation for a while.

## The monetary story

The classic shorthand is the quantity equation:

```
M * V = P * Y
```

Money stock times how often each unit is spent (velocity) equals the
price level times real output. If M grows much faster than Y, and V is
roughly stable, P must rise. That is the "too much money chasing too few
goods" picture, and it is the right first model for persistent, broad
inflation — especially the extreme cases. Hyperinflations are, without
serious exception, episodes of governments financing deficits by
creating money.

Two cautions. Velocity is not actually constant, so the equation is an
accounting identity rather than a forecasting formula. And "demand-pull"
inflation does not require literal money printing: any source of
spending that pushes demand beyond what the economy can produce at
current prices — a boom, a large fiscal expansion, very low interest
rates — does the same work.

## The scarcity story

Now the other case. A drought destroys a wheat crop; a war closes a
shipping route; a pandemic idles semiconductor plants. Supply falls.
Prices in those categories jump.

Three features distinguish this from monetary inflation:

1. **It is a level shift, not a rate.** If wheat rises 40% once and then
   stays there, the index rises for twelve months and then, with no
   further change, the measured annual rate falls back — the jump has
   dropped out of the year-over-year comparison. This "base effect"
   makes headline inflation fall without any price falling.
2. **It is partly self-limiting.** With a fixed household budget,
   spending more on fuel means spending less on other things, which puts
   *downward* pressure on those other prices.
3. **It comes with falling output, not rising output.** Demand-driven
   inflation shows up alongside a hot economy: low unemployment,
   overtime, backlogs. Supply-driven inflation shows up alongside a
   weak one. That co-movement is the best practical diagnostic
   available.

## Why the distinction has teeth

A central bank raising interest rates reduces spending. Against
demand-pull inflation, that attacks the cause. Against a bad harvest, it
does nothing to grow wheat; it just adds a recession to the shortage.
The textbook advice is therefore to "look through" a one-off supply
shock.

The catch — and this is the part that makes the job hard — is the
**second round**. If a shock lasts long enough, workers bargain for
higher wages to restore purchasing power, firms raise prices to cover
higher wages, and people begin to *expect* continued inflation and act
accordingly. Expectations that were anchored come loose, and a temporary
shock has been converted into a persistent process. At that point the
original cause is irrelevant and the monetary tools apply again.

## Reading a real episode

Real episodes are almost always mixtures, and the weights are genuinely
disputed among economists. The global inflation of 2021–2023 involved
pandemic supply disruption, a shift of demand from services toward
goods, large fiscal transfers, very loose monetary policy, and an energy
shock — and the relative contribution of each is still argued over. Be
suspicious of any confident single-cause account.

Three questions do most of the diagnostic work. Is the rise broad across
categories, or concentrated in a few? Is output strong or weak
alongside it? Are measures of expected future inflation — from surveys
or from bond markets — drifting up, or holding steady? Broad, hot, and
drifting is a monetary problem. Narrow, weak, and anchored is a
scarcity problem that will probably fade on its own.
