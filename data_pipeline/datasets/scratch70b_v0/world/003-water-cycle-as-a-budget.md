The schoolroom water cycle is a loop of arrows: evaporation, clouds,
rain, rivers, ocean, repeat. It is not wrong, but it teaches nothing you
can compute with. The useful version is a **budget**: a set of *stocks*
(how much water sits in each reservoir) and *flows* (how much moves
between them per year). Once you have both, you can derive things the
arrow cartoon cannot tell you.

## Stocks

All figures below are order-of-magnitude estimates that differ somewhat
between sources; treat them as approximate.

| Reservoir | Volume (km³) | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Oceans | ~1,340,000,000 | ~96.5% |
| Ice caps, glaciers, permanent snow | ~24,000,000 | ~1.7% |
| Groundwater | ~23,000,000 | ~1.7% |
| Lakes, rivers, soil moisture | ~200,000 | ~0.015% |
| Atmosphere | ~13,000 | ~0.001% |

Notice how tiny the atmosphere is. All the water vapour in the sky at
once, if it fell everywhere at the same time, would make a layer about
25 mm deep over the whole Earth. Yet global average precipitation is
close to **1,000 mm per year**. The sky delivers roughly forty times its
own contents every year.

## Flows

Globally, evaporation must equal precipitation over the long run,
because the atmosphere cannot store a growing surplus. Both are roughly
**500,000 km³ per year**. Splitting by surface:

- Over the ocean: about 413,000 km³ evaporates, about 373,000 km³ falls
  back as rain.
- Over land: about 73,000 km³ evaporates and transpires, about 113,000
  km³ falls as precipitation.

Land receives about 40,000 km³ per year more than it loses to the air.
That surplus has to go somewhere, and it does: it is **river discharge
and groundwater flow back to the ocean**, and it closes the budget. The
ocean's deficit and the land's surplus are the same 40,000 km³ seen from
two sides. This is the single most useful number in the whole system,
because it is the renewable freshwater supply that everything on land
ultimately draws from.

## Residence time: the trick worth learning

For any reservoir in steady state:

```
residence time = stock / flow rate
```

Apply it to the atmosphere: 13,000 km³ divided by 500,000 km³ per year
is about 0.026 years, or roughly **9 days**. That is the average time a
water molecule spends airborne between evaporating and falling.

That one number explains a lot:

- Weather is short-memory. A drought cannot be caused by the atmosphere
  "running out"; it is caused by changed circulation.
- Water vapour cannot be a long-lived driver of climate change on its
  own. Anything you add is rained out within days, so vapour acts as a
  fast *feedback* on temperature, not a slow forcing.
- Pollutants that dissolve in cloud water get scrubbed from the air on a
  timescale of days to weeks.

Run the same calculation elsewhere and the picture flips. The ocean:
1.34 billion km³ divided by ~413,000 km³/yr is roughly **3,000 years**.
Deep groundwater can have residence times of thousands to hundreds of
thousands of years. This is why "fossil water" is a real category, and
why pumping a deep aquifer is closer to mining than to harvesting: the
stock is large, but the inflow is so slow that on human timescales the
resource does not refill.

## What the budget framing buys you

The cartoon suggests every part of the cycle is equally alive. The
budget shows the system is layered by speed: a frantic 9-day atmospheric
loop sitting on top of a millennial ocean and a near-static ice and
groundwater store.

It also makes local questions tractable. A river basin has the same
structure — precipitation in; evaporation, transpiration, and discharge
out; soil moisture, snowpack, and groundwater as storage. If withdrawals
exceed the inflow, storage falls, and you can predict roughly how long
until a reservoir empties. Whenever you hear a claim about water
"disappearing," the right question is not whether water is destroyed —
it is not — but *which reservoir it moved to, and how long it will stay
there.*
