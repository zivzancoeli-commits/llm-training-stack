Democracies differ in the details, but almost every representative
legislature runs a bill through the same functional stages. Learning the
*functions* rather than one country's vocabulary lets you read any
system, including one you have never seen.

## Stage 0: where a bill comes from

Most legislation that passes is drafted by the executive — ministries
that administer a policy area propose changes to it. Individual members
can also introduce bills, but in systems where the government controls
the timetable, these rarely become law without government support.
Behind the text there is usually a professional drafting office that
converts a policy intention into legal language, because bills must
interlock with existing statutes rather than float free.

## Stage 1: introduction

The bill is formally laid before the chamber and published. This step
is usually procedural — no debate on the merits — but it matters,
because publication starts the clock and lets outsiders read the text.
Many systems require a bill to be publicly available for some minimum
period before it can be voted on, precisely to prevent surprise.

## Stage 2: committee scrutiny

This is where the real work happens, and where outside observers
consistently underrate the action. Chambers are too large to edit text
line by line, so bills go to a smaller standing committee organised by
subject.

A committee typically can: take evidence from officials, experts, and
affected groups; propose amendments clause by clause; and recommend that
the bill proceed, proceed as amended, or not proceed. Committee
composition usually mirrors party strength in the chamber, so a
government with a majority normally has a majority in committee too —
but committee debate is more detailed, less performative, and more
likely to catch drafting errors.

Committees are also the main **chokepoint**. A bill that is never
scheduled for committee consideration simply dies, without anyone having
to vote against it. Killing by inaction is the most common fate of
legislation everywhere.

## Stage 3: return to the full chamber

The amended bill comes back for general debate and votes on remaining
amendments, then a final vote on the whole text. Three things determine
the outcome:

- **Party discipline.** In parliamentary systems, where the executive
  survives only while it commands a majority, discipline is very tight
  — a lost vote can bring down the government. In systems with a
  separately elected executive, legislators can defect more freely.
- **Thresholds.** Ordinary bills usually need a simple majority of those
  voting, subject to a quorum. Constitutional amendments typically
  require a supermajority, and sometimes ratification by regions or a
  referendum.
- **Timetabling rules.** Who controls the agenda controls outcomes. Some
  chambers allow unlimited debate unless a supermajority votes to end
  it, which effectively raises the passage threshold; others let the
  majority impose a strict schedule.

## Stage 4: the other chamber

In a bicameral legislature the bill repeats the process in the second
chamber. Second chambers are usually constituted differently —
representing regions, or elected on a different cycle — so they can hold
a different majority.

Disagreement is resolved in one of three ways: a **joint committee**
that negotiates a single reconciled text both chambers then vote on
without further amendment; **shuttle**, where the bill passes back and
forth until the texts match; or an **override**, where the lower chamber
can eventually prevail after delay. Many systems restrict the second
chamber's power over taxation and spending bills, on the principle that
money should be controlled by the chamber closest to the voters.

## Stage 5: assent, publication, commencement

A head of state signs or promulgates. Depending on the constitution this
is ceremonial, or carries a real veto that the legislature may be able
to override with a supermajority. The text is then published in an
official gazette, and it takes effect — either immediately, on a stated
date, or when a minister issues a commencement order, which can be
much later or never.

## Stage 6: what happens after

Passing the bill is not the end of policy.

- **Delegated legislation.** Statutes often set a framework and
  authorise ministries to fill in detail by regulation. A great deal of
  the operative law is made here, with lighter scrutiny.
- **Judicial or constitutional review.** In many systems a court can
  strike down or reinterpret a law, sometimes before it takes effect.
- **Implementation.** A law with no budget line, no staff, and no
  enforcement body changes little.

Two habits will serve you across any system: ask *who controls the
agenda*, and ask *who has a veto*. The number of independent veto
players — chambers, courts, coalition partners, regions — predicts how
hard it is to change anything, better than the formal description of the
system does.
