Look at a list of the world's largest cities and a striking share are on
a coast, an estuary, or a navigable river near the sea. This is a real
pattern with a mechanical cause, and it is also frequently overstated.
Both halves are worth holding at once.

## The mechanism: transport cost per tonne-kilometre

Before railways, moving heavy goods overland was brutally expensive. A
cart pulled by animals carries a modest load, needs a road, and feeds
its own engine along the way. Water needs no road, and buoyancy carries
the weight for free — a boat's operator only has to overcome drag.
Historians commonly estimate that pre-industrial water transport cost
somewhere between a tenth and a thirtieth of land transport per
tonne-kilometre; the exact ratio varies by period and route, but the
order of magnitude is not in doubt.

Ships also scale unusually well. Drag grows roughly with wetted surface
area while capacity grows with volume, so a bigger hull costs less per
tonne carried. Overland transport barely scales at all: two carts cost
about twice one cart. That asymmetry is why maritime trade concentrated
into a small number of large ports rather than spreading evenly along
the coastline.

## Break-of-bulk and the estuary sweet spot

A port is a **break-of-bulk point**: cargo must be unloaded and
transferred to another mode. Handling is expensive, so activity that can
happen at the transfer point does happen there — warehousing, sorting,
finance, insurance, brokerage, and then the manufacturing that likes to
sit near imported inputs.

The very best sites are not open coast but **river mouths**, which
combine deep-water access with an inland waterway network: London on the
Thames, Shanghai near the Yangtze, Buenos Aires on the Río de la Plata,
Hamburg on the Elbe, Kolkata on the Hooghly. New York's dominance over
rival American ports was cemented when the Erie Canal (1825) gave it a
water route to the Great Lakes interior.

## Then agglomeration takes over

Once a place is large, it grows for reasons unrelated to its original
advantage. Deep labour markets let workers and firms find better
matches. Suppliers cluster near customers. Universities, courts, and
capital markets locate where the people are. This is **path
dependence**: the port explains why the city started, but not
necessarily why it is still there. Many great ports are now cities whose
port is a minor employer.

## Why so many capitals are coastal — and why many are not

Colonial administrations sited capitals where ships from the imperial
centre arrived, because the capital's job was to govern a territory whose
lifeline ran to the sea. That accounts for a large share of coastal
capitals in the Americas, Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Oceania.

But the counterexamples are numerous and instructive: Madrid, Moscow,
Beijing, Delhi, Berlin, Tehran, Ankara, Addis Ababa, Mexico City. Inland
capitals cluster where states formed around a land empire rather than a
maritime one. Interior placement offers defensive depth, control over
land routes, and roughly equal distance to the provinces — and it keeps
the seat of power away from a coastline that a hostile navy can reach.

A third category is the **deliberately built inland capital**, created
to separate political power from the dominant commercial city or to
balance regions: Washington, Ottawa, Canberra, Brasília, Abuja,
Islamabad, Astana, Naypyidaw. Their existence is direct evidence that
geography is not destiny — states chose against the port logic on
purpose.

## What is changing

Containerisation cut port labour requirements dramatically and moved
operations to large automated terminals, often away from historic
waterfronts. Something on the order of 80% of world merchandise trade by
volume still moves by sea, so the maritime advantage in *goods* is
intact. But the fastest-growing economic activities — software,
finance, research, design — ship over fibre, and their location is
driven by talent, universities, and regulation rather than draught
depth.

Meanwhile, the old advantage carries a new bill. Coastal cities
concentrate exposure to storm surge and sea-level rise, and several
major deltas are subsiding faster than the sea is rising, largely
because of groundwater extraction. The geography that built these cities
is now the geography that most expensively threatens them.
