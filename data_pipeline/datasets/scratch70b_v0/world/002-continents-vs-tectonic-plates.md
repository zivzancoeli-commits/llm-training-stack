People often recite "seven continents" and "the tectonic plates" as if
they were two names for one thing. They are not. They are answers to
different questions, and the mismatches between them are where the
interesting geology lives.

## Two different definitions

A **continent** is defined by *what the crust is made of and whether it
sticks up above the sea*. Continental crust is granitic in bulk
composition, thick (typically 30–50 km), and relatively low density
(around 2.7 g/cm³). Oceanic crust is basaltic, thin (about 7 km), and
denser (around 3.0 g/cm³). Continental crust floats higher on the
mantle, the way a thick block of pine floats higher than a thin sheet of
oak. Continents are the parts that float high enough to be dry.

A **tectonic plate** is defined by *motion*. The lithosphere — crust
plus the rigid uppermost mantle — is broken into pieces that move as
near-rigid units over the weaker asthenosphere below. A plate boundary
is simply a place where two of those units are moving relative to each
other, which is why boundaries are marked by earthquakes and volcanoes
and interiors usually are not.

Composition and motion are independent. So a single plate can carry both
oceanic and continental crust, and a continent can be split across
plates.

## The mismatches

- **The Pacific Plate is huge and carries almost no continent.** It is
  overwhelmingly oceanic crust. If plates and continents were the same
  list, this plate would not exist.
- **The African Plate carries Africa *and* a large area of Atlantic and
  Indian Ocean floor.** Its western edge is the Mid-Atlantic Ridge,
  thousands of kilometres offshore, not the coastline. The coastline of
  a continent is almost never a plate boundary; it is a "passive
  margin," a scar from an old rift that has since gone quiet.
- **India is geographically part of Asia but rides its own plate.** The
  Indian Plate is still driving north into Eurasia, which is why the
  Himalaya keeps rising and why Nepal and northern India have large
  earthquakes. Two continents on the same continental landmass, on
  different plates.
- **Iceland straddles the North American and Eurasian plates.** You can
  stand in a rift valley with one plate on each side.
- **Coastal California west of the San Andreas Fault sits on the Pacific
  Plate**, sliding northwest past the rest of North America. Los Angeles
  is on a different plate from Sacramento, even though both are in the
  same state and the same continent.
- **Part of far-eastern Siberia is on the North American Plate**, which
  is a purely geometric fact with no cultural consequence at all.

## Why continents are old and ocean floor is young

Here is the payoff that makes the distinction worth learning. At a
subduction zone, one plate dives beneath another. Dense oceanic
lithosphere subducts readily. Buoyant continental crust mostly does not
— it is too light to sink, so it crumples, thickens, and builds
mountains instead.

That single density fact explains an enormous asymmetry in the rock
record. Ocean floor is continuously created at ridges and destroyed at
trenches, so almost none of it is older than about 200 million years.
Continental crust, unable to sink, accumulates. The oldest surviving
continental rocks are on the order of 4 billion years old, and the
ancient cores of continents — cratons — are the most stable real estate
on the planet.

## How to reason with this

If someone asks "where will the next big earthquake be?", the continent
list is useless and the plate list is the right tool: look for
boundaries. If someone asks "why is there coal here and not there?",
plates matter less than the ancient environment of that continental
block.

Also note that the continent list is itself a cultural convention, not a
measurement. Different school systems teach five, six, or seven
continents, mostly disagreeing about whether Europe and Asia are one
thing and whether the Americas are one or two. The plate list is
empirical but still fuzzy at the edges: there are around seven or eight
major plates and dozens of smaller ones, and the exact count depends on
how small a fragment you are willing to name.
