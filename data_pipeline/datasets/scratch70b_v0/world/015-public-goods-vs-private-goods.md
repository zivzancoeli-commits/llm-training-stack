"Public good" in economics does not mean "good for the public." It is a
technical classification based on two independent properties of the
good itself.

## The two tests

**Rivalry.** Does one person's use reduce what is available to others?

**Excludability.** Can a supplier practically prevent non-payers from
using it?

Two yes/no questions give four categories.

| | Excludable | Non-excludable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rival** | Private good (a sandwich) | Common-pool resource (an ocean fishery) |
| **Non-rival** | Club good (a streaming service) | Public good (a lighthouse) |

## The sandwich

If I eat it, you cannot — perfectly rival. The shop can refuse to hand
it over until you pay — perfectly excludable.

Both properties together are what make ordinary markets work well here.
Excludability lets the seller charge, so there is a revenue signal and
a reason to produce more. Rivalry means the price *should* be positive:
each extra sandwich has a real resource cost, and the price rations the
limited supply to whoever values it most.

## The lighthouse

The classic textbook case. Its light is **non-rival** — one more ship
using it costs nothing and dims nothing for the others. And it is
**non-excludable** in the obvious sense: you cannot beam light only at
ships that paid.

Both properties break the market logic in the opposite direction. Since
each additional user costs nothing, the efficient price is *zero* — but
at a price of zero nobody funds the lamp. And since you cannot exclude,
every captain has an incentive to say "someone else will pay; I will
benefit anyway." That is the **free-rider problem**, and its consequence
is not that nothing gets built but that too little does, relative to
what everyone collectively wants. Hence the standard remedy: fund it
through taxation or a compulsory levy, so contribution is not optional.

## The honest complication

The lighthouse example has a well-known twist worth knowing, because it
teaches something important. Ronald Coase, writing in 1974, examined the
history of English lighthouses and found that many were built and
operated privately, funded by dues collected from ships **when they
docked in port**.

The trick is that the operators did not exclude anyone from the light.
They tied payment to something adjacent that *was* excludable — harbour
access. The economics of Coase's case are still argued over, since
collection relied on legal powers granted by the state. But the general
lesson holds and is broadly useful: **excludability is not a fixed
property of a good. It depends on available technology and
institutions.** Roads were non-excludable until electronic tolling.
Broadcast television was non-excludable until encryption. When
excludability changes, the category changes, and so does the case for
public provision.

## The other two boxes

**Club goods** are non-rival but excludable: a streaming library, an
uncongested toll road, a patented design, most software. Serving one
more user costs almost nothing, but access can be gated. Private supply
works here, yet it is not fully efficient — charging a positive price
for something with near-zero marginal cost excludes people who would
have benefited at no cost to anyone. That tension underlies debates
about drug pricing, copyright terms, and paywalled research.

**Common-pool resources** are rival but non-excludable: fisheries,
groundwater, grazing land, the atmosphere's capacity to absorb
emissions. Here the failure runs the other way. Each user gets the full
benefit of taking more while the cost of depletion is spread over
everyone, so the resource is overused — the tragedy of the commons.
Elinor Ostrom's fieldwork showed the outcome is not inevitable:
communities frequently sustain such resources through local rules,
monitoring, and graduated sanctions, without either privatisation or
central control.

## Using the framework

Two habits make it genuinely useful.

First, **congestion moves things between boxes**. An empty road is
non-rival; a jammed road is intensely rival. A good can be a public good
at low usage and a common-pool resource at high usage, and the right
policy differs between those states.

Second, **classify before prescribing**. If a good is non-excludable,
expect under-provision and look for a funding mechanism. If it is rival
and non-excludable, expect over-use and look for an access rule. Getting
the diagnosis right tells you which failure you are dealing with, which
is more than half the work.
