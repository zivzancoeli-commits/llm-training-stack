## The astronomical part

The Earth turns once on its axis in about 24 hours. That is 360 degrees
in 24 hours, or **15 degrees per hour**, or one degree every four
minutes. "Noon" in the oldest sense means the moment the Sun crosses
your local meridian — the highest point it reaches that day. Because
the Sun can only be overhead at one line of longitude at a time, local
solar noon is a *different instant* at every longitude.

Work the arithmetic. Two towns 100 km apart on an east–west line at
mid-latitudes are roughly 1.4 degrees of longitude apart, so their solar
noons differ by about six minutes. For a farmer, six minutes is
nothing. For a person catching a train, six minutes is the difference
between boarding and watching it leave.

## Why the clock had to be standardized

Before roughly 1840, most towns kept their own local mean time, set by
a sundial and adjusted by a clockmaker. Railways broke this. A timetable
must state one time, and a railway crossing many towns cannot publish a
different noon for each station. British railways adopted a single
"railway time" (Greenwich time) starting around 1840, and the practice
spread. North American railways adopted standard time zones in 1883.
The 1884 International Meridian Conference in Washington recommended
Greenwich as the prime meridian and a system of hour offsets from it.

So the causal chain is: fast transport and telegraphy make *simultaneity
across distance* matter → a shared clock becomes more valuable than a
locally accurate one → people accept that their clock noon drifts away
from their solar noon by up to half an hour or so, in exchange for
coordination.

## Where the politics enter

Nothing about astronomy says a zone must be an hour wide, or that its
edges must be straight. A time zone is a *legal* object: a government
declares what its clocks read. Once you see that, the odd cases stop
being odd.

- **China** spans roughly 60 degrees of longitude — about four hours of
  sun — but has used a single national time (UTC+8, Beijing time) since
  1949. In the far west, official noon can fall long before the Sun is
  overhead. That is a deliberate statement of national unity, at the
  cost of local convenience.
- **India** uses UTC+5:30, and **Nepal** uses UTC+5:45. Half-hour and
  quarter-hour offsets exist because a country can pick the compromise
  that best fits its own span, and because a distinct offset is a mild
  assertion of independence.
- **Spain** sits mostly at the longitude of Britain and Portugal but
  keeps Central European Time, a legacy of a wartime alignment with its
  neighbours that was never reversed. Spaniards therefore eat and sleep
  "late" by the clock and roughly on time by the Sun.
- **Kiribati** shifted the International Date Line eastward in 1995 so
  that all of its islands would share one calendar date, rather than
  running a business week split across two days.

Daylight saving time is the same idea applied seasonally: shift the
legal clock so that human activity lines up better with daylight. Some
countries use it, some abandoned it, and the boundaries between "uses
it" and "does not" are drawn by legislatures, not by physics.

## What to take away

Two separate things are stacked on top of each other. The astronomical
layer is rigid: 15 degrees per hour, and the Sun cannot be overhead
everywhere at once. The political layer is soft: which lines to draw,
how wide, and where to bend them around a border, an island group, or a
national identity. Time zone maps look jagged because the second layer
is fitting itself over the first.

A useful test of understanding: if the Earth rotated twice as fast, the
astronomical layer would change — you would want zones about 7.5 degrees
wide to keep the same clock-versus-Sun error. But the *reasons* the
boundaries wiggle would be exactly the same, because those reasons are
about trade, borders, and administration, not about the sky. Precise
zone counts change as laws change; today there are roughly forty
distinct offsets in use, but that number is not fixed.
