## The core reason: the same beam spread over more ground

Sunlight arrives at Earth as an essentially parallel beam carrying a
fixed amount of power per square metre — about 1,361 W/m² measured
perpendicular to the beam above the atmosphere.

Now consider where that beam lands. If the Sun is directly overhead, a
one-square-metre bundle of sunlight illuminates one square metre of
ground. If the Sun is 60° from vertical, that same bundle is smeared
across a patch two square metres in area, so each square metre receives
half as much. In general, the energy delivered per square metre of
ground goes as the **cosine of the angle between the Sun and vertical**.

That is the whole first-order story. Near the equator the midday Sun is
close to overhead year-round, and cos of a small angle is near 1. Near
the poles the Sun is always low, and cos of a large angle is small. On
an annual average, the top of the atmosphere over the equator receives
roughly 420 W/m², while the poles receive somewhere around 170–180 W/m²
— roughly a factor of two and a half. Values vary a little by source and
method, but the ratio is robust.

Note what this is *not*. It is not that the poles are farther from the
Sun. The Earth's radius is about 6,371 km, negligible against 150
million km. Distance is irrelevant; **angle is everything**.

## Three amplifiers

Geometry alone would give a temperature difference. Three feedbacks make
the real one larger.

**Atmospheric path length.** Light arriving at a shallow angle travels
through much more air before reaching the ground, so more of it is
scattered and absorbed on the way. The low-angle beam is not just
spread thinner; it is also dimmed more.

**Albedo.** Snow and ice reflect 50–90% of incoming sunlight; open ocean
reflects only about 6%. So the cold regions throw away most of the
little sunlight they do receive. This is a feedback loop: cold makes
ice, ice reflects sunlight, reflection makes it colder. It also runs in
reverse, which is a major reason the Arctic has warmed faster than the
global average.

**Evaporation and the water cycle.** Warm tropical oceans evaporate
heavily, and that latent heat is released again when the vapour
condenses — often thousands of kilometres away. This moves enormous
amounts of energy poleward and is part of why the tropics are not even
hotter.

## Why the gradient is not as steep as the sunlight

Here is the part that turns a fact into an understanding. If each
latitude simply radiated away exactly what it absorbed, the poles would
be far colder than they are and the tropics far hotter.

They do not, because the atmosphere and ocean **transport heat
poleward**. The tropics absorb more energy than they radiate to space;
the high latitudes radiate more than they absorb. The difference is
carried by winds, storm systems, and ocean currents. Weather, at the
largest scale, is the machinery that redistributes this imbalance —
mid-latitude storms exist because there is a temperature gradient to
flatten.

## Complications worth holding

**Seasons come from tilt, not distance.** Earth's axis is tilted about
23.4°, which is why each hemisphere leans toward the Sun for half the
year. Earth is actually nearest the Sun in early January, during
northern winter — proof that distance is not the driver.

**Polar summer is odd.** At the pole in midsummer the Sun never sets, so
the *daily total* insolation there can exceed the equator's. Yet it
stays cold, because the light is arriving at a very low angle through a
lot of atmosphere onto a highly reflective surface, and because six
months of darkness follow.

**The poles are not symmetric.** The Arctic is an ocean surrounded by
land; the Antarctic is a high continent, averaging over 2 km in
elevation, surrounded by a circumpolar current that isolates it. The
Antarctic is substantially colder, and elevation and isolation, not
sunlight, are the reasons.

**Latitude is not the only variable.** Elevation, ocean currents,
continentality, and prevailing winds all shift local climate. Latitude
sets the energy budget; the rest decides how a particular place spends
it.
