Start with the right relationship. **Speech is the language; writing is
a technology for recording it.** Every normal human community has
spoken language, acquired by children without instruction. Writing was
invented only a handful of times, must be taught explicitly, and many
languages have never had it. So the question for any script is: *which
part of speech does it encode, and at what grain?*

There are broadly two things a written symbol can point at: a **sound**
or a **meaning**. Real systems mix both, and the interesting part is the
proportions.

## The spectrum, from meaning to sound

**Logographic (or morphographic).** A symbol stands for a morpheme — a
unit of meaning — rather than for a sound sequence. Chinese characters
are the main living example.

The common description "picture writing" is misleading. Most Chinese
characters are **phono-semantic compounds**: one component hints at
meaning, another hints at pronunciation. Estimates commonly put these at
around 80–90% of characters. So even the most "meaning-based" system in
wide use is riddled with sound information; it just encodes it at the
level of the whole syllable-morpheme, and imperfectly, since the
pronunciation hints were frozen centuries ago.

**Syllabary.** One symbol per syllable, with no systematic relationship
between symbols for syllables sharing a consonant. Japanese hiragana and
katakana are syllabaries; so is the Cherokee script devised by Sequoyah
in the 1820s. Syllabaries suit languages with simple syllable structure.
Japanese has few possible syllables, so about fifty symbols suffice.
English, with syllables like *strengths*, would need thousands.

**Abugida.** A consonant symbol carries an inherent vowel, and other
vowels are marked by systematic modifications. Devanagari (Hindi,
Marathi, Nepali), Bengali, Thai, Tamil, and Ethiopic work this way. The
unit is still the syllable, but it is *analysed* into parts, which cuts
the number of shapes needed dramatically.

**Abjad.** Consonants are written; vowels are largely inferred, with
optional diacritics for learners and sacred texts. Arabic and Hebrew are
abjads. This works well for Semitic languages, where the consonantal
root carries the core meaning and vowel patterns carry grammar —
*k-t-b* relates to writing across many derived forms, so writing the
consonants captures the lexical content and lets context supply the
rest.

**Alphabet.** Separate symbols for consonants *and* vowels, both on the
same footing. Greek produced this by taking Phoenician consonant letters
whose sounds Greek did not use and repurposing them as vowels — an
adaptation, not a design. Latin, Cyrillic, Greek, and Armenian scripts
follow.

**Featural.** Korean Hangul, created in the fifteenth century, goes one
level below the phoneme: letter shapes systematically encode articulation
— several consonant shapes schematically depict the position of the
tongue or lips — and letters are grouped into syllable blocks. It is
among the few scripts with a documented deliberate design.

## The trade-offs

**Learning cost versus stability across varieties.** Alphabetic systems
have few symbols and are quick to start reading with. Chinese characters
require memorising thousands, but they buy something real: a text can be
read by speakers of Chinese varieties that are mutually unintelligible in
speech, because the characters attach to meaning rather than to one
pronunciation. Written classical Chinese served across East Asia for the
same reason.

**Orthographic depth.** Even within alphabets, the sound-to-symbol map
varies. Finnish, Spanish, and Korean are *shallow*: pronunciation is
largely predictable from spelling. English and French are *deep*.
English is deep because its spelling froze around a pronunciation that
then changed, and because it absorbed vocabulary from several languages
along with their spelling conventions — hence *though*, *through*,
*rough*, *bough*. Deeper orthographies correlate with slower early
reading acquisition, though skilled adult readers converge.

**Mixing is normal.** Japanese runs kanji (logographic), hiragana
(grammatical endings), and katakana (loanwords) in a single sentence,
plus Latin letters. This is not disorder; the different systems mark
different kinds of information visually, which partly compensates for
Japanese not using spaces between words.

## The best illustration is on your keyboard

Numerals. The symbol **7** is a logograph: it encodes a meaning, not a
sound. A reader of English says "seven," of Spanish "siete," of Mandarin
"qī" — same symbol, different sounds, same concept. So do **%**, **&**,
and **€**. Every literate person already uses both encoding strategies
daily and switches between them without noticing.
