Imagine a small square with several dumpling stalls. Every weekday
lunch hour, office workers come out and buy plates of dumplings. We
want to know what the price will be and how many plates get sold — and
we want to derive it rather than assert it.

## The two schedules

**Demand** is a list of how many plates buyers would buy at each price,
holding everything else fixed. It slopes down: at a higher price, some
people bring lunch from home instead, or buy noodles next door.

**Supply** is a list of how many plates sellers would make at each
price. It slopes up: at a higher price it becomes worth firing up a
second steamer, paying a helper, or standing out in the rain.

| Price per plate | Plates demanded | Plates supplied |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $3 | 120 | 40 |
| $4 | 100 | 60 |
| $5 | 80 | 80 |
| $6 | 60 | 100 |
| $7 | 40 | 120 |

## Finding the equilibrium by reasoning, not by looking it up

Try $4. Buyers want 100 plates; sellers will make 60. Forty hungry
people are left over. Someone offers a bit more to jump the queue, or a
vendor notices they sell out by 12:20 and raises the price. Pressure is
upward.

Try $7. Sellers make 120, buyers take 40. Eighty plates go cold. A
vendor would rather sell at $6 than throw food away. Pressure is
downward.

At **$5, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at 80 plates**. Nobody
who wants dumplings at $5 goes without, and no vendor is left holding
stock. That is equilibrium — not a fair price, not a good price, just
the price at which the two plans are consistent.

## Shifts versus movement along

This is where most people get confused, so be strict about it.

A change in *price* moves you **along** a curve. A change in anything
else *shifts* the whole curve.

- A nearby office tower opens and 300 more workers arrive: demand shifts
  right. At every price, more plates are wanted. The new equilibrium has
  both higher price and higher quantity.
- The price of pork doubles: supply shifts left. At every price, fewer
  plates are worth making. Price rises and quantity falls.
- A noodle stall next door cuts its prices: dumpling demand shifts left,
  because noodles are a substitute.

Note the diagnostic value. If you see price *and* quantity both rise,
demand moved. If price rises while quantity falls, supply moved. The
pattern tells you which side of the market was disturbed.

## Elasticity, in one paragraph

How much quantity responds to price is *elasticity*. Dumplings at lunch
are fairly elastic: substitutes are ten metres away. Insulin is
inelastic: there is no substitute and the buyer cannot wait. Elasticity
determines who absorbs a tax or a cost shock. If demand is very
inelastic, sellers can pass a cost increase to buyers almost fully. If
it is very elastic, they mostly eat it.

## Side note: a price ceiling

Suppose the city, worried about lunch costs, caps dumplings at **$4 per
plate**. Read it straight off the table: at $4, buyers want 100 plates
and sellers will make 60. There is a **shortage of 40 plates** that does
not go away, because the mechanism that used to close it — the price
rising — is now illegal.

The demand does not evaporate; it gets rationed some other way:

- **Queues.** People pay in time instead of money. A 20-minute wait is a
  real cost, but unlike a higher price it does not encourage any vendor
  to make more dumplings. The cost is burned rather than transferred.
- **Quality erosion.** Smaller plates, cheaper filling, less pork.
- **Favouritism and side payments.** Regulars get served; strangers do
  not.
- **Exit.** The most marginal vendor — the one whose costs sat just
  under $5 — stops showing up, which shifts supply left and makes the
  shortage worse over time.

Two important qualifications. First, a ceiling *above* the equilibrium,
say $6, does nothing at all: the market already clears below it, so the
rule never binds. Second, the simple model assumes many small
competing sellers. Where one seller has real market power, or where
buyers cannot easily judge quality, a price rule can improve outcomes.
The lesson is not "controls always fail," it is: **if you fix the price,
you must expect some other variable to move instead, and you should
predict which one.**
