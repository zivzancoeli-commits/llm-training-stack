Food does two different jobs: it supplies **energy** to run the body,
and it supplies **materials** to build and repair it. Most diet
arguments get muddled because they discuss one macronutrient's role in
one job as if it settled the other. Keep the two columns separate.

## Energy: the accounting

By convention, the metabolisable energy in food is approximated as:

| Macronutrient | Energy |
| --- | ---: |
| Carbohydrate | ~4 kcal per gram |
| Protein | ~4 kcal per gram |
| Fat | ~9 kcal per gram |
| Alcohol | ~7 kcal per gram |

These are rounded averages, not exact constants — digestibility varies
by food, and whole nuts, for instance, appear to deliver somewhat less
than the label figure. Still, they are good enough to reason with.

Fat carries more than twice the energy per gram because its carbons are
more reduced — there is more oxidation left to do. That is also why the
body stores surplus energy as fat rather than carbohydrate: it is the
lightest way to carry a reserve. A typical adult stores only about
400–500 grams of glycogen, worth roughly 1,600–2,000 kcal, but tens of
thousands of kcal as body fat.

## Carbohydrate: the fast fuel

Digestion breaks starches and sugars into simple sugars, mostly glucose,
absorbed into the blood. Glucose is burned directly, stored as glycogen
in liver and muscle, or converted to fat if there is a large surplus.

Two roles worth knowing. Liver glycogen buffers **blood glucose** for
the whole body between meals; muscle glycogen is a local reserve the
muscle keeps for itself. And the brain is a heavy, picky customer,
using roughly 120 grams of glucose a day. Carbohydrate is not
"essential" in the strict nutritional sense, because the liver can make
glucose from amino acids and glycerol, and during prolonged fasting the
brain shifts substantially to ketone bodies derived from fat. But the
body will go to real trouble to keep blood glucose in range, which
tells you how central it is.

Fibre is carbohydrate that human enzymes cannot break down. It largely
passes through, slowing absorption and feeding gut bacteria, and diets
higher in fibre are consistently associated with better outcomes.

## Protein: mostly not fuel

Protein is digested into **amino acids** — twenty in ordinary use, of
which nine are *essential*, meaning the body cannot synthesise them and
must obtain them from food. Amino acids build enzymes, muscle,
antibodies, transport proteins, and much of the machinery of the cell.

The important idea is **turnover**. Body proteins are constantly broken
down and rebuilt; you need a daily supply not because protein is being
burned but because recycling is imperfect. There is no dedicated protein
storage depot, so a persistent shortfall is met by breaking down
functional tissue.

Common intake references sit around 0.8 g per kg of body weight per day
as a minimum for healthy adults, with athletes, older adults, and people
recovering from illness often advised more. The exact optimum is
genuinely debated, and recommendations vary between national guidelines.

Protein quality depends on the amino acid profile. Animal sources
generally supply all nine essential amino acids in useful proportions;
individual plant sources are often lower in one or two, which is easily
handled by eating a variety over the day rather than by combining
specific foods at each meal.

## Fat: structure, signalling, and transport

Fat is not just stored energy. Every cell membrane is a phospholipid
bilayer. Steroid hormones are built from cholesterol. Vitamins A, D, E,
and K are fat-soluble and require dietary fat to be absorbed. Two
polyunsaturated fatty acids — linoleic acid (an omega-6) and
alpha-linolenic acid (an omega-3) — are **essential**, in the same
strict sense as the essential amino acids.

This is why very low fat intake causes problems that have nothing to do
with calories. The types differ in their effects on blood lipids —
saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated, and industrially produced
trans fats — with trans fats being the one near-universal negative in
the evidence.

## Why fads mislead

Most popular diets work, when they work, by making people eat less
overall — through restriction rules, higher satiety, or eliminating a
large category of foods. When a diet removes one macronutrient, whatever
replaces it is doing half the causal work, and single-nutrient claims
almost never account for that substitution. Add the fact that nutrition
studies mostly rely on self-reported intake over long periods, and you
should expect modest, uncertain effects rather than dramatic ones.
Energy balance, adequate protein, both essential fatty acids, fibre,
and the micronutrients cover most of what is firmly established.
