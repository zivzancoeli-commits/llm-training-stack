title: Percent change vs percentage points using poll numbers
approx_words: 570
---

Two headlines describe the same poll. One says "support fell by 10 points." The other says "support fell by 25%." Both can be literally true at the same time, and if you don't know the difference between percentage points and percent change, you will misread one of them.

The setup: a candidate polled at 40% support in March and 30% in April.

Percentage points measure the raw gap between two percentages by subtraction: 40 - 30 = 10 percentage points. This is an absolute difference. It answers "how far apart are these two percentages on the 0-to-100 scale?"

Percent change measures the drop relative to where you started: (new - old) / old = (30 - 40) / 40 = -10/40 = -0.25, a 25% decrease. This is a relative difference. It answers "how big is the change compared to the original value?"

So "down 10 points" and "down 25%" are the same event. Neither headline is lying; they're answering different questions. The trouble starts when a reader (or writer) treats "10 points" as "10%," or vice versa.

Work the reverse direction to see why the distinction has teeth. Suppose the candidate's support later rises 25% from the April level of 30%. That's 30 * 1.25 = 37.5%, not back to 40. A 25% drop followed by a 25% rise does not return you to the start, because the two percentages are taken of different bases: the drop was 25% of 40 (which is 10 points), while the rise is 25% of 30 (which is only 7.5 points). Percent changes are not symmetric, and they don't add across steps. Points, by contrast, do add: down 10 points then up 10 points really does return you to 40.

Verify the original computation with a concrete population, the most reliable check for any percentage claim. Imagine 1,000 poll respondents. In March, 400 supported the candidate; in April, 300 did. The candidate lost 100 supporters. As a fraction of the March supporters, that is 100/400 = 25%: the percent change. As a share of the whole electorate, it is 100/1000 = 10%: the 10-percentage-point drop. Both numbers fall out of the same head-count, which confirms they're two views of one fact rather than two facts.

The common mistake, in both directions: reading "interest rates rose from 4% to 6%" as "a 2% increase." That's a 2-percentage-point increase but a 50% increase (2/4 = 0.5), and on a mortgage, a 50% increase in the rate is enormous. Conversely, a report that "risk increased 100%" sounds terrifying until you learn the risk went from 0.01% to 0.02%: a one-hundredth-of-a-point change. Relative changes make small things sound big; absolute point changes make big things sound small. Skilled communicators pick whichever framing serves their story, so a skilled reader recomputes the other one.

The habit to build: whenever you see a change in any percentage, immediately write down both numbers. Old value, new value, difference in points, and difference as a fraction of the old value. Four numbers, ten seconds. If a claim gives you only the relative change ("cut by a third!"), ask "of what base?" before reacting. And when you write about percentages yourself, say "percentage points" explicitly when you mean subtraction; the two extra words are the difference between informing your reader and accidentally misleading them.
