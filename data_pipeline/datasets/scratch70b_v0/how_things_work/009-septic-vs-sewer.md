# How a septic vs sewer system differs in where waste goes

Everything downstream of a drain has the same job: separate solids,
digest the organic load with bacteria, and return water clean enough not
to poison the ground or a river. Septic and sewer systems do the same
biology. They differ in *where* the treatment happens and *who* owns the
consequences of it going wrong.

## Sewer: treatment happens far away, at scale

In a sewered neighbourhood, the house drain joins a lateral pipe to a
main under the street. Gravity carries the flow, which is why sewer mains
are laid on a continuous downhill slope and why hilly areas need lift
stations that pump the flow back up to keep the gradient going.

At a municipal wastewater treatment plant the flow passes through stages.
Screens and grit chambers pull out rags, wipes, and sand. Primary
clarifiers are large quiet tanks where solids settle and grease floats,
removing maybe half the suspended solids. Secondary treatment is the
biological heart of the plant: aeration basins where blown air feeds a
dense population of bacteria and protozoa that eat dissolved organic
matter, followed by another clarifier that settles the resulting
biological floc. Many plants add tertiary steps for nitrogen and
phosphorus, then disinfect with chlorine or ultraviolet light before
discharging to a river or ocean under a permit that sets numeric limits.
The settled solids are digested, dewatered, and disposed of or
land-applied as biosolids.

The householder's interface with all of this is a bill and a pipe.

## Septic: treatment happens in your own yard

A septic system serves one property, typically where mains are too far
away to be economical. It has two parts.

The **septic tank** is a buried watertight tank, often 3,000-6,000 litres,
with an inlet baffle and an outlet baffle. Its job is separation and
partial digestion, not purification. Solids settle to the bottom as
sludge; grease and light material float as a scum layer; the relatively
clear liquid in the middle, called effluent, exits through the outlet
baffle, which is positioned below the scum and above the sludge.
Anaerobic bacteria in the tank slowly break down the sludge, but not
fast enough to keep up, so sludge accumulates and the tank must be
pumped out every three to five years for a typical family.

The **drain field** (leach field) is where actual treatment happens.
Effluent flows into perforated pipes laid in gravel trenches a metre or
so below the surface. It percolates down through unsaturated soil, and
that soil is the filter: aerobic bacteria in a biologically active layer
called the biomat consume remaining organics, soil particles adsorb
phosphorus and viruses, and the water eventually joins the groundwater.
Sizing the field depends on a percolation test, because sandy soil
accepts water fast and clay barely accepts it at all.

The critical divergence from sewer: the treatment capacity is your soil,
and it does not scale on demand.

## Limiting case: what if the septic tank is never pumped?

Follow the chain. Sludge builds from the bottom and scum from the top,
so the clear middle zone shrinks. Eventually solids reach the level of
the outlet baffle and start leaving the tank with the effluent.

Solids in a drain field are a disaster, because they clog soil pores.
The biomat thickens into an impermeable layer, and the field's
acceptance rate collapses. Effluent has nowhere to go, so it backs up.
The symptoms are unmistakable: suspiciously lush green grass over the
trenches, soggy ground or standing effluent, sewage odour outside, and
drains in the house that gurgle and then back up.

The cost asymmetry is the whole argument for maintenance. Pumping the
tank is a few hundred dollars. A failed drain field must be replaced,
which means excavating a new field on land that passes a perc test — an
expense in the tens of thousands, sometimes on a lot with no suitable
space left. A drain field killed by neglect does not recover on its own.

Several everyday choices load the same dice. Flushing wipes, sending
cooking grease down the sink, and putting heavy kitchen waste through a
garbage disposal all accelerate sludge and scum accumulation. A course of
antibiotics is fine, but dumping bleach or solvents kills the tank's
bacterial population. And a leaking toilet flapper that adds hundreds of
litres a day of clean water still matters, because hydraulic overload
pushes solids out of the tank before they have time to settle.

On a sewer, all of these are somebody else's problem, absorbed by a plant
with engineers and redundancy. On septic, they are yours, and the
feedback arrives slowly enough that by the time you notice, the expensive
part has already failed.
