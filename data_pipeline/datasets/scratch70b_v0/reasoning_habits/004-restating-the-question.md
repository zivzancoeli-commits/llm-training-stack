# Restating the Question in One Sentence

A surprising fraction of wrong answers are correct answers to a different question. The solver computed flawlessly and answered what they *assumed* was asked. The cheapest insurance against this failure is a habit that costs one sentence: before solving, restate the question in your own words, and check that your restatement still matches the original.

The classic trap looks like this: "A shirt costs $40 after a 20% discount. What was the original price?" The eye catches "$40," "20%," and "price," and the hand computes 40 × 0.8 = 32. But the question asked for the price *before* the discount, and the restatement would have caught it: "I need the number that, after losing 20%, becomes 40." Said aloud, that sentence makes the correct equation — 0.8x = 40, so x = 50 — nearly unavoidable. The misread survives only in silence.

Restating works because reading and understanding are different acts that feel identical from the inside. When you read a problem, your brain pattern-matches it to familiar templates and quietly substitutes the template for the text. "Find how many more" becomes "find how many." "Which of these does NOT..." becomes "which of these does..." "At least" becomes "exactly." Rewriting the question forces the substitution into the open, where you can compare it against the source. You are not testing your math; you are testing your reading, which fails more often.

Make the restatement do real work by holding it to three standards:

**One sentence, your own words.** If you can only repeat the problem verbatim, you may be holding sounds rather than meaning. Paraphrase is the test of comprehension. "They want the original price, given the discounted one" is understanding; re-reading the problem aloud is not.

**Name the object the answer will be.** Is the answer a count, a length, a probability, a name, a yes/no? "The answer is a number of *hours*" or "the answer is *which runner*, not a time" pins the target. Many multi-part word problems are lost at the final step, where a solver correctly finds an intermediate value — the speed, say — and reports it as the answer when the question asked for the arrival time.

**Check the restatement against the text, word by word for the dangerous words.** The dangerous words are small: *not, each, per, at least, at most, more than, remaining, difference, both, either*. These words invert or reshape the whole problem and are precisely the ones skimming eyes drop.

The habit has a diagnostic bonus. Sometimes you try to restate the question and find you *can't* — the sentence won't form. That inability is data. It means the confusion you would have discovered at minute ten, mid-calculation, has been discovered at minute zero, where it is cheap. You now know to re-read the setup, or to work a tiny example until the question's meaning becomes concrete, before spending any effort on solution machinery.

At the end of the problem, close the loop: read the original question one final time and check that the thing you are about to box matches it. "They asked for how many more red than blue; I have the counts 17 and 11; the answer is 6, not 17." This ten-second bookend — restate at the start, re-read at the end — costs less than one percent of the solving time and removes the most humiliating class of error: the perfect solution to the wrong problem.
