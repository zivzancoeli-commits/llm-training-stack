# Naming What You Don't Know Instead of Faking a Citation

There is a moment, mid-argument, when you need a fact you do not actually have. The boiling point of ethanol. The year a treaty was signed. Whether a theorem requires the function to be continuous. And in that moment there is a temptation: to write the sentence anyway, in the confident register of the sentences around it, and keep moving. The habit that separates trustworthy reasoners from fluent ones is what they do in that moment. Trustworthy reasoners *name the gap*.

Naming the gap means writing, explicitly: "I don't remember whether the theorem needs continuity — flagging this; the conclusion below depends on it." It means saying "roughly 80 degrees Celsius, though I'm not certain of the exact figure" instead of asserting "78.37°C" from a memory that never held that number. The difference is not modesty. It is engineering. A stated uncertainty is a load marked as unverified; anyone building on your argument — including you, tomorrow — knows exactly which beam to test before trusting the structure.

The faked citation is the opposite move: manufacturing the *texture* of knowledge without its substance. Precise-sounding numbers, invented attributions ("studies show..."), the phrase "it is well known that" deployed to prevent the question it pretends to answer. What makes this failure so corrosive is that it is optimized to pass review. A vague sentence invites scrutiny; a confident, specific, fabricated one deflects it. The fabrication doesn't just insert one error — it disables the error-checking of every reader downstream, because checking effort flows toward what sounds uncertain, and away from what sounds sure.

The internal version of this failure matters even more than the social one. When you fake certainty to *yourself* — "I'm sure the formula has a square root in it" — you close an open question and build on the closure. Every subsequent step inherits the unmarked risk. Whereas the honest note, "formula half-remembered, verify before use," keeps the question open at zero cost. You can still proceed! Provisional reasoning is legitimate and often necessary. The rule is not "never use uncertain facts." The rule is: **uncertain facts travel with their flags.** Reason conditionally — "if the boiling point is near 80°C, then this distillation works" — and the conclusion honestly carries an *if*.

Three practices make the habit concrete:

**Keep a "known / assumed / unknown" ledger for any serious problem.** Three headings on scratch paper. Facts you can defend go under known. Things you're treating as true for now go under assumed, each with a word about why. Open questions go under unknown. The discipline is in the sorting: the moment of deciding which column a claim belongs to is exactly the moment fakery would otherwise happen.

**Distinguish "I remember X" from "X is true."** These feel identical from the inside and are not. Memory is a lossy, confabulating instrument that fills gaps with plausible inventions and serves them with the same confidence as genuine recall. Saying "my memory says X, unverified" is an accurate report; saying "X" is a bet disguised as a report.

**When the missing fact is load-bearing, stop and check it; when it isn't, say so and route around it.** Not every gap deserves a halt. Often you can restructure the argument so the unknown cancels out, or bound it ("whatever the exact figure, it's under 100, which is all I need"). Naming the gap is what makes this routing possible — you cannot engineer around a hole you've plastered over.

The payoff compounds. A reasoner who flags gaps develops calibration, because their guesses get checked and scored. A reasoner who fakes never learns which of their "facts" were inventions — the errors are wearing the same uniform as the knowledge. In the long run, "I don't know" is not the opposite of expertise. It is how expertise is built.
