# Time-Boxing a Stuck Proof: What to Write Down Before You Walk Away

There is a specific kind of stuck that every prover knows: you have circled the same three ideas for forty minutes, each lap slightly more frantic and slightly less clear. Grinding onward from this state rarely produces the proof; it produces variations on the circle. The disciplined move is to leave — but to leave *correctly*. A time-box without an exit ritual just abandons the work. A time-box with one converts today's failure into tomorrow's head start.

First, the box itself. Before starting a hard problem, decide the budget: say, forty-five minutes of genuine effort. The deadline is not defeatism. It is an acknowledgment of how insight works: progress on hard problems often arrives *after* disengagement, when the mind stops forcing the dominant (wrong) framing and background processing loosens it. Mathematicians have reported for a century the experience of a proof arriving on a walk, in the shower, at the moment of waking. You cannot schedule that arrival, but you can make it possible — and staying hunched over the same page, re-deriving the same dead end, actively prevents it by keeping the failed framing rehearsed and dominant.

The exit ritual is the heart of the habit. When the timer ends, do not close the notebook. Spend ten minutes writing a *stuck report* with five sections:

**1. The statement, in your own words.** Not copied — restated. Half the time, this act alone exposes that you've been proving something subtly different from what was asked, and occasionally it un-sticks you on the spot.

**2. What you tried, and how each attempt died.** One line per attempt: "Induction on n: the inductive step needs the claim for n−2, not n−1." "Direct computation: works for the symmetric case only." This list is the most valuable artifact you will produce, because your future self — or the friend you eventually ask — will otherwise re-walk every one of these dead ends. A recorded failure is a fence around a pit.

**3. The exact gap.** Finish this sentence: "I could complete the proof if I could show ___." Compressing the stuckness into a single missing lemma is itself hard, and itself progress. A problem reduced to a named gap is smaller than the problem you started with, and the gap's shape often suggests where to hunt: a missing inequality points toward known bounds; a missing construction points toward examples.

**4. What the difficulty smells like.** One honest guess at *why* it's hard: "I think my invariant is too weak." "I suspect the claim is false for even n and I should hunt for a counterexample." These hypotheses are frequently wrong, but they give the returning mind a door to try first, instead of a blank wall.

**5. The smallest unresolved case.** Record the tiniest concrete instance you cannot yet handle. Returning to a specific object — this graph, this 3×3 matrix — re-engages faster than returning to an abstraction.

Then actually walk away. Not to the phone — the value of the break comes from undemanding time, a walk or a chore, where the problem can resurface unbidden. And when you return (tomorrow is often better than in an hour), begin by reading the stuck report *before* re-reading the problem, so that the new session starts at the frontier instead of at the trailhead.

One more benefit deserves naming. The stuck report is a calibration instrument. After a dozen of them, patterns emerge in section 4: perhaps you habitually trust induction too long, or abandon computation too early. No single session teaches you this; the paper trail does. Time-boxing is often sold as time management. It is better understood as knowledge management — the difference between being stuck, and knowing precisely *where* you are stuck, which is halfway to being unstuck.
