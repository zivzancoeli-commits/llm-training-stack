# Analogies that leak: atoms are not tiny solar systems

Every schoolchild meets the picture: a nucleus like a sun, electrons like planets wheeling around it in neat orbits. The picture is wrong in almost every way that matters — and yet it taught two generations of physicists something true. Understanding how an analogy can be simultaneously broken and useful is a core logical skill.

## What an analogy actually argues

An argument from analogy has this shape:

1. System X resembles system Y in respects R1, R2, R3.
2. Y has further property P, and P depends on (or flows from) R1–R3.
3. Therefore, X plausibly has property P too.

The load-bearing clause is the second half of premise 2. An analogy earns its inference only when the *shared* features are the ones doing the causal or structural work behind P. Resemblance alone buys nothing; *relevant* resemblance buys a presumption.

**A valid use.** The solar-system model shares with the atom one deep structural fact: a light body bound to a heavy central body by an attractive force that weakens with the square of distance (gravity there, electrostatic attraction here). Properties that flow from *that shared structure* transfer well. Most of the atom is empty space; the mass concentrates in a tiny center — which is exactly what Rutherford's gold-foil scattering revealed, and the analogy helped everyone grasp it instantly. Inverse-square orbital mechanics even predicts sensible energy relationships for hydrogen's single electron. Where the resemblance is real, the inferences it licenses are real.

## The tempting invalid cousin

1. Atoms are like solar systems.
2. Planets can orbit at any radius, spiral gradually inward or outward, and two planets can share similar orbits.
3. Therefore, electrons can orbit at any radius, spiral continuously, and pile into the same orbit.

**Counterexample — delivered by nature itself.** Every one of those transferred properties is false. Electrons occupy only discrete energy levels; there is no gradual spiral, only quantum jumps. Classical electromagnetism says an orbiting (accelerating) charge must radiate energy continuously and crash into the nucleus within about a hundred-millionth of a second — so if the analogy held fully, no atom would survive and no chemistry would exist. And electrons obey the exclusion principle: no two can share one quantum state, which is why electron "shells" fill up and the periodic table has its shape. Planets suffer no such rule. The premises (the resemblances) are true as far as they go; the conclusions drawn from the *unshared* features are false. The analogy leaked precisely where the systems' underlying physics diverges: gravity versus electromagnetism, classical versus quantum.

## The general lesson: map the leak

Treat every analogy as a cargo manifest with three columns:

- **Transfers safely:** properties that follow from the genuinely shared structure (central force, mostly-empty space, bound orbits as energy wells).
- **Leaks:** properties that depend on features only one system has (continuous orbits, radiative stability, co-orbiting bodies).
- **Unknown:** properties you haven't yet traced to either side — the danger zone where analogies quietly overreach.

The same audit applies everywhere. "The brain is a computer" transfers usefully (information processing, memory as state) and leaks badly (no clean hardware/software split; neurons rewire as they run). "A nation's budget is a household budget" transfers the bare idea of constraints and leaks on nearly everything else (households don't issue their own currency). "Genes are blueprints" transfers *encoded specification* and leaks on one-to-one part mapping — a recipe is the better metaphor.

An analogy is a bridge built from known territory toward unknown. The valid move is to walk it while testing each plank: *does this property ride on what our two systems truly share?* The fallacy is to drive a truck across at full speed because the first three planks held.
