# Newton's Third Law, One Skateboard at a Time

Newton's third law is usually recited as "for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction," which is memorable and easy to misuse. A cleaner statement: **forces come in pairs acting on different objects**. If A pushes on B, then B pushes on A with equal magnitude and opposite direction, at the same time. Neither force is the "cause" of the other; they are two ends of a single interaction.

## The skateboard experiment

Stand on a skateboard on smooth ground, holding a heavy ball. Throw the ball forward, hard. You roll backward.

Trace the forces during the throw. Your hands push the ball forward — that's one force, acting *on the ball*. Simultaneously, the ball pushes back on your hands with equal magnitude — that's the paired force, acting *on you*. The ball accelerates forward because of the force on it; you (plus skateboard) accelerate backward because of the force on you. The skateboard's wheels matter only because they remove friction that would otherwise let the ground cancel your recoil.

The key phrase is *on different objects*. A frequent confusion: "if the forces are equal and opposite, why don't they cancel?" Forces cancel only when they act on the *same* object. The forward force acts on the ball; the backward force acts on you. Each object feels one net force and accelerates accordingly. Nothing cancels.

## Same force, different accelerations

Equal forces do not mean equal motion. Newton's second law (F = ma) says acceleration is force divided by mass. Suppose you and the skateboard together have mass 60 kg and the ball has mass 3 kg. The forces are equal in magnitude, so the accelerations are in inverse ratio to the masses: the ball's acceleration is 60/3 = 20 times yours. The ball flies; you drift.

## Check: momentum bookkeeping with numbers

Conservation of momentum gives us a number check. Before the throw, everything is at rest: total momentum = 0. Momentum is conserved (no significant external horizontal force), so afterward the total must still be 0.

Say the 3 kg ball leaves at 6 m/s forward. Its momentum is 3 kg × 6 m/s = 18 kg·m/s forward. Your momentum must then be 18 kg·m/s backward. With a mass of 60 kg:

v = 18 kg·m/s ÷ 60 kg = 0.3 m/s backward.

Units check out: (kg·m/s)/kg = m/s. ✔ And the magnitude passes a plausibility test — a slow drift backward, roughly walking-shuffle speed, which matches what actually happens when people try this.

Limiting cases confirm the logic. Throw a ping-pong ball (0.003 kg): your recoil speed drops by a factor of 1000, to 0.0003 m/s — imperceptible, as expected. Throw something with your own mass: you and it separate at equal speeds. The formula degrades gracefully at both extremes.

## Why this matters beyond skateboards

This is precisely how rockets work — a fact people resist because "there's nothing to push against in space." The rocket doesn't push against air; it pushes exhaust gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward. The skateboard-and-ball scene *is* a rocket, run one throw at a time. A rocket just does it continuously, throwing tiny bits of "ball" (propellant) at very high speed.
