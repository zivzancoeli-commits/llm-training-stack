# DNA vs RNA: One Working Distinction and One Common Mix-Up

Textbooks list many DNA/RNA differences — the sugar (deoxyribose vs ribose), the bases (thymine vs uracil), strandedness (double vs single) — and students memorize the list without a way to organize it. Here is one distinction that does real explanatory work, followed by the mix-up that most often corrupts it.

## The working distinction: archive vs working copy

**DNA is the archive. RNA is the working copy.**

DNA stores the genome — the long-term instructions — and the cell treats it accordingly: it is kept chemically stable, double-stranded, proofread during copying, and actively repaired when damaged. The double helix itself supports this role: because the two strands are complementary (A pairs with T, G with C), damage to one strand can be repaired by reading the other. Redundancy is an archival feature.

RNA is transcribed from DNA as needed, does a job, and is degraded. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries a gene's sequence to the ribosome, where it is translated into protein. It is usually single-stranded, chemically less stable (the extra –OH group on ribose makes RNA prone to self-cleavage), and short-lived — minutes to hours in many cells. That instability is not a defect; it is a control mechanism. If working copies expired never, the cell couldn't turn genes *off* by simply stopping transcription.

This one distinction predicts the memorized facts rather than sitting beside them. Why is DNA double-stranded? Archives need redundancy and repair. Why does RNA use a less stable sugar and tolerate single strands? Working copies should be cheap, flexible (single strands can fold into functional shapes, as in tRNA and ribozymes), and disposable. Why does the cell bother with an intermediary at all? For the same reason you photocopy a rare manuscript instead of passing the original around the office.

(Honest caveat: biology has exceptions. Some viruses use RNA as their genome, and some RNAs — ribosomal RNA, for instance — are long-lived machines rather than transient messages. The archive/working-copy frame describes the cellular division of labor, not an unbreakable law.)

## The common mix-up: "RNA is made of protein" / "genes are proteins"

The most frequent confusion is collapsing the *information* molecules and the *product* molecules into one category — saying that genes are proteins, or that RNA is a kind of protein, or that the ribosome "converts RNA into DNA."

Keep the flow straight. The standard direction of information is:

**DNA → (transcription) → RNA → (translation) → protein**

Transcription copies a DNA sequence into RNA — same language, nucleotides to nucleotides, like copying text. Translation is the genuinely different step: it converts nucleotide language (read three letters at a time, as codons) into amino-acid language. Proteins are built *from* the instructions; they are not the instructions. A protein cannot be "read back" into a gene — cells have no machinery for protein → RNA, which is one reason acquired protein damage isn't inherited.

## Check by limiting case

Test the frame: what should happen if you destroy a cell's mRNA but leave DNA intact? Protein production halts, but the cell retains all its information and can resume — an inconvenience, not an identity loss. Destroy the DNA and leave the mRNA: existing instructions run briefly, then the cell has no way to make new working copies. Losing the archive is fatal; losing today's photocopies is not. The asymmetry is the whole point of the design.
