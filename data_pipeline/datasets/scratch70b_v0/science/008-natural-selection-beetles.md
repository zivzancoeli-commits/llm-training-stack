# Natural Selection Is Not "Trying": A Beetle-Color Example

Everyday language sabotages evolution. We say giraffes evolved long necks "to reach" high leaves, or bacteria mutate "in order to" resist antibiotics. The phrasing implies intention — organisms striving toward a goal, or nature aiming at one. Natural selection contains no trying at all. It is a filter, not a plan. A concrete example makes the difference visible.

## The beetle population

Imagine a population of 1,000 beetles living on dark tree bark. Their shell color varies: most are brown, some darker, some lighter, because the genes affecting pigment vary among individuals. Where does the variation come from? Random mutation and the reshuffling of genes in reproduction. "Random" here means random *with respect to usefulness*: a mutation making a beetle lighter is no less likely to occur just because lightness happens to be harmful on dark bark. The variation-generating machinery does not know or care what would help.

Now add birds. Birds hunt by sight, and lighter beetles stand out against dark bark. Suppose in each generation, light beetles have a 40% chance of surviving to reproduce while dark beetles have a 60% chance. No beetle chooses anything; no beetle darkens itself by effort. Each one is born with a color and takes its chances.

Run the arithmetic forward. Start with 500 light and 500 dark. After one round of survival: 200 light, 300 dark — the population is now 60% dark. The survivors reproduce, passing color genes to offspring (with a little fresh mutation noise). Each generation, the same filter tilts the ratio further. Within some tens of generations, dark beetles dominate, not because anyone tried, but because a repeated biased filter compounds — the same way a loaded coin, flipped enough times, produces a lopsided tally without ever intending one.

That is the entire mechanism, and it has exactly three requirements: **variation** (beetles differ), **heritability** (offspring resemble parents), and **differential reproduction** (the difference affects how many descendants you leave). Where all three exist, selection happens automatically. It is closer to sorting than to striving.

## What the "trying" story gets wrong, precisely

The intentional phrasing fails in a testable way: it predicts that need should generate the right variation. It doesn't. If the birds vanish and the bark lightens, dark beetles do not start mutating toward lightness at a higher rate. The population lightens only if lighter variants *already exist or arise by chance*, and then only through the same slow filtering. Populations without the needed variation don't adapt — they decline or die out, which is why extinction is common. A "trying" nature wouldn't let that happen; a filtering one does, constantly.

Same correction for antibiotics: resistance mutations arise at low rates whether or not the drug is present. The drug doesn't inspire them; it kills the non-resistant, leaving resistant lineages to inherit the dish.

## Check: switch off the ingredients

Test the mechanism by deleting each requirement, as a limiting case:

- **No variation** (all beetles identical brown): birds eat some, but survivors are genetically the same as the eaten. No change across generations. Selection has nothing to sort.
- **No heritability** (color set only by diet, not genes): dark survivors produce offspring of random color. The filter's work is erased each generation. No cumulative change.
- **No differential survival** (birds are blind to color): both types survive at 60%. Ratios drift only by chance, with no consistent direction.

Evolutionary change appears only when all three switches are on — which confirms the mechanism is the filter itself, not any goal, effort, or foresight inside the beetles.
