# Plate Tectonics: Moving Lithosphere, Not Continents Swimming

The cartoon version of continental drift shows continents as rafts floating across an ocean of liquid rock, paddling from one arrangement to another. Nearly every element of that picture is wrong, and the corrections are more interesting than the cartoon.

## Correction 1: the moving pieces are plates, not continents

Earth's rigid outer shell — the **lithosphere** — is broken into a dozen or so large plates plus smaller ones. The lithosphere is the crust *plus* the uppermost, cold, stiff part of the mantle, roughly 100 km thick on average (thinner under young ocean floor, thicker under old continents). A plate typically carries both continental and oceanic portions: the South American Plate includes the continent *and* half the South Atlantic seafloor, moving together as one unit. Continents don't plow through the ocean crust; they ride embedded in a larger rigid slab, like logs frozen into an ice floe rather than boats in water.

## Correction 2: the layer beneath is solid, not a magma ocean

Below the lithosphere lies the **asthenosphere** — hot, weak mantle rock. It is almost entirely *solid*. We know this because shear waves (S-waves) from earthquakes travel through it, and shear waves cannot pass through liquid. But solids at high temperature and pressure can *creep*: over millions of years, solid rock flows like extremely stiff putty, or like glacier ice — indisputably solid, yet flowing downhill. The asthenosphere's viscosity is around 10²⁰ Pa·s; honey is about 10 Pa·s, so this "fluid" is some nineteen orders of magnitude stiffer than honey. Plates move because a solid that yields over geologic time lies beneath a colder solid that doesn't.

## What actually drives the motion

The engine is heat escaping Earth's interior, and the dominant mechanism is gravity acting on the plates themselves. At mid-ocean ridges, plates pull apart and mantle rock rises and partially melts, making fresh oceanic lithosphere. As that lithosphere ages and cools over tens of millions of years, it grows denser than the mantle below. At subduction zones it bends and sinks, and the sinking slab *pulls* the rest of the plate along ("slab pull") — currently understood to be the largest driving force, with a push from elevated ridges assisting. Mantle convection and plate motion are best seen as one coupled system, with the cold sinking plates being convection's descending limb rather than passengers on it.

The plate boundaries are where the action concentrates: earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountain belts trace the plate edges on a world map with striking fidelity — which is itself evidence that rigid plates, not independently wandering continents, are the moving units. Plate interiors are comparatively quiet.

## Check: do the rates make sense?

Plates move at roughly 1–10 cm per year — famously, about fingernail-growth speed. GPS measurements confirm these rates directly today; magnetic stripes on the seafloor record them over millions of years, and the two methods agree.

Number check: the Atlantic Ocean is about 4,000–5,000 km wide. At a spreading rate of ~2.5 cm/year (25 km per million years), opening it takes on the order of 4,500 km ÷ 25 km/Myr ≈ 180 million years. The oldest Atlantic seafloor is indeed about 180 million years old (Jurassic). The arithmetic and the rock record match. ✔

A limiting-case check on the "swimming" misconception: if continents truly floated on liquid, S-waves would vanish below ~100 km depth, seafloor would show no systematic age pattern, and earthquakes would not concentrate along narrow boundary lines. All three predictions fail against observation — the rigid-plates-on-creeping-solid picture passes exactly where the cartoon breaks.
