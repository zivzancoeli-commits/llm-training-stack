# Why Ice Floats — and Why That Matters for Lakes

Most substances shrink when they freeze. The liquid's molecules, jostling loosely, settle into a tighter, ordered crystal, so the solid is denser and sinks in its own liquid. Solid wax sinks in melted wax. Water breaks this rule: ice floats. The reason is a piece of molecular architecture, and the consequence is that lakes freeze from the top down instead of the bottom up.

## The mechanism: hydrogen bonds force an open crystal

A water molecule is bent, with an oxygen that hogs electrons and two hydrogens left slightly positive. This lopsided charge lets each molecule form hydrogen bonds — directional attractions — with neighbors: each water can donate two hydrogen bonds and accept two, four in total, arranged roughly toward the corners of a tetrahedron.

In liquid water, these bonds constantly form and break, and molecules can slip into gaps, packing fairly efficiently. When water freezes, the hydrogen bonds lock in fully, and satisfying all four bonds at the proper angles forces the molecules into a hexagonal lattice full of empty space — a rigid scaffold with built-in holes. Order, in this case, costs density.

The numbers: liquid water near 0 °C has a density of about 1.000 g/cm³; ice is about 0.917 g/cm³. Ice is roughly 8–9% less dense, so it floats with about 9% of its volume above the waterline (buoyancy requires the submerged fraction to equal the density ratio, 0.917). Icebergs really are ~90% underwater — the folk claim checks out numerically.

There's a related oddity: liquid water is densest not at 0 °C but at about 4 °C. Just above freezing, water is already forming transient ice-like patches, which open up the structure; warming from 0 to 4 °C collapses some of that openness faster than thermal expansion counteracts it.

## Why lakes care

Follow a lake through autumn. Surface water cools, becomes denser, and sinks; warmer water rises to be cooled in turn. This convection stirs the lake — until the surface reaches 4 °C. Below that, further-cooled surface water is *less* dense than the 4 °C water beneath, so it stays on top. Stratification sets in: coldest water floats above a stable ~4 °C reservoir.

Then the surface freezes, and the floating ice becomes an insulating lid (ice, and the snow on it, conduct heat poorly). Heat loss from the depths slows enormously. Fish and other organisms overwinter in liquid water near 4 °C at the bottom.

## Check: what if ice sank?

Run the counterfactual. If ice were denser than water, each night's surface ice would sink, exposing fresh liquid to the cold air, which would freeze and sink in turn. Lakes in cold climates would fill with ice from the bottom up, and — because sunlight and summer warmth penetrate poorly into deep water — much of that ice would never melt. Temperate and polar lakes would be mostly solid, with at best a thin summer melt layer. Aquatic life as we know it in such regions would be untenable.

The limiting-case check also runs the other way: in climates that never approach freezing, the anomaly is irrelevant, and lakes there indeed behave like the "normal liquid" story predicts — density stratification driven simply by warm-on-top. The strange behavior of water matters exactly where, and only where, the mechanism says it should.
