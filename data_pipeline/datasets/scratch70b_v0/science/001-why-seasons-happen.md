# Why Seasons Happen (Tilt, Not Distance)

A common guess is that summer happens when Earth is closer to the Sun. It's a reasonable guess, and it's wrong. Earth's orbit is very nearly circular; the distance to the Sun varies by only about 3% over a year. In fact, Earth is *closest* to the Sun in early January — the middle of winter for the Northern Hemisphere. If distance drove the seasons, everyone on the planet would have summer at the same time. They don't. When it's July in Chicago, it's winter in Buenos Aires.

## The mechanism: axial tilt

Earth's rotation axis is tilted about 23.5° relative to the plane of its orbit, and the axis points in (nearly) the same direction in space all year — toward Polaris, roughly. As Earth travels around the Sun, this fixed tilt means that for part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun, and half a year later it leans away.

Leaning toward the Sun does two things:

1. **Steeper sunlight.** When your hemisphere tilts sunward, the Sun climbs higher in the sky at noon. Light arriving closer to straight-down spreads its energy over a smaller patch of ground. Think of a flashlight aimed straight at a table versus at a shallow angle: the shallow-angle beam smears into a long ellipse, so each square centimeter gets less power. The relevant quantity is power per unit area (watts per square meter), and it scales with the sine of the Sun's elevation angle.

2. **Longer days.** A tilted-toward hemisphere spends more of each 24-hour rotation on the daylight side. More hours of sunlight means more total energy delivered per day.

Both effects push the same direction, and together they change the daily energy input dramatically — far more than the 3% distance wobble ever could. That's why the tilt wins.

Note the lag: the longest day in the Northern Hemisphere is around June 21, but the hottest weeks usually come in July or August. Land and especially oceans take time to warm up, like a pot that keeps heating after you turn the burner to high.

## Check: what if Earth had no tilt?

A good way to test a mechanism is to remove it and see what the explanation predicts. Set the tilt to 0°, keeping the same slightly elliptical orbit.

- Every location would get the same day length (12 hours) and the same noon Sun angle all year.
- The only remaining variation would be the 3% distance change, which alters incoming sunlight by roughly 7% (intensity goes as one over distance squared, and 1.03² ≈ 1.07). That's a mild, *globally synchronized* wobble — both hemispheres slightly warmer in January, slightly cooler in July.
- Seasons as we know them — opposite in the two hemispheres, with big swings in day length — would vanish.

That prediction matches nothing we observe, which is exactly the point: the observed pattern (opposite seasons in opposite hemispheres, day length swinging with latitude) is the fingerprint of tilt, not distance.

One more limiting case: at the equator, "leaning toward" and "leaning away" nearly cancel — noon sun stays high all year and days stay near 12 hours. Consistent with experience, equatorial regions have weak temperature seasons, often organized instead around wet and dry periods. The mechanism predicts its own exceptions, which is what a correct mechanism should do.
