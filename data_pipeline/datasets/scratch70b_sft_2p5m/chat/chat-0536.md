### User
The kid from 2B rings my doorbell and runs. He's about six. I've caught him twice from the peephole. The parent was in the hallway the second time, smiling like it was hide-and-seek with my door. It happened four times this week. I work from home. Each ring yanks me out of a call or a thought. I don't want to scare a child. I also don't want to be the building's doorbell. First time saying something to the parent. Script me like I won't melt.

### Assistant
Talk to the parent, not the kid, and do it in the hallway when nobody is mid-prank. Catching a six-year-old in the act invites a scene. A calm sentence to the adult is the job.

"Your kid's been ringing my doorbell as a game. I work from home. I need it to stop." That's it. No joke to soften. Softening is how the parent heard hide-and-seek the first time.

You can add one practical: "If he needs something real, he can knock and wait. The run-away ring is the problem." You are not banning a child from existing in a hallway. You are naming a specific behavior.

I will not invent a building rule about doorbells or children in corridors. If your building has a front desk or a lease clause about disturbances, that's yours to read. Start with the parent. Most of this is social, not legal theater.

Don't lecture the kid about respect. That's the parent's job. If you scold the child while the parent smiles, you become the villain in a story you didn't write.

Is this always daytime, or are there evening rings too?

### User
Daytime, after school, and once at 7:40 p.m. The 7:40 one is what made me mad. Parent texted me after that — they got my number from the building list — "hope he didn't bother you, he's just friendly." Friendly is doing a lot of work there.

### Assistant
Reply to that text with the same facts, in writing. Writing is useful because the smile isn't in the thread.

"The doorbell game is bothering me, including at 7:40. Please have it stop. If he needs us, knock and wait." Short. No emoji. No "no worries!!" which would cancel the message.

If they double down on friendly, you can say "I believe he's friendly. The ringing still needs to stop." You are not required to debate a child's character. Character is not the complaint. The button is.

For the next ring: do not throw the door open in anger. That's how you scare a six-year-old and give the parent a story. Let it ring if you're on a call. If you're free and you see the parent in the peephole, open and repeat the hallway sentence. Once. Then close.

If it continues after a clear text and a clear in-person, then you involve the building: a note to the office or whatever channel you already use for noise, facts only, dates if you remember them. I will not promise what the office will do. Escalation is for after the parent has been told in words a reasonable adult can understand.

### User
What if the kid looks crushed and the parent says I'm targeting him because I don't like children.

### Assistant
You like children enough not to yell at one. That's already in your favor. Don't accept the frame.

"I'm not targeting him. I don't want my doorbell used as a game." If the kid is standing there looking crushed, you can say to the kid, gently, "You didn't ruin anything. I need the ringing to stop." Then you look back at the parent. The parent is the one who called it a game in the hallway.

You do not have to prove you like children as a category. That's a trap door. People who want to keep the game will always find a moral you failed.

If you feel yourself melting, end with "Thanks for handling it" even if they haven't yet. Then go inside. Melting on the threshold is how the 7:40 happens again.

Doorbell is yours. Game is theirs to cancel. Text sent, one hallway sentence, no scolding the child as a stand-in for the adult. You can be firm and still not be a monster in 2B's story.
