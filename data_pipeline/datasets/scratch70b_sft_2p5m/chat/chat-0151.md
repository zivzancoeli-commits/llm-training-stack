### User
I volunteered at the food pantry. Shift was supposed to end at 2. It's 3:40. The coordinator keeps finding "one more cart." I said yes twice already because I didn't want to be the kid who clocks out. I'm tired and I still have a paper due. Talk to me like I'm fourteen and you actually mean the nice parts, not like a poster.

### Assistant
You already stayed an hour and forty minutes extra. That's specific. That's not nothing.

Kind isn't you disappearing until they run out of carts. Kind is: you tell the truth about the clock and you still finish the cart in your hands.

Say, to the coordinator, not to the air: "I can finish this cart. I need to leave at 4. I have a paper." That's kind and specific. "I have a paper" is a real reason. You don't need a fake dentist.

Don't apologize five times. One "thanks for today" is enough.

Are you a minor with a parent expecting you, or just a student with a deadline? That changes who you also text.

### User
Seventeen. Parent thinks I get off at 2. I texted "running late" with no time. The coordinator is older and stressed. There's a line of people and I feel like leaving is stealing food from them, which I know is dumb, but.

### Assistant
Leaving at the time you signed up for is not stealing food. The pantry exists because of a schedule, not because one seventeen-year-old has no homework. If they need more hours, they need more volunteers or a shorter line, not your paper.

Text the parent a time, not a vibe: "Leaving at 4. Coordinator asked me to stay. I'll bus after." Specific. "Running late" makes parents invent crashes.

To the coordinator, same energy: "I'll finish the open bags on this table. I can't start a new cart. I have to go at 4." If they say "just the walk-in cooler," that's a new cart with a different name. You can say no to the cooler.

Kind-and-specific also looks like handing off: "These three bags are packed. The pasta is on the left. I'm going." Information is kinder than vanishing mid-bag and kinder than staying until you resent the pantry.

You are allowed to be tired. Tired plus polite is still leaving.

If they look disappointed: you can notice it without fixing it with another hour. "I can do my usual 12 to 2 next week" is a future yes. A future yes is not a today hostage.

### User
What if they say they really need me and the other volunteer already left.

### Assistant
Then the staffing problem is already true, and it was true at 2. One extra person staying until 6 doesn't make a system. You can say, "I get that it's short. I still have to leave at 4." Repeat the time. Repeating a time is not sass. It's a clock.

Don't volunteer a midnight return. Don't offer Sunday if you don't mean Sunday. Kind is not stacking promises.

If you can, before you walk out, do one concrete close-out: tape the open box, put the clipboard where they keep it, tell them the line number if you were counting — only if you were actually counting. Don't invent "there's about forty people" if you didn't count. Guessing a crowd to sound useful is the opposite of specific.

Paper: when you get home, you don't owe a speech about civic duty in the first paragraph. You eat, you start the paper, you tell the parent the true end time. If the pantry texts "can you come back," you answer tomorrow. Tonight is the assignment you already have.

If you want to help the short-staff thing without bleeding tonight, that's a later conversation: "I can add one hour next Saturday" or "I can't add hours during school weeks." Pick one that's true. Fourteen-year-old-smart version: extra time is a gift you plan, not a trap you fall into because a cart appeared.

### User
Finish this cart, 4 o'clock out loud, text parent "leaving at 4," no new cooler, no fake crowd numbers.

### Assistant
That's kind because it's clear. You already gave them almost two extra hours. End at 4, go write the paper, come back at the time you actually signed up for. That's how volunteer shifts stay possible.
