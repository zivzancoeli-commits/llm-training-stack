### User
My friend Priya told a joke at brunch about someone's accent. Half the table laughed, the other half did that tight smile. I think I should say something to her but I also don't want to be the hall monitor. I almost know how I'd start and then I chicken out.

### Assistant
Before I hand you a script: when you say it landed badly, do you mean people were hurt, or that it just died in the air and you found it cringey?

Those are different talks. One is "you stepped on someone." The other is "that bit didn't work, and I don't want you to keep doing it." I can help with either, but I need that first.

### User
Someone at the table — not the target, another friend — got quiet and left early. Priya didn't notice. I think it was the accent thing, but I'm not 100% sure. I still want to tell Priya it didn't land.

### Assistant
Okay. You're close. Don't lead with a verdict about her character. Lead with what you saw, then one ask.

You're not the hall monitor if you talk to Priya in private and you don't impersonate the person who left. You also don't need a TED Talk on comedy ethics. You need a short, slightly awkward conversation, which is what almost-there people skip.

Try this shape:

"Hey, at brunch, the accent joke — the table split. I laughed a second late because I was uncomfortable, and [quiet friend] left early. I don't know if that's why. I wanted to tell you it didn't land for me, and I don't want to watch you do that bit again."

That's you. Not "everyone hated it." Not "you're a bad person." You are allowed to say your own reaction as a fact.

If she says "it was just a joke," you don't argue the word joke. You say, "I know you meant it as a joke. It still landed like a jab at how someone talks." Then you stop. Extra paragraphs make you sound like you're prosecuting.

If she asks who was mad, you don't name the quiet friend as a victim unless that person asked you to. You can say, "I'm telling you because I was there, not because I was elected."

If you're chicken because you still like her: like her out loud. "I'm saying this because I want you at brunch, not because I want a fight." That's not softening the point. That's telling her why you're bothering.

You almost have it when you can feel the start in your mouth. The miss is usually adding a joke of your own to cushion hers. Don't. Cushioning with another laugh tells her the bit worked.

Want to practice her likely pushback, or do you want a text instead of a call?

### User
Text. I'll over-explain on a call. Also what if I'm wrong and the friend left because of parking?

### Assistant
Then you still told Priya your reaction, which you actually had. You don't need a courtroom standard. You need "I was uncomfortable and I don't want that bit again." Parking can be true and the joke can still have been ugly. Both can sit in the same brunch.

Text, short enough that you won't edit it into mush:

"Priya — the accent joke at brunch didn't land for me. The table got weird and I should've said so instead of doing the polite laugh. Please don't do that one around me. Still want to hang, just not that bit."

Send it. Don't add a paragraph about cancel culture, her childhood, or how you're probably overreacting. Those are the chicken-out clauses.

If she replies with a laughing emoji only, you can answer once: "I'm serious about the joke. The hang is still on." Then you let her be an adult.

If she gets defensive, you don't pile on screenshots. You already said the thing. Your job was the signal, not a retrial.

You're not looking for a perfect moral win. You're looking for a friend who hears "that one missed" and can live. If she can't, that's information too, and you don't have to collect it all tonight.
