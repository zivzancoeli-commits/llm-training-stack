### User
First day in a shared kitchen. Four roommates I barely know. Someone already asked if I wanted to "just do a big Costco run together and split everything." I do not want that. I have a tight grocery habit and I do not want to fund someone else's oat milk. I also do not want to invent a fake allergy or a fake meeting. How do I say no without becoming the difficult one on day one? Talk like you do not buy the roommate-commercial.

### Assistant
You say no. You do not need a costume.

"I'm going to keep my food separate and shop on my own. I'm not in for a group Costco split." That is a complete sentence. It is not an insult. It does not require a medical story or a calendar lie. People who push after a clear no are doing the pushing. You are not obligated to make it easier for them by faking a reason they can argue with.

Shared kitchens work on shelves and names, not on vibes. On day one, pick a shelf or a fridge bin, put your name on it with tape, and cook a normal meal. You are not joining a commune unless you say so.

If they look disappointed, you can add one optional line: "I'm happy to take turns on the trash and the sink." That is a yes to chores, which is different from a yes to a warehouse club marriage. Do not trade a grocery no for a fake yes on something you also hate. One honest no is cleaner than two mixed signals.

I am not going to tell you splitting is always a scam. Sometimes it saves money. You already know it does not fit how you shop. That is enough.

### User
They said "it is just easier" and smiled like I was being precious. One of them is older and has that uncle energy, actually. I can feel myself about to say "maybe later" to get out of the kitchen. I do not mean maybe later.

### Assistant
Do not say maybe later if you mean no. "Maybe later" is how you end up at Costco on Sunday holding a family-pack of yogurt you will not eat.

Repeat the no without upgrading the explanation. "I heard you. I'm still shopping for myself." Then you open the fridge, put your milk on your shelf, and start chopping whatever you brought. Hands busy. Conversation over. You do not need to win the smile contest.

If uncle-energy keeps going — "you'll come around," "we all share" — you can be blunt: "I'm not coming around on this. Separate food." Flat. Not a debate club. You are not on trial for individualism. You are a person who does not want a shared cart.

Difficult, in a kitchen, is leaving raw chicken on a plate and disappearing. Difficult is not declining a Costco membership on day one. Anyone who conflates those is selling a story.

### User
What about pots? They said the big pot is "everyone's." I have a small saucepan. I do not want a fight about a pot I did not buy. And labels. Is labeling leftover containers paranoid or just adult?

### Assistant
A pot that was there before you is house gear unless someone says it is theirs. You can use it, wash it, put it back. If you do not want your identity in that pot, use your saucepan. You do not need permission to use your own pan. You also do not need a speech about communal property theory.

Labels on your leftovers are adult. "Jordan, Tuesday" on tape is how food stops becoming mysterious soup. Label your stuff. Do not relabel theirs. If someone eats your labeled food, that is a later conversation with a fact, not a day-one manifesto.

You already know the no. The only trick is not decorating it. No allergy. No invented shift. No maybe. Shop for yourself, name a shelf, wash what you use, decline the group cart in one sentence, and let them adjust. Skeptical uncles respect a closed door more than a long excuse. Give them the door.
