### User
Night bus. Stranger next to me started chatting. Fine. Then they asked about my "attachment style" and whether my last relationship was "avoidant" and I froze. I'm intern-new at adult conversations. Translate the jargon, then tell me how to get out without a TED talk. They're still there. I'm texting you from the window seat.

### Assistant
Plain speech first.

"Attachment style" is pop-psych talk for "how you act when you get close to people." People online sort that into boxes with names. Those boxes are not a blood test. A stranger on a bus cannot know yours, and you don't owe them a quiz answer.

"Avoidant" in that slang means "you pull away when things get close." It's a description some therapists use in a real room, and a weapon some dating posts use. On a bus, it's a stranger trying to skip to intimacy by using classroom words.

You froze because they jumped from weather to your last relationship's supposed diagnosis. That's a jump. You're allowed to not take it.

I will not diagnose you or your ex. I don't know either of you.

Are you trying to stay polite until your stop, or do you want the chat over now even if they look disappointed?

### User
Over now. Stop is twelve minutes. They also said I have "main character energy" and asked if I "do the work." I don't know what work.

### Assistant
More translation.

"Main character energy" means "you seem like the center of a story." On a bus it can be a compliment, a flirt, or a way to make you perform. You don't have to accept the role.

"Do the work" in this dialect usually means "go to therapy / journal / talk about feelings on purpose." It is not a job assignment. A stranger doesn't get to assign it.

Out, intern-clear, no essay:

Look at them less. Short answers. "I don't talk about that on the bus." If they keep going: "I'm going to listen to my headphones now." Then put them in, even without music if you have to. That's a door.

You don't need a story about a boyfriend waiting, a headache, or a phone call from your manager. Those are costumes. The true reason is enough: too personal, not doing it.

If they call that rude: rude is a word they can have. You're still allowed to stop. Twelve minutes of silence is not a crime.

Body: bag on the seat edge if there's space to make a little wall, angle toward the window, don't laugh to soften the no. Laughing is how intern-you accidentally invites question two.

If they ask "so what is your attachment style" again, you can repeat the same sentence. Repeating is not failing intern training. Explaining the concept back to them is extra labor you don't owe.

Safety: if they touch, lean in hard, or follow the topic after headphones, you get up and move seats or stand near the driver. That's not jargon. That's space. Don't debate attachment theory in the aisle.

### User
They just asked why I'm being "closed off." That's more of the same, right?

### Assistant
Yes. "Closed off" is the same family of words: they're naming your boundary as a personality flaw so you'll open to prove you're healthy. You don't have to prove health to a night bus.

Answer, if you answer: "I'm not talking about dating." Then headphones. If you don't answer, that's also an answer.

Don't teach them why the question is intense. Teaching becomes a new conversation, which is how twelve minutes turn into their stop plus yours.

When you stand to get off, a nod is optional. "Have a good night" is optional. You can just leave. Interns over-close. The bus doors are the close.

After you're off: you don't need to replay whether you were cold. Cold is a weather word. You were a person who didn't want to do a therapy intake at 11 p.m. next to a window.

If you see them on this route again, you can sit farther up from the start. That's planning, not paranoia.

You wanted a translation and an exit. The jargon was closeness-words used too fast. The exit is one sentence, headphones, move if you need, get off. No TED talk. That's the whole ride.
