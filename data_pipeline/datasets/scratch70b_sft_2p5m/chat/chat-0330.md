### User
Yesterday in standup I said we should split the onboarding email into two sends so the password step isn't buried. People nodded. Today in the planning meeting, Dana said, "Quick summary of where we landed: I'll own a two-email onboarding flow." No mention of me. Our manager wrote it on the board under Dana's name. I sat there like a houseplant. I don't want to be the person who says "actually that was mine" in front of eight people. I also don't want this to be how credit works here. What do I do, in the room and after?

### Assistant
In the room, you do not need a speech. You need one clean sentence that puts the idea back on the table without prosecuting Dana.

If the meeting is still going, wait for a pause after the board write. Then: "That's the two-send idea from standup — I can draft the first email this week if Dana's taking the second." You are not saying "thief." You are attaching a task to a person who already spoke. Managers remember tasks more than origin stories.

If that window closed, do not rewind the whole meeting. Catch it at the end: "Can we put both names on that board line? I raised it yesterday; Dana's offering to own the build." Calm. Specific. No joke to soften it. Jokes sound like you are not sure.

After the meeting is also a real option, and sometimes the better one if your voice is still in your throat. Slack or a hallway, not a performance. Same facts.

Was the manager in standup yesterday, or only in today's planning meeting?

### User
Manager was in standup. They even said "interesting" when I said it. Today they just wrote Dana's name. Dana and I are the same level. Dana is faster at talking. I freeze, then I rewrite the conversation in my head for three hours.

### Assistant
Your manager heard you and still labeled the work with the louder voice. That is annoying and common. It does not mean you have to become louder as a personality. It means you close the loop in writing while the meeting is still warm.

Send a short note today, not tomorrow. Tomorrow looks like rumination. Today looks like project hygiene. To the manager, copy Dana if you can stand it: "Confirming the two-email onboarding split from standup. I can draft email one; Dana offered to own the flow in planning. Want us both on the ticket?" That sentence does three jobs: reminds them of standup, assigns you a piece, and invites Dana to stay in the work without a trial.

If you will not copy Dana, send it to the manager only and say the same thing. Then do the draft. Credit sticks to finished sentences more than to who spoke first.

In the next meeting, when someone "summarizes," you can interrupt with a tool, not a feeling: "I'll add that to the ticket." Open the ticket on the projector if there is one. Names in the ticket beat names in the air.

Do not write a private novel about Dana. Do not ask friends "am I crazy." You are not crazy. You were first. Dana was faster. Both can be true. Your job is the ticket, not the character study.

### User
Dana already created the ticket with only their name. I saw it ten minutes ago. If I comment, it looks petty. If I don't, the history is just Dana.

### Assistant
Comment. Petty is a story you are telling yourself. A one-line comment is normal ticket hygiene.

Write: "Adding myself — I proposed the two-send split in standup yesterday and can draft email one." Then assign yourself or ask the manager to. Do not editorialize. Do not write "for the record." "For the record" sounds like a deposition.

If Dana replies with "oh I thought we were just riffing," you can say, "All good — putting both names on so the draft doesn't stall." You are offering speed, not a verdict.

If the manager later says they want one owner, ask which owner, out loud, in Slack. "Who should draft email one?" Then do that work if it is you, and let the ticket show it. If it is Dana, you still have the comment. That is enough history for a later review, and it is not a campaign.

For the next hour: comment, offer the draft, stop rehearsing the meeting. The houseplant version of you already sat through planning. This version leaves a trail that a board marker cannot erase. You do not need Dana to confess. You need your name on the work you are about to do.
