### User
I found this leaflet in the kitchen. It's from the clinic my dad went to last week. There's a whole page of side effects in tiny print and half of them sound like they belong in a disaster movie. He's been quieter than usual and I don't want to freak him out, but I also don't want to pretend I understood it. How do I even talk about this without sounding like I know more than I do?

### Assistant
You don't have to decode the leaflet, and you don't have to pretend you did. A lot of these sheets list things that *can* happen, not things that *will*. I can't tell you what the medicine is doing in his body, and I won't guess from a page of warnings. That would be bluffing.

A useful first line is: "I saw the paper from the clinic. I don't actually know what half of it means, but it looked intense. Are you okay talking about it, or do you want me to leave it?" That admits you don't know and gives him an exit.

One thing I don't have yet: is this a new prescription, a procedure follow-up, or something he already takes? That changes the question you ask.

### User
It's new. He started it after the appointment. The leaflet has words like "seek urgent care if" and then a list that includes chest pain, swelling, yellow skin, weird bruising. He's been sitting in his chair a lot. He says he's tired. That's it. No drama. I keep rereading the list like it's a riddle I'm supposed to solve.

### Assistant
You're not supposed to solve it. Those lists exist because clinics would rather print a scary net than miss a rare problem. That does *not* mean the list is happening to him. Tired after starting something new can be ordinary, or it can be the thing you mention to a nurse. I cannot tell which, and anyone who claims they can from a kitchen leaflet is performing.

Talk in plain language. Skip "adverse events." Try: "This paper made me nervous because it lists worst cases. I don't know if any of that applies. You've been extra tired. Is that new since the appointment?" If he shrugs, you can still say you'd feel better asking the clinic whether tiredness is expected this week. That is not a diagnosis. That is refusing to sit on a question you cannot answer.

If he gets defensive: "I'm not trying to manage you. I don't understand the sheet, and I don't like pretending I do." You're allowed to be fourteen and still say the paper is badly designed for humans.

### User
What if he asks me what I think the tiredness *means*? I don't want to invent an answer just to sound calm.

### Assistant
Then say you don't know. Out loud. "I don't know what it means. I'm not going to guess. We should ask someone whose job is this." That is not a failure.

Offer to sit next to him while he calls, or write the question down so he doesn't have to perform confidence on the phone: he started last week, more tired than usual, no chest pain or swelling that you've seen — is that expected, and what should make you call back? Stick to what you observed. Don't upgrade "quiet in a chair" into a syndrome.

I would not Google the brand name in front of him tonight. Search results love the worst paragraph. If you look anything up later, treat it as vocabulary, not a verdict.

### User
Okay. So the talk is: I saw it, I don't understand it, you seem more tired, do you want to call, and I won't fake a diagnosis. That feels almost too simple.

### Assistant
Simple is the point. Your job is not to match the leaflet's density. Your job is to not lie with confidence.

If he says he's fine and you still have a pit in your stomach, you can call the clinic and say you're his kid, you're not asking for private details, and you want to know whether extra tiredness in week one should be reported. They may say they can only speak to him. Then you hand him the phone with the question written. You still didn't bluff.

If anything on that urgent list actually shows up — chest pain, swelling, yellow skin, sudden bruising you can see — you don't debate the leaflet. You get a human who can look at him. Until then, unsure plus a phone call beats a made-up explanation that sounds calm for ten minutes and then falls apart.
