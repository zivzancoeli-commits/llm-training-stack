title: Talk through a first meeting with a new roommate's partner

### User
My roommate's partner is coming over tonight. We've never met. I moved in three weeks ago. I keep imagining myself either frozen by the fridge or oversharing about the recycling. I want to be decent, not audition for "fun third roommate." I'm new enough at adult living that I don't want to guess the etiquette wrong.

### Assistant
Kind and specific beats charming and vague. You don't need a persona. You need a few concrete moves so you're not inventing manners in real time.

Start with a short greeting that includes your name and one true detail, not a bit. "Hey, I'm Mara. I just moved into the back room." Then stop. Let them have a turn. People who are nervous fill the air with extra jokes. You don't have to.

Ask one question that is about them as a person, not about the relationship: what they do with weekends, whether they eat here often, if they have a food thing you should not wreck in the shared fridge. "Do you cook here a lot?" is kinder than "So how serious is this?" The second one is not your business on night one.

I don't know your house rules yet. Do you share a kitchen only, or is the living room basically theirs already? That changes how long you should linger.

### User
Kitchen and living room are shared. Their bedroom is down the hall. They've been together longer than I've lived here, obviously. My roommate said "just be normal," which is the least useful sentence I've heard this month. I don't want to vanish into my room like they're a fire drill, and I don't want to host.

### Assistant
"Be normal" is intern-speak for "please don't make this my problem." Translate it into actions.

Tonight, occupy the shared space for a bounded amount of time. Twenty minutes of overlapping presence is enough to show you're not hiding, and short enough that you're not hosting. Make tea. Wash the mug you already dirtied. Say hi, use names, ask the cooking-and-fridge question, mention one logistics fact they might need: "Trash goes out Tuesday. I labeled my shelf with the blue tape." Specific help is warmer than "mi casa." Then you can say, "I'll be in my room if you need the Wi-Fi password; it's on the router." That's an exit that isn't a slam.

If they offer a hug and you're not a hug person, a wave plus "I'm more of a handshake person" is kind and specific. Don't fake a hug. Don't start one to prove you're friendly.

Avoid teasing the couple, ranking the apartment, or asking when they're moving in. That conversation can arrive without your probe.

If the partner seems shy, match shy. Quiet is not rude. If they seem chatty, you can still keep your answers short and real: where you work in one sentence, what you're watching, whether you mind noise after ten. Pick one. Don't deliver a biography.

### User
What if my roommate leaves to take a call and I'm alone with the partner on the couch? That's the scene I'm dreading.

### Assistant
Have a small, true topic ready that isn't the relationship. The building's laundry card. The weird light switch. A show you both might have seen. One question, then you can look at your phone without performing fascination. You can also stand up: "I'm going to grab a glass of water — want one?" Tasks rescue intern-brain better than speeches.

If the silence gets heavy, name it lightly without making it their fault: "I'm a little rusty at first-meet small talk. I'm glad you're here, I'm just not a talk-show host." That's kind, specific, and it gives them permission to be ordinary too.

If they ask something too personal — rent, your dating life, why you moved — you can decline without frost: "I'm still settling, I'd rather keep that for later." Later can mean never. That's allowed.

When your roommate comes back, you don't need to recap the conversation like a report. A look that says you're fine is enough. You're not being graded.

### User
So: name, one true detail, fridge question, one logistics fact, twenty minutes, no couple-teasing, exit to my room with a reason. If I'm stuck on the couch, water or laundry card, not an interview.

### Assistant
Yes. Kind is the tone. Specific is the content. You are a roommate, not a mascot. Do those few things and you will have been decent, which is the actual assignment, even if nobody says "good job" at the end of the night.
