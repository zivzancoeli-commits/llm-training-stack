### User
Dinner at my uncle's. He knows I work with computers, which in his head means I can explain "the crypto." He wants to know if he should put some of his retirement into "that Bitcoin thing" because a guy at the gym made money. The table will go quiet and look at me. I am not a financial advisor. I can explain a database more easily than I can explain his gym friend. How do I handle this without a lecture or a hot tip?

### Assistant
You refuse the hot tip out loud. That is the whole job.

When the table looks at you, you can say, "I work with computers. I don't give investment advice, and I don't know your gym friend's situation." Then, if you want to be useful, you give a tiny plain-language picture without a buy or sell: people buy digital tokens on platforms; prices jump around; plenty of people lose money; plenty of stories at gyms are the winners talking. That is not a forecast. That is weather.

Do not sketch a plan for his retirement. I will not either. I won't quote returns, tax treatment, or what a "safe" slice is. Those are advisor questions, and dinner is not an advisor.

If he pushes "just tell me if it's real," you can say, "The tokens exist. That doesn't make them a good place for money you can't lose." Real is not the same as suitable.

Have you already been cast as the family tech priest, or is this a new assignment?

### User
I'm the priest. Last year they asked me about a printer and I never recovered. He already said he'll "just buy a little" if I say it's not a scam. So my shrug might get treated as a blessing.

### Assistant
Then you make the shrug unblessable. "I'm not saying buy a little. I'm saying I won't tell you to. If you want to put retirement money somewhere, talk to whoever already handles that, or a licensed person you choose. Not me, not the gym." Repeat if he jokes. Jokes are how blessings sneak in.

If you explain anything technical, keep it analog: it's more like collectible cards with a live price than like a savings account. If that metaphor feels sloppy, skip it. A bad metaphor becomes a family scripture. "I won't explain it in a way that sounds like a tip" is allowed.

Don't pull up a chart at the table. Charts look like a pitch. Don't name coins. Naming is shopping. Don't mock him. Mockery makes him double down with the gym guy, who at least sounds confident.

If another relative tries to translate you — "so they're saying it's fine" — you correct in the same breath: "That's not what I said. I said I won't advise." Then you ask someone to pass the dish. Passing food is a subject change that still looks like manners.

I will not tell him crypto is a scam or not a scam as a blanket. Some projects are frauds; some are just volatile. Sorting them is not a dinner trick, and it is not your unpaid job.

### User
What if he follows me to the driveway for "one honest opinion"?

### Assistant
Same opinion, colder air. "Honest opinion: I won't help you decide this. If you do anything, use money you can lose and a person whose job is this, not your nibling." Then you get in the car. Driveway talks are where priests get ordained by accident.

At dinner: no blessing, no chart, no coin names, one correction if someone mistranslates you. You can love your uncle and still not be his gym. Computers are not a crystal ball. Say that, eat, leave with your shrug unconverted. If grandma later says you "gave the okay," you correct her the same way you correct the table. The gym friend can keep his story. Your job is not to notarize it. Sit, pass the dish, go home still unordained.
