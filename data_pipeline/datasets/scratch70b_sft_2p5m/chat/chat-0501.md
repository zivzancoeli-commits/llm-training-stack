### User
We have a retro Friday. A coworker pinged me: "Want to skip and actually get work done? It's always the same anyway. If we both don't show, they can't make it about us." I don't love retros. I also don't want to be half of a silent protest. They made it sound like loyalty. Help me answer without becoming the team's hall monitor.

### Assistant
Don't skip as a pair to dilute blame. That's not loyalty. That's a buddy system for absence.

You can skip only if you independently have a reason your lead already accepts — a conflict, a sick day, a deadline they know about. "It's always the same" is a feeling, not a calendar exception. If you don't have a real conflict, you go, or you tell the person who runs the retro that you have a conflict. You don't conspire.

Reply to the ping: "I'm going to attend. I don't want to skip as a pair." If you agree the meeting is stale, that's a different sentence for the retro itself: "We're repeating items. Can we pick one and actually assign it?" That's participation, not a walkout.

I will not invent your company's attendance rule. I don't know if retros are required. Required or not, recruiting you as cover is the part to refuse.

Have they skipped before, or is this a new campaign?

### User
They skipped last month and joked that nobody noticed. I noticed because I got asked where they were. So "they can't make it about us" is already false. I don't want to snitch and I don't want to lie if someone asks me on Friday.

### Assistant
Someone already asked you last month. That's the future. If you skip together, you inherit a question you will have to answer. "I don't know" is a lie if you do know. "They asked me to skip" is snitching if you didn't agree to be a reporter. The clean path is not to be in the story: you show up.

You don't volunteer last month's joke to a manager. You also don't cover. If asked where they are on Friday, you say you don't know their status, which will be true if you didn't coordinate. That's why you refuse the plan.

If they push "come on, it's forty minutes of stickies," you can say, "Then it's forty minutes. I'm still going." You don't have to defend agile as a religion. You have to not be their alibi.

During the retro, if it is actually the same, you can be the person who names the loop once. One specific leftover action from last time. Then you stop speechifying. A stale retro gets worse when the only energy in the room is eye-rolling from people who almost didn't come.

### User
They reacted with "wow, okay corporate."

### Assistant
Let them have the nickname. You are not required to prove you're cool by hiding. Corporate, in this case, means you won't fake a double no-show. That's a low bar.

Don't write a paragraph about professionalism. Don't apologize for going to a meeting on the calendar. Send nothing, or send "see you there." Then go, sit, say one true thing if it helps, leave.

If they skip anyway and someone asks you in the retro where they are, you still don't cover and you still don't volunteer last month. "I don't know their status" remains true if you didn't coordinate. If they ping you from a cafe during the retro — "tell them I'm in a dentist" — you don't. A second lie is not made smaller by the first nickname.

You can still like this person. You can still eat lunch. You don't have to join a protest of two. Stale stickies are a meeting design problem. They are not solved by emptying two chairs.

Their loyalty test was cheap. You didn't fail it. You declined a buddy skip. That's the whole team activity you actually owe. Go on Friday. Be boring. Let "corporate" mean you showed up for the calendar you already have.
