### User
I have to put a 30-minute meeting on people's calendars for Thursday. Usually I send a blank invite titled "quick sync" and then we waste ten minutes asking why we're here. My manager said, in passing, "put an agenda in the invite this time." I have never done that without sounding like I'm running a corporate retreat. There are four of us. The actual point is: decide whether we ship the help-doc rewrite this sprint or park it. I don't want a five-bullet manifesto. I also don't want to show up and have someone say they didn't know they needed to read anything.

### Assistant
An agenda is a grocery list, not a keynote.

Title the invite with the decision, not the vibe. "Help-doc rewrite: ship this sprint or park" is better than "quick sync." People scan titles on their phones. "Quick sync" means nothing.

In the description, three lines beat five bullets. Something like: Goal: decide ship vs park this sprint. Bring: current outline link, any blocker in one sentence. Out: a yes/no and an owner if yes. That is an agenda. It is also the meeting.

Attach the outline if it already exists. Do not attach a 20-page draft and call it "light pre-read." If you need them to read one page, say "please skim the outline (5 min) before Thursday." If you do not need pre-read, say "no pre-read; we'll look live." Ambiguity is how people show up unprepared and then blame the invite.

Thirty minutes is enough for one decision. Do not add "also quick thoughts on tone" unless you want a second meeting to crash into the first.

Who owns the calendar — you, or does your manager need to be the one whose name is on the box?

### User
I own it. Manager will attend. One person on the invite is always late and never reads descriptions. Another will want to talk about screenshots for twenty minutes.

### Assistant
You still write the agenda for the people who will use it. The late person gets the same invite. You can put the decision in the title so even a lock-screen view works. At minute one, you read the goal out loud. That rescues the person who did not open the body.

For the screenshot person, put a fence in the agenda: "Screenshots only if they block the ship/park decision." When they start a gallery, you point at that line. You are not being rude. You are being the clock.

Send the invite now with a real end time. If you need a follow-up on screenshots, say so at minute 25 and offer a separate 15-minute hold, not a spontaneous extension that eats lunch.

Do not write "let's align" or "circle back" in the description. Those are weather. The description should be checkable. After the meeting, the notes are: shipped or parked, owner, date. You can paste that into the invite thread so the late person has it.

If your manager wanted an agenda as a test of seriousness, this is the serious version: short, decision-shaped, no retreat language.

### User
Is it weird to put "please be on time" in an invite? I feel like that's aimed at one person and everyone will know.

### Assistant
Skip "please be on time." It reads as a dunk, and the late person has heard it. Put "we'll start on the decision at :00 and end at :30." That is a schedule, not a scold. Start when you said. The late person walks into a decision already in motion. That is the natural consequence. Narrating their lateness in the invite is extra.

Send it today, not Thursday morning. Title with the fork. Three lines in the body. Link the outline. No pre-read theater. You are not running a retreat. You are trying to leave Thursday with a yes or a no. That is the whole point of putting words in the box instead of "quick sync." If someone replies "can we also talk X," park X in a new invite. Protect the thirty minutes you actually booked.
