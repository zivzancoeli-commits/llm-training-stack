### User
I'm at a place where the menu says the salad comes with "optional candied pecans." I do not want them. I have never sent a modification that wasn't a joke with friends. The server is about to come back. I feel like I'm going to say it as a question and then fold if they hesitate. I don't have an allergy I know of; I just don't want sweet nuts on greens. How do I ask without a speech or a fake allergy?

### Assistant
You ask for what you want. Optional means the kitchen already treats it as a toggle. You are not inventing a special diet. You are using the word they printed.

When they arrive, look at the menu, point if that helps, and say, "The salad, please — no pecans." That is the order. Not "is it okay if," not "this might be annoying." Questions invite them to talk you out of it. A statement is kinder to both of you because it is faster.

If they hesitate, they may be checking whether the pecans are mixed in already. Wait for that check. If they say it's already tossed, you can pick another dish or pick around them. You don't have to fake an allergy to win a toss. I will not encourage a fake allergy. That's how kitchens get dangerous for people with real ones, and it's a lie you don't need for a preference.

Don't explain your philosophy of savory salads. They have other tables.

Are you eating with people who tease when someone customizes, or are you alone?

### User
Two coworkers. One of them always says "just eat it" about food. The pecans aren't a crisis. I still don't want them. I also don't want a side conversation about being picky while the server is standing there.

### Assistant
Order to the server, not to the coworker. Eyes on the person taking the ticket. "Salad, no pecans." If the coworker jumps in with "just eat it," you can let that remark hit the air and still finish the sentence to the server: "Still no pecans, thanks." Then you hand the menu over. The server wants a clean ticket. You are giving them one.

After the server leaves, you don't have to litigate picky. A shrug works: "I don't want them." If the coworker keeps going, you can say, "You can eat yours how you want." Then ask about the meeting you are supposedly having lunch about. Coworkers who police plates will take a mile if you write an essay.

If the kitchen sends pecans anyway, you don't have to eat them and you don't have to make a scene. You can flag the server once: "I asked for no pecans — could I get one without, or I'll pick them off." Picking them off is allowed. Sending a plate back is allowed if you still don't want it. Choose the option that matches how much you care, not how much you want to prove a point to the coworker.

I won't tell you the kitchen "has to" remake it. Houses differ. Ask once, accept the answer, eat or swap.

No fake allergy at any step. Preference is already enough for an optional topping.

### User
The server is walking over and my mouth is dry. I might ramble.

### Assistant
Breathe out. One clause. "House salad, no pecans, please." If more words try to arrive, swallow them. If you ramble anyway, you can land the plane with the same clause at the end. They will hear the last clear instruction.

You are not requesting a moral exception. You are declining an optional garnish. Say it, hand over the menu, drink water. Lunch can be greens without candy. That's a normal ticket. If the coworker starts the "just eat it" line while the server is still there, you keep your eyes on the ticket. The pecans were optional on the page. Optional on the page is optional in the mouth. You already decided. One clause is the whole courage.
