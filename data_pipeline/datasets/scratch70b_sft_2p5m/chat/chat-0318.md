### User
I'm at a backyard thing. My cousin introduced me to her coworkers as "Nibbles," which is a name I have not used since I was nine. They laughed. I laughed. I hate it. She does this every time there are new people, like it's a bonding trick. I have two hours left here. I don't want a scene at the grill.

### Assistant
You can retire a nickname without a scene. The laugh you already did was social shock. It is not a lifetime license.

Use your actual name in the same breath, to the coworkers, not a speech at your cousin: "It's [name] — she likes old nicknames." Smile if you have to. Then use your name again when you shake a hand. People will follow the last label they hear if you repeat it.

Your cousin may keep going. Then you get one private sentence by the drinks table, not the grill audience: "Please use [name] with people I don't know. Nibbles is for family if I even want that, and I don't tonight."

I will not tell you the nickname is legally anything. It is a social habit. Habits break when the person who does them gets a consistent no.

Do you still answer to it with grandparents, or is it only her?

### User
Grandparents, sometimes, and I let it go because they are old. She is my age. She already said "don't be sensitive" to someone else about a different joke. So I know my odds.

### Assistant
"Don't be sensitive" is her tell. You still ask. You do not argue sensitivity as a concept. You name the behavior: not in front of new people, not at work-adjacent parties.

With the coworkers, keep introducing yourself correctly. If she yells Nibbles across the yard, you can raise a hand and say your name as you walk, like a translation. It feels extra. It works.

You can let grandparents have a pass without giving her one. Different relationships, different energy. That is not hypocrisy. That is triage.

If she doubles down to get a laugh, you do not have to play along the third time. A non-laugh is information for the circle. You do not need to scowl. You can just not perform.

Two hours: you can also drift to a different knot of people. You do not have to remain at the cousin-plus-coworkers cluster as a mascot.

### User
If I correct her in front of them I'll look like I can't take a joke. That is the trap.

### Assistant
The trap is real. Correcting the guests with your name, and her later by the drinks, splits the audience so you are not putting her on stage. If she puts you on stage anyway, a calm "I go by [name]" is a joke-take so small it barely counts as conflict.

For the next ten minutes: reintroduce yourself to the two coworkers you will actually talk to, get a plate, stand not at her elbow, and if she uses it again, translate once. Save the private sentence for a refill moment. Then eat. You do not need to win the yard.

Nibbles is a kid. You are at a party with adults. Use the adult name. Let her be slightly annoyed. Annoyed is cheaper than two hours as a punchline. The grill does not need a scene. It needs you to stop answering to a name you outgrew.

### User
One of the coworkers already used it back to me. Do I correct them too, or only her?

### Assistant
Correct the coworker once, lightly: "I go by [name] — old family joke." Then smile and ask about their job. They will copy whatever you answer to. If you keep answering to Nibbles, the name sticks for the rest of the afternoon.

Your cousin can hear that correction. That is useful. You are not scolding her in public; you are labeling yourself. If she pouts, let her. You still have a plate and two hours. Use the adult name every time someone tries the other one. Consistency is the whole trick, not a speech at the grill.
