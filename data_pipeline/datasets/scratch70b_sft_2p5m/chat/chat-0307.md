### User
We're on the holiday Zoom. My niece is six and finally started to show a drawing. My uncle keeps talking over her with a story about his knee. She went quiet and is now coloring off-camera. My sister looks tired. I am not the parent. I am the aunt/uncle on a laptop in a kitchen. I don't want a fight in the Brady Bunch squares and I also don't want the kid to learn that Zoom is for adults with knees.

### Assistant
You can make space without putting your uncle on trial. Kids disappear fast on calls. Adults fill silence because silence feels like a technical problem.

Do not mute him as a stunt unless you are the host and that is a last resort. Muting an elder on a family call becomes the story. Use your voice first, directed at the kid, not at him: "I want to see the drawing — hold it up again?" Names the object. Pulls the camera. If he starts the knee, you can say, "Uncle, two minutes, I don't want to miss the picture." Then look at the kid.

I do not know your family's rank order. Some uncles take a cue. Some double down. You only get one light redirect before it is a conflict.

Are you the host with mute powers, and is the kid in the same house as your sister or on her own tablet?

### User
Sister's tablet, she is holding it. I am not host. Grandma is host and she is not muting anyone. My uncle is loud because he doesn't wear his hearing thing. The drawing is a blue dog.

### Assistant
Blue dog is enough to work with. You can be specific: "Is the dog's name on the picture?" Questions to the kid are a lane. If you only lecture the uncle, the kid still does not get a turn.

If sister's face is tired, you are backing her, not replacing her. A private text to sister while you smile on camera: "Want me to keep asking about the drawing?" If she says no, you stop. She lives with the aftermath. You do not.

If she says yes or does not answer, keep the questions short so the kid can nod. Loud uncle plus a hearing gap means he may not know he crushed the moment. After the kid is done, you can tell him off-call, not in the Brady grid: "She was showing a drawing and it got lost. Next time let's let her finish." Today, in the grid, protect the two minutes.

If he talks over again, repeat the kid's name and the dog. Do not explain neuro anything. Do not diagnose shyness. Invite: "Show us the tail."

Grandma-as-host who will not mute is a constraint. Work inside it. You can also chat the host "can we let [kid] go first" if grandma reads chat. Some do not.

### User
If I text my sister during the call she'll think I'm criticizing her parenting. I don't mean that.

### Assistant
Then skip the text, or make it only "blue dog is great" with no strategy. Do the redirect yourself so she does not have to manage you plus the uncle.

For the next ten minutes: ask to see the blue dog, hold a two-minute lane with one uncle cue if needed, clap when the kid is done, then let the knee story exist. After you hang up, one sentence to your sister: "I loved the drawing." Not a debrief unless she starts it. If you still need to talk to the uncle, a later phone call, not a pile-on in the Zoom chat.

The kid learns what the call is for by who gets a turn. You can give a turn without a family trial. Blue dog, two minutes, then the adults can have their knees. That is a decent holiday.
