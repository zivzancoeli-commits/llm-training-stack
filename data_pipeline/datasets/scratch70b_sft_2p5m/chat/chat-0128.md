### User
Landlord texted "quick look at the sink, ten minutes." That was forty minutes ago. He's still here, opening cabinets, asking about my weekend, standing in the doorway of the bedroom. I'm on my break, already tired, and I can feel myself about to either snap or start fake-laughing. I need to get him out without turning this into a war.

### Assistant
Don't fake-laugh. Don't snap. Use bored-and-clear, like you're ending a meeting that ran over.

Walk to the sink — the thing he named — and say, "The ten minutes is up. If the sink's done, we can stop here. If you need another visit, text a time." Then stand near the door of the room he's in, not blocking like a bouncer, just making the exit the obvious next object.

If he's in the bedroom doorway, don't joke about it. Say, "That's my room. We can stay in the kitchen." Wait. Don't fill the silence with "haha no worries." Silence is allowed. Fake cheer tells him the extra time is free.

I won't invent your lease. I don't know your notice clause. Read the PDF after he leaves unless he's opening drawers. During, you get the body out of extra rooms. After, you deal with paper.

Tired-coworker tone: low volume, short sentences, no smile you don't feel.

### User
He just asked if he can "take a peek at the smoke detector in the hall closet." That's not the sink.

### Assistant
You can say no to the extra peek without a story.

"Not today. Today was the sink. If you want the detector, send a time." That's a no. You didn't invent a yoga class or a sleeping roommate. You named the appointment he already used.

If he says "it'll take two seconds," you repeat: "Not today." Repeating is not a fight. Explaining your whole childhood is a fight.

If the detector is actually chirping and you want it fixed, that's a different sentence: "The sink is done. You can do the detector if we stay in the hall and we're out in five." You're trading on your terms, still not hosting a tour.

If you don't want him in the closet, you don't have to confess why. "The closet's not part of this visit" is enough.

De-escalation is not making him feel adored. Fewer rooms, shorter clock, don't match his chatty energy. Walk toward the exit.

If he picks at caulk or a window: "Text me a list. I'm not doing a full walkthrough now." Write the list yourself after.

If you feel unsafe — not just annoyed — skip the clever lines. Neighbor, phone, leave if you can. Annoyance is this script. Fear is another person in the picture.

### User
Mostly annoyed. He's lonely, I think. I don't want to be his afternoon.

### Assistant
Then you don't have to be.

You can acknowledge the human without opening a second hour. "I need to get back to work" is true if you're on a break. You don't owe him a detailed calendar. If you're not actually working, "I need the apartment back" is also true. A ten-minute sink visit is not a social call.

Avoid the fake-cheerful trap of offering tea, asking about his kids, or laughing at a joke you didn't think was funny. That's how forty became forty. Kind is not the same as available.

When he's finally at the door: "Thanks for the sink. Next time, please stick to the thing in the text." Then close the door. You can thank him for the actual repair. You don't thank him for the extra thirty minutes.

After he's gone, a short record: "Today's visit ran 40 minutes. Going forward I need the listed issue only, and the time we agreed." Check the lease tonight if this is a pattern. If he replies wounded: "I still need the shorter visits."

Tired people over-explain or explode. The middle is two short sentences and a door.
