### User
Last-year me would have watched seven videos and then bought the wrong thing. The latch on my back door is busted. The little tongue that goes into the frame does not catch. Door still closes if I hip-check it but it does not stay latched. Small-town hardware, one aisle of locks, a person at the counter who will actually talk. Give me two options and which one you would pick. I am not trying to become a locksmith.

### Assistant
Two options, then a pick. Bring the broken latch if it unscrews. If it will not, bring a photo of the door edge and the hole in the frame, plus how thick the door is. A phone photo is enough.

Option A: replace just the latch piece — the part in the door edge with the spring tongue — with a matching one. Same size, same backset, which means the distance from the door edge to the knob center. Small-box, swap-the-guts. You keep the knob if the knob is fine.

Option B: replace the whole passage set, knob plus latch, as a kit. Do this if the knob is wobbly, the rose is cracked, or you cannot find a latch that matches. Still a screwdriver job on a standard door. If it is a weird old mortise lock, tell the counter before they hand you a box.

I would pick A if the knob feels solid and the latch comes out in two screws. I would pick B if you are already fighting the knob. Last-year you bought random because you guessed. This-year you let the counter compare the old part to the bin.

Is this a yard door that should lock, or just a click-shut door?

### User
Yard door. It used to lock with the little button on the knob. Button still turns but the tongue is what fails, so lock or not, it does not grab the frame. I do not know backset. I have a tape measure. I would rather not buy a whole new knob because it matches the front door. Also I keep thinking I should "adjust the strike plate" because a video said that.

### Assistant
Strike plate is the metal rectangle on the frame the tongue slides into. Sometimes a door sags and the tongue hits the plate instead of the hole. That is different from a dead spring in the latch. Check in ten seconds: with the door open, thumb the tongue. Does it spring out and in, or is it limp. Limp is the latch. If it springs but misses the hole, look at whether the plate is high or low. I would not start by chiseling the frame. Last-year you would.

Yard door that should lock: you need a latch that still works with the button, not a hallway passage latch. Say at the counter: button on the knob, tongue does not throw. I will not invent a brand or a price. Ask them to match the old tongue length so it actually reaches the plate.

Keep the knob if you like the match. That is Option A, recommended. Measure backset: from the edge of the door to the center of the knob. Write it on your hand. Do not say I think it is the standard one.

If the strike is the miss, they can tell you to loosen two screws and shift the plate a hair. Shifting a plate is cheap. Replacing a matching knob because a video said so is how you own two styles.

### User
What do I say so I do not get the whole "are you a homeowner" quiz. I rent. Landlord is slow. I just want the door to click.

### Assistant
You say I rent, I want the door to latch and lock like it did. I would like to replace the latch and keep this knob. Put the old part or the photo on the counter. You do not owe a lease reading. If your lease wants written permission for lock changes, that is your paperwork, not mine to invent. A same-size latch swap is often treated like a repair. A whole new keyed set can annoy a landlord. Another reason A is the pick.

Two options restated: A, match and swap the latch, keep the knob, recommended. B, full kit if A will not seat or the knob is dying. Strike-plate nudge only if the tongue is still springy and missing the hole.

Walk in, measure on your hand, old part in a bag, one sentence. You leave with a small box, not a new identity as a locksmith. Last-year you watched the strike-plate video until dark. This-year you is in and out before the counter person finishes a coffee.
