### User
My friend Priya used to be funny. Now every hang is a status meeting. Slack drama, her manager's calendar, the deck, the deck again. I like her. I do not like being her unpaid standup. Last Saturday I asked how her weekend was going and she answered with a sprint board. I laughed because I didn't know how to stop it. I don't want to be mean. I also don't want another two hours of Jira. What do I say?

### Assistant
Before I hand you a script: when you leave those hangs, what do you actually want more of? A different topic, a shorter visit, or fewer hangs until she's less underwater? Those are three different asks, and mixing them makes you sound like you're attacking her personality.

### User
I want the same number of hangs. I want twenty minutes of work max, then food, a show, a walk, anything with a plot that isn't her boss. I'm not trying to become her therapist or her exit plan. I just miss talking like people.

### Assistant
That's clear, and it's allowed. You're not asking her to quit. You're asking for a ratio.

Don't open with "you only talk about work." People hear that as "you're boring" and then they either defend the job or go quiet for a month. Open with what you want instead, plus one true observation.

Try this, in person, not a paragraph text: "I like hanging out. Lately almost the whole time is work talk and I check out. Can we cap work at the first bit and then do something else?" If you need a physical cap: "If we start on the deck, we switch when the food arrives." Specific beats noble.

If she asks whether she's annoying, answer the question she asked, not a TED talk about boundaries. "A little, yeah, when it's two hours of Slack. I still want to see you." Honesty without a character assassination.

You already almost have this. The missing piece is saying the cap before you're irritated, not after you've laughed through another sprint board.

### User
She'll say work is her life right now and I wouldn't get it. She might be right. I have a normal job. Hers is a startup that eats people. I don't want to pretend I get the hours. I also don't want to nod for ninety minutes.

### Assistant
You can admit you don't live her hours without handing her the floor forever. "I don't get that schedule. I still don't want it to be the whole hang." That's not a debate about whose job is harder.

If she needs to vent, give it a container: "Ten minutes, then I'm changing the subject." Set a timer if you're both the type who lose track. When it rings, you change it. You don't wait for her to feel done. Venting is never done.

Have a next topic ready so the cap isn't a cliff. A show you both stalled on. The weird bakery. Her dog. Your dumb neighborhood mystery. Offer one. "Want to walk to the river after this?" is a subject change with a body.

If she keeps talking over the cap, interrupt once, kindly, with the plan: "I'm hitting pause on work. Come on, we're going." Standing up helps more than a speech.

You don't have to match her intensity to be a good friend. You have to show up and not disappear into polite laughter.

### User
What if I try this and she still only texts me screenshots of Slack? Do I just... not reply?

### Assistant
Reply, just not as a project manager. One line that names the pattern: "I'm not a good Slack reader tonight. Tell me one sentence if you need a yes or no. Otherwise let's pick a night for actual hanging." Then stop answering thread by thread. Matching screenshot energy trains the channel.

If a screenshot is actually "should I send this email," that's a yes/no. Answer yes/no. Don't workshop the tone unless you enjoy it.

Keep the Saturday plan. The hang is where the ratio lives. Texts can stay small.

You're not mean for wanting a conversation with a plot. Say the cap. Mean the cap. Leave the laugh-track version behind.
