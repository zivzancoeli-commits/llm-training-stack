### User
The storm took the power on our block for a night. Power came back. The house two doors down still runs a generator from morning until late. It sits near the property line. Our windows rattle. I understand a night of it. We are on day four. I don't know the people well. I waved after the storm. I don't want to be the neighbor who complains about safety. I also cannot take another evening of the drone. Do I knock, note, or wait for them to notice they have grid power?

### Assistant
Four days after the grid is back is a different conversation than the night of the storm. You can acknowledge the storm and still ask about the noise.

Knock in daylight, not at 10 p.m. when you are raw. "Hi — glad you got through the storm. Our power's been on. The generator is really loud at our place. Any chance you can shut it down in the evenings, or move it if you're still using it?" Then you listen. They may have a reason: a fridge on a separate line, medical equipment, a cautious delay. You will not know until you ask.

I will not invent whether generators are legal on your street or how many decibels are allowed. If the city has a noise number, you can use it later. First is a human ask.

Do not lecture them about the climate or about "the grid." Ask for evening quiet or a relocation farther from your wall.

Have you confirmed your own power is stable, including evenings, or are you still blinking?

### User
Our power has been fine since Monday night. Their porch lights are on too, which is why I'm confused. Maybe they are running it for a sump or a workshop. I don't know. I work from the back room which faces them. Headphones help until the rattle.

### Assistant
Porch lights on and a generator still running is a fair reason to knock. Confusion is information you can say out loud: "I might be missing something — our block has power. Is the generator still needed?" That is less of an attack than "turn it off."

If they need it for a pump, you can ask about hours. "Could it stop after 8?" is a negotiation. If they need it all day, ask whether it can sit on the far side of their house. You cannot redesign their lot. You can request a direction.

If nobody answers, a note with your name and number: same questions, plus "happy to text." Do not write a manifesto about day four.

For your workday: back room is the worst room. If you have another corner for calls this week, use it. That is a patch, not a surrender.

If they get defensive about "you don't know what we went through," you can say, "The storm was bad. The evenings are hard on our side now that the grid is up." Then stop competing for who suffered.

### User
What if they ignore the note and it runs through the weekend, and I start thinking about calling the non-emergency line? I don't want a feud.

### Assistant
A feud starts faster with a surprise official visit than with a note you signed. Try the door, then the note, then a second polite ask. If you use a non-emergency line later, that is your call after you have asked. I will not tell you they will send anyone. Some towns care about generators. Some do not.

This afternoon: knock, one question, one evening ask. If you hear a reason, you can live with a defined window for a few more days. If you hear nothing, the note. Keep the storm gratitude in the first sentence so you are not only the rattle. Then go back to the other corner of the house and do your work. Four days is enough to speak. Speak while it is still daylight.
