### User
My aunt forwarded a PDF. Subject: "tell me what this says." It is a letter on lawyer letterhead. I took two years of Spanish in school. This is not Spanish class. There are words I kind of recognize and a deadline that looks like a date. She wants an answer before dinner because she is scared. I am scared of being wrong. She thinks "you went to college" means I can translate legal mail. How do I help without pretending I understood a contract?

### Assistant
You do not translate it. You help her get a real reading.

Say that plainly: "I can't reliably translate a legal letter. I might get the date wrong. We need someone whose job is this." College is not a law license and it is not fluency.

What you can do before dinner: look at the letterhead and the date with her, help her find a legal aid clinic, a bar association referral line, or a trusted person who actually reads that language and legal mail. Help her make the call. Sit with her while she scans the PDF to them if they accept email. That is help.

Do not paste it into a random website and announce a verdict. Do not guess "this means you owe" or "this means ignore." Wrong help is how deadlines die.

I will not interpret the PDF. I cannot see it, and I am not her lawyer.

Is the letter in Spanish, or another language, and does she have anyone besides you who actually speaks it daily?

### User
It's Spanish. She speaks it. She reads it slowly. She wanted me because she thinks official words are a trick. I speak it worse than she does. There's a date about two weeks out. She is at my kitchen table.

### Assistant
Then she may understand more of the Spanish than you. The "trick" feeling is about institutions, not about your college.

Sit down and have her read it out loud to you in Spanish, then tell you in the language you share what she thinks it says. You listen for a date, a phone number, a court or office name, an amount if there is one — without confirming the amount as truth. Write those on paper. That is a map, not legal advice.

Then you search, together, for help: legal aid, a tenants' or consumers' clinic if the letterhead suggests that world, a trusted bilingual attorney referral. I will not name a fake clinic. Use real local names she already knows or a government legal-aid search. Call before dinner if lines are open. Leave a voicemail if not.

You can say, "I won't tell you what it legally means. I will help you call someone who can." Repeat if she pushes.

Two weeks is a reason to start the call tonight, not a reason for you to become the translator of record.

### User
She asked me to "just tell her if she should ignore it." I know that's the trap.

### Assistant
It is the trap. You do not answer ignore-or-not.

"I won't tell you to ignore a lawyer letter. Let's get someone who can read it with you." If she is disappointed, she can be disappointed and still use your kitchen as a call center.

If the letter has a phone number on the letterhead, she can call that number later to ask what the letter is, after she has advice, or she can wait for the clinic. I will not tell her to call opposing letterhead blindly. I will tell you not to green-light ignoring it.

Help is the table, the call, the refusal to fake fluency. Dinner can still happen. The PDF does not become your homework. You went to college. That does not make the letter yours. Get her a reader who is actually qualified. Stay while she starts that. Then you can eat. That is the honest version of "tell me what this says." You said: I can't, and here is the next human who might. That is enough, and it is safer than a bluff at a kitchen table.
