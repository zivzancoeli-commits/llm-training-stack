### User
In standup this morning Dana recapped the client timeline like it was something she put together. It was my spreadsheet. I built the dates, I flagged the two risks, I even put the little note about the vendor lead time. She said "we mapped this out" and then walked through my rows almost word for word while I sat there with my camera on like a houseplant. After the call two people pinged me "nice work on the plan" so it is not like nobody knows, but Dana is the one who spoke. I was about to send her a Slack that was not my finest writing. I need a next move that is not a blowup and is not me swallowing it until the next time.

### Assistant
Before I pile advice on you: did Dana have that spreadsheet in front of her because you had already shared it with her as a working draft, or did she present it as if the work originated in the meeting?

### User
I had shared it with her yesterday as "here's the draft, can you look before we show it." She sent a thumbs-up and one comment about a date that was already right. This morning she screenshared her copy, which was my file with her name in the footer because she exported it. So yes, she had it because I handed it to her. And also she walked through it like she was the author. I was one sentence away from jumping in and I froze.

### Assistant
That is a different problem than a random grab in a public channel. She had legitimate access. She still presented your work as if the thinking was hers. You almost had the right instinct: the correction belongs in the room, or immediately after, not in a novel-length Slack.

You already know most of the move. You do not need a confrontation speech. You need one factual sentence in a place people can hear, plus a quieter boundary about how you share drafts.

In the next meeting, or in the thread that followed standup if there is one, you can say: "Quick clarification on the timeline: I put the draft together yesterday. Dana reviewed it. The vendor lead-time note is mine; happy to walk through the two risk flags if useful." That is not a prosecution. It is labeling the work. People who pinged you already suspect this. You are making it easy for them to attach the credit to the right person without forcing them to pick a side.

If you would rather not do it in front of the whole standup, send Dana a short message first, then put the same clarification in the project channel:

"Hey — this morning it sounded like the timeline was a joint walkthrough. I need my name on the draft I built. Next time, please intro it as my plan you reviewed, or pass me the screen share. I will do the same for your docs."

The second sentence is the actual fix. Credit arguments that stay about feelings go in circles. Credit arguments that change the intro sentence next week tend to stick.

You froze because the social cost felt high in a live call. That is ordinary, not a character flaw. The freeze does not mean you lost the point. It means you say the sentence now, while the meeting is still in people's heads, instead of waiting until the story hardens.

What you should not do: the unfinest Slack. Sarcasm in writing lives forever and makes you the emotional one even when you are right. Also do not go to her manager first unless this is a pattern you already documented. One meeting is a correction. A pattern is a different conversation, with dates.

You were already ninety percent of the way there: you noticed it, other people noticed it, and you wanted a clean next step instead of a blowup. Send the short message. Put your name back on the file footer. Next draft, share a link that still shows you as owner, not an export she can rebrand. That last bit is boring and it prevents the sequel.
