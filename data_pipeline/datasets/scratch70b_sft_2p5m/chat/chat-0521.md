### User
My aunt gave me a giant scented candle that I cannot have in the house — I get headaches from that kind of perfume, and I also don't have a safe place to burn it. She asked "do you love it?" while I was holding it. I said it was pretty. She is visiting through Sunday. I don't want to lie for three days and I don't want to crush her. She already said she can "get me more in that scent." Help me tell the truth without a seminar on fragrance.

### Assistant
Pretty was a dodge. You can still correct it before more scent arrives.

Kind and true can be the same short sentence: "Thank you for thinking of me. I can't use scented candles — they give me a headache. I don't want you to buy more." You don't need a lecture on phthalates. You don't need to insult her taste. The headache is a body fact. The no-more is the important clause.

If she looks deflated, you can add one place for the care to land: "I loved that you brought something." Then you stop. Don't invent a fake favorite smell for her to hunt. That's how you get a different candle tomorrow.

I will not diagnose your headaches or tell you a brand is safe. If a clinician already told you to avoid fragrance, you can say that in one clause. If not, "they give me a headache" is enough.

Are you willing to let her take it back, or do you just need her not to buy a sequel?

### User
I can let her take it back. I don't want to put it in the trash in front of her. She wraps things like they're ceremonial. There's a lot of paper. I feel like the paper is part of why "pretty" came out of my mouth.

### Assistant
Paper is a trap. Unwrap the feeling from the object. You can keep the paper in the memory and still refuse the smoke.

When you talk, have the candle in sight so it isn't an abstract betrayal. "I should have said this when I opened it. I can't use it. Would you like it back for someone who can, or should I pass it to a friend who likes this scent?" Offering her a dignified landing — back to her, or onward to a friend — is kinder than a trash can ceremony. If you don't have a friend who wants it, don't invent one. Take-back is cleaner.

If she says "just try it once," you can say, "I know my head. I won't try it." Trying once is how a visit becomes a migraine and a story about how you didn't even give it a chance.

Don't perform a big apology. Over-apology teaches her that asking "do you love it" is a test you failed, not a question that deserved an answer. You answered late. Late is still useful before Sunday's sequel shopping.

If other relatives are in the room, you can still say it. Audience makes it harder, not dishonest to wait for a kitchen moment. Kitchen is fine. Don't wait until the airport.

### User
What if she says I used to like her candles when I was a kid?

### Assistant
Kids and adults have different heads. "I did like them then. I don't tolerate the scent now." That's a closed timeline. You don't have to litigate every holiday from 2009.

Tell her before she shops. Thanks, headache, no more, take it back or I'll pass it on if that's true. Kind is specific. Pretty was fog. You can love your aunt and still not burn her gift. That's a complete thank-you, just a later one. If she wraps a sequel anyway, you don't have to light it to prove gratitude. Gratitude can be the sentence. The wick can stay unused. Sunday still counts as in time.
