### User
Book club is in forty minutes. I read forty pages and then my brain filed it under "later." Later never came. The host sent a list: "unreliable narrator," "liminal space," "the ending's negative capability," "how the motif of water interrogates domestic labor." Everyone else is going to nod. I don't want to fake-read and I don't want to sit there like I got hit by a truck. Translate this into normal human so I can be in the room without performing a paper.

### Assistant
You didn't fail a class. You missed homework. Forty minutes is not a speed-run. It's enough to know what the fancy words mean so you can ask real questions.

Unreliable narrator: the person telling the story might be wrong, lying, or only seeing their slice. Not "the author is bad at writing." You can say, "I don't know yet if I trust this voice. They keep explaining how reasonable they are." That's the actual idea.

Liminal space: a doorway situation. Not home, not the destination. Airports, night hallways, a kitchen at 2 a.m. People say "liminal" when they mean "this scene feels like waiting." If you saw a bus station in those forty pages, use it. If you didn't, don't invent one.

Negative capability, in regular talk: sitting with "I don't know" without punching the story until it gives you a moral. The host probably means the ending doesn't wrap like a sitcom. You can say you haven't reached it and you hope it doesn't explain itself to death.

Motif of water interrogates domestic labor: they think the book keeps putting water next to housework — dishes, laundry, baths — on purpose, about who does the boring wet jobs. "Interrogate" just means "pokes at." If you saw a sink, ask whether we're supposed to notice who is always at it.

Which of those words is actually on the printed list you're scared of?

### User
All of them are on the printed list. There's also "diegesis" and "the male gaze as structure not just looking." I can fake a laugh. I cannot fake diegesis.

### Assistant
Don't fake the laugh as a substitute for a sentence.

Diegesis: the world inside the story, what the characters know as real. A song in the room they're in is in the world. A soundtrack only for you is outside it. Ask, "Do we think this character hears that, or is it just for us?"

Male gaze as structure, not just looking: not only "a guy stares." It means women get described like objects in a window while men get to act, and it keeps happening in how scenes are set up. If your forty pages were mostly a man's thoughts about a face, say that. If they weren't, don't force it. "I only got forty pages. What did you mean by structure?" puts the homework back on the person who used the phrase.

You can start with the truth: "I under-read. I have the opening, not the ending." Clubs that punish honesty are performances. You still don't owe a lie.

Skim the last five pages you did read. Pick one object — a sink, a bus ticket, a lie — and walk in with that. When the jargon hits, translate it to the object. You don't need the whole book to notice a pattern starting.

### User
What if they ask my favorite scene.

### Assistant
You name a scene you actually have. "The kitchen in chapter two, if that's a chapter, when they wouldn't say the thing." If they push past your pages, "I haven't gotten there. Don't spoil it unless we're spoiling." That's not jargon. That's a boundary a fourteen-year-old would respect, which is the right amount of respect for a Tuesday night.

Sit, drink whatever they're pouring, don't apologize every five minutes. One honest under-read, one object, three translated words. You can be in the room as a person who started the book. That's more useful than a person who memorized the host's list.
