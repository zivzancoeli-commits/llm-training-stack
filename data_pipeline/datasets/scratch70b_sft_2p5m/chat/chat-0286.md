### User
I always go home for the holiday. This year I said yes in July and now I cannot do it. Work is a mess, the tickets got expensive, and I am tired in a way that makes me mean on a three-day visit. I haven't told them. My mom already asked what pie I want. I feel like I'm canceling them, not a trip.

### Assistant
You are canceling a trip. They will feel it as canceling them. Both can be true. Waiting until the pie question sits on the table just makes the drop heavier.

Tell them before you buy a different plan, and before you vent to cousins who will forward it. The people who need the sentence first are the people who are holding a seat.

Do not lead with money if money is only part of it. Do not lead with "work is crazy" if the real line is that you are too tired to be a good guest. You can name more than one reason. You should not hide behind a fake one they will poke holes in.

I do not know your family's culture around holidays, guilt, or who else is coming. I will not script a speech that assumes they will be fine.

Have you already spent money that cannot be recovered, or is this still a "we assumed you'd fly" situation? And is this a phone call family or a text family when the news is bad?

### User
No tickets yet, they just assumed. Phone call will go worse because my dad gets quiet and my mom fills the quiet. I was going to text and then call after they read it. That might be cowardly.

### Assistant
A text that says the fact, then a call, is not cowardly. It is how you stop the first two minutes from being a gasp. The call is still required. A text alone will look like you slipped the news under the door.

Text first, short: "I need to say this before we keep planning food. I'm not coming home this year. I'm sorry. Can I call in twenty minutes?" That gives them a private reaction and you a buffer so you do not improvise.

On the call, do not stack every reason like a lawyer. Two is enough. "I'm wiped, and I cannot do the travel well this year. I wanted to tell you before pie plans got more real." If they ask whether this is about a fight, answer that honestly. If it is not, say "this is not a fight." If it is, do not use the holiday as a vague punishment. Name the fight later, separately.

Offer one concrete substitute so the conversation has a floor: a video call on the day, a visit in a quieter month, sending the pie you were going to eat. Pick one you will actually do. A fake later-visit is worse than none.

If they pile on "but Grandma," you are not obligated to solve the whole guest list. You can say, "I know it matters. I'm still not able to come." Repeat once. Do not debate the meaning of family until you are repeating yourself into a yes you will resent.

### User
If I cry on the phone they'll think they can talk me back into it. And my aunt will text me within an hour.

### Assistant
Crying does not mean you are available to be flipped. If they start negotiating, use a broken record: "I hear you. I'm not coming this year." Then ask a logistics question: "Do you still want me on the video call at 2, or is that more stressful?" Logistics are a way to leave the tug-of-war.

If the aunt texts, you do not owe her the first explanation. "I already talked to Mom and Dad" is a complete reply. If you want a sentence more, "I'm staying put this year" is enough. Do not litigate tickets with someone who was not going to pay for them.

For the next ten minutes: write the three-line text, send it, set a twenty-minute timer, drink water, and jot the two reasons plus one substitute on paper so the call has a spine. When the timer hits, call. If nobody picks up, leave a voicemail with the same two reasons and ask them to call back. Do not spend the evening in the family group chat.

You said yes in July when the holiday was a cartoon. You are allowed to update a cartoon. Tell them as adults. Stay off the pie thread until the call is done.
