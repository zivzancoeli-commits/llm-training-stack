### User
Sunday lunch. My aunt does a thing where she looks at my plate and then at my body like she's doing math. Last time she said "you're looking healthy" in the voice that does not mean healthy. I always laugh and change the subject. I am done laughing. I want one sentence I can use when she starts, including if she pretends it's concern. I don't want a scene in front of my parents. I also don't want to spend the car ride home dissecting my body with them as a follow-up.

### Assistant
You can refuse the topic without putting on a trial. The goal is a closed door, not a TED talk about diet culture at the table.

One sentence is enough if you will actually repeat it. Pick one and stop shopping for a perfect one. Examples that stay dry: "I'm not talking about my body." "Please don't comment on my weight." "That subject is closed for me." You do not owe a medical briefing. I will not invent what you should eat, what a number should be, or whether her "concern" is clinically justified. I am not your clinician and she isn't either just because she has eyes.

When she uses coded words — healthy, glowing, did you try, you used to — you treat them as the same comment. Don't debate the code. Name the category: "I'm not discussing my body." Then you put food in your mouth or you ask someone else a question about their job. The subject change is a physical act, not a speech.

Scenes happen when people argue the content. You are not arguing the content. You are declining the game.

Will she likely do this in front of the whole table, or in the kitchen when you're alone with the serving spoons?

### User
Both. Kitchen first, then a little remark at the table so everyone can hear she's being "nice." My mom will try to smooth it by saying auntie means well. That smoothing is its own pile.

### Assistant
Kitchen is easier because there is no audience to manage. If she starts there, you use the sentence once, then you pick up a dish and walk back to the table. You don't linger to make her feel understood. Understanding is how you used to laugh.

At the table, same sentence, lower volume, no smile you don't mean. You do not need to match her public volume. You need a boundary the people next to you can hear once. Then you talk to the cousin about school or the dog. If she repeats, you repeat the same words. New explanations are an invitation to negotiate.

When your mom says she means well, you can answer your mom later in the car, not as a second show. In the moment, a short "I heard you" to mom is optional. You don't have to fight your mother in front of the aunt. You also don't have to nod like the comment was a gift. Neutral face is allowed.

If they escalate — "we're family, we can say anything" — you can say, "Family can also drop this." Then you stop. You cannot control whether someone huffs. You can control whether you dissect yourself for their comfort.

I will not script a medical comeback. Don't invent a condition to make her stop. You don't need a diagnosis to own the topic of your body. "I don't discuss that" is complete.

### User
What if she brings a "helpful" article on her phone after lunch?

### Assistant
You don't take the phone. "I'm not reading that." Hands stay in your lap or on your keys. If she starts summarizing, you stand up to help with dishes or you go find your shoes. Leaving the room is not a tantrum. It is how you keep the car ride from starting early.

In the car, if a parent starts the autopsy — what you should have said, what she meant, whether you've gained — you use a version of the same door: "I'm not doing a recap of my body. Please drop it." If they keep going, you can put on a podcast or look out the window. You already did the hard part at lunch. You don't have to grade your performance.

Sunday: one sentence, repeat as needed, no laugh, no article, no car seminar. You are allowed to be a relative who eats and talks about other things. That is not a scene. That is you declining to be the math problem.
