### User
There's a person on my team who hits reply-all on every thread, including "thanks" and "got it" and sometimes whole conversations that didn't need twenty people. My inbox is a junk drawer. I don't want to be the office cop. I also don't want to keep muting things and missing the one email that matters. What's a move that isn't a passive-aggressive poster in the kitchen?

### Assistant
Two options, then I'll tell you which one I'd actually do.

Option A: you change how you receive mail. Filters, mute thread, "unread" only from certain people, a rule that files FYI lists. You never have the talk. Your inbox gets quieter. The reply-all person never learns. This is the path if you don't have standing, or if this person is senior and allergic to feedback.

Option B: you have one private, specific conversation. Not a team-wide note. Not a joke in Slack. You pick one recent thread, show the extra noise, and ask them to reply to the sender unless the whole list needs it. This is the path if you work with them daily and they're not cruel, just unaware.

I'd do B if they're a peer and the habit is new-ish. I'd do A if they're a vice president of thanks-emails. Most reply-all problems are unawareness, not a plot. Unawareness can take one sentence. Plots need a manager.

Is this person a peer you can talk to, or someone you'd only approach through your boss?

### User
Peer. Same level. We've even joked about email. I just never said it was them. If I go to my boss I'll look like I can't handle a small thing. I don't want a written HR moment either. I want my mornings back.

### Assistant
Then B, private, oral if you can, short Slack if you can't catch them. No paper trail speech.

Don't lead with "you always." Lead with one thread and the effect. "When the whole list gets 'got it,' I have to hunt for the actual ask. Could you reply to just me unless everyone needs it?" That's specific. It doesn't call them a child. It names the job: keep the list for things the list must see.

If they get embarrassed, let them. Embarrassment is how habits break. Don't pile on with three more examples. One is a request. Five is a case file.

If they say "I want to be transparent," translate that. Transparency is not the same as copying twenty people on a nod. You can say, "Transparency for decisions, sure. Thanks-notes can be one-to-one." You're offering a rule they can remember, not a vibe.

Keep doing A in the background anyway. Mute the loudest FYI list. That's not surrender. That's you not waiting for their personality to finish loading. A skeptical uncle would tell you not to bet your mornings on somebody remembering a chat. He'd also tell you not to CC the department about manners. Both can be true.

### User
What if they keep doing it after I ask? And should I say something in the moment the next time a thanks-all lands?

### Assistant
If they keep doing it, you don't escalate to a public shaming. You repeat the ask once, then you tighten your filters, and if it's actually breaking work — missed deadlines because signal is buried — you tell your boss about the inbox problem, not about their soul. "Our threads are noisy and I'm missing action items" is a work issue. "Dave says thanks too much" is a personality issue. Bosses can act on the first.

In the moment, I wouldn't reply-all with "please don't reply-all." That's a hall-of-mirrors joke and it adds another email. If you must, a private ping: "this one could've been just us." Once. Then stop coaching in real time. You're not their inbox tutor.

Two options were: change your mail, or have the one talk. Recommendation: have the one talk this week, set a filter today, and don't write a manifesto. That's how you get mornings back without becoming the kitchen poster.
