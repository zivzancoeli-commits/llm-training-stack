### User
The screen-door latch on the back porch gave up. The little metal tongue that catches the strike is bent, and the door slams and then yawns open when the wind hits. I'm standing in Miller's Hardware, which is one aisle of paint and one aisle of everything else. There's a kid at the register who asked if I need "a latch or a closer." I need the door to stay shut so the cat doesn't tour the alley. I'm not buying a new door. Talk to me like I already suspect you're going to overcomplicate this.

### Assistant
Two real options. I am not selling you a lifestyle.

Option A: replace just the latch set — the part on the door with the tongue, and maybe the strike plate on the frame if that is chewed up. You unscrew the old one, take it to the counter, match the size and the screw holes, screw the new one on. If the tongue is bent and the rest of the body is sound, some people only swap the latch mechanism. Matching the hole pattern matters more than matching the brand in your head.

Option B: add or replace a screen-door closer, the pneumatic tube at the top that keeps the door from flying and helps it pull shut. That does not fix a bent tongue by itself. It stops the slam-and-bounce that bent the tongue in the first place. If the latch still does not catch, the closer alone is a fancy slow-motion open door.

My recommendation: do A first, and only add B if the door is getting slammed by wind or by people who let it fly. You said the tongue is bent and the door yawns in wind. The catch is the failure. A closer is a helper. Fix the catch. Bring the bent piece to the kid. Say, "I need this latch, same size, for a wood screen door." Not a closer unless the old closer is missing or clearly dead.

I will not quote you a price. Miller's writes that on the sticker. I also will not invent a building code. You want the cat in and the alley out.

Does the strike plate on the frame still have a clean hole the tongue can enter, or is that metal chewed into an oval?

### User
The strike is a little chewed but the tongue is the obviously bent part. There's an old closer up top that leaks oily dust when I touch it. The kid started talking about "security latches" like someone is going to steal my recycling. The cat is named June. June is the whole security system.

### Assistant
Skip security latches. You are not fortifying a vault. You are keeping June from researching dumpsters.

Still two options, same recommendation with the extra detail: replace the latch, and while you are there, replace the closer because yours is already announcing itself with oily dust. That is not me upselling. A leaking closer is a closer that is done being useful. If you only have patience for one purchase today, buy the latch. Prop the door or hook a simple eye-and-hook for a day if wind is bad. Come back for the closer when you can stand another five screws at the top.

How to talk to the kid without a seminar: put the bent latch on the counter. "Match this. Screen door. I don't need a lock, I need it to catch." If he walks you to a wall of chrome, pick the one whose backset and screw holes line up with your old piece. Hold them together. If the holes miss, it will rattle forever.

For the chewed strike: if the tongue still drops into the opening and the door sits flush, leave the strike. If the opening is so oval the tongue slips past, replace the strike plate too. Same small packet, same screwdriver. Fill stripped screw holes with a toothpick and wood glue if the screws spin — that is a shop trick, not magic.

Install order when you get home: door off the wind if you can, or have someone hold it. Latch first, test the catch with the door almost closed, then adjust the strike by loosening screws and nudging a millimeter, not by bending the new tongue. Then the closer, if you bought it, set to a slow close so it does not slam the new latch to death.

June does not need a lecture. You need a tongue that meets metal. Buy the match for the part in your hand. Leave the recycling un-stolen. Walk out with a small bag, not a new philosophy of doors.
