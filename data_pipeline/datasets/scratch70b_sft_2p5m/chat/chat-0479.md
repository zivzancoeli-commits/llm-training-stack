### User
The play is Thursday. I sewed this kid's tree costume over two weekends. Last night I glued on the bark pieces because the fabric paint looked flat. This morning half of them peeled off onto the kitchen floor like wet cereal. The director already moved the dress rehearsal once because another kid's cape wasn't done. I told them mine would be ready. It is not ready. I don't want to show up with a bald tree and I also don't want to buy a cheap Amazon forest and pretend I made it. Help me salvage this without lying to a ten-year-old.

### Assistant
Stop gluing tonight. Glue that already failed will not improve because you are embarrassed. Peel what is loose. Don't chase clinging bits; those drop on stage under lights.

You have two honest jobs: a costume that stays on a moving child, and a sentence for the director that is not a speech. Unfinished and attached beats textured and shedding.

I will not invent a brand of adhesive. Use what already worked: thread. Large stitches. Ugly from two feet is fine. Ugly that survives a bow is the requirement.

If a piece is too stiff to sew, skip it. Painted patches and fabric scraps tacked on will read as bark from the seats. They are watching a kid remember lines, not your craft table.

Have you already told the director it was done, or only that it would be?

### User
I texted "costume is done, just finishing details" on Sunday. That was before the glue. The kid tried it on tonight and started crying because a chunk came off in their hand. Their other parent is the type who will buy a replacement at 9 p.m. and call it helping. I don't want that fight in the living room. Dress rehearsal is tomorrow after school.

### Assistant
Tomorrow after school is your deadline, not Thursday. Dress rehearsal is where costumes get sweat and panic. Thursday is too late to discover the rest of the bark is a liar.

Text the director tonight, short: "Tree costume is wearable. Some bark fell off. I'll sew what I can before rehearsal. Kid can still go on." Do not write an essay about glue chemistry. Do not apologize four times. One true status is enough. You already oversold "done." Correct it before someone else describes it as a disaster.

With the kid, skip the adult shame. Hold up the costume. Say, "The glue didn't hold. That is my mistake, not yours. We are going to stitch the pieces so they stay. You are still the tree." Then let them pick two pieces to hand you. Kids calm down when they have a job that is not "stop crying."

If the other parent starts toward a store, say, "I don't want a new costume tonight. I want this one stitched." No debate about who cares more. If they buy something anyway, ask the kid once, quietly, not in front of a bag as a referendum.

Sew shoulders and chest first. Those move. Hem can wait. Matching thread is vanity. Holding is the job.

### User
What do I say if the director sighs at me in the hallway like I am the reason the show is cursed?

### Assistant
You say, "I know. Glue failed. We're sewing. Kid is in the costume tonight." Then you stop talking. A sigh is not a court. You do not have to audition as the most sorry parent in the building.

If they ask whether it will last Thursday, you tell the truth you actually have: "It should, because it will be stitched, not glued. If a piece drops, I'll have a needle in my bag." That is a plan. "I promise it will be perfect" is how Sunday's text happened.

Don't volunteer household politics. Directors need a child who can walk onstage without a trail of foam.

Tonight: peel, stitch the moving parts, text the director, one calm sentence to the kid. Tomorrow bring a needle and sit where you can see the wings. If something falls, fix it between scenes. Ordinary play maintenance, not a moral failure.

You already made a costume that exists. Glue was a bad last layer. Finish with thread. Tell the truth once. Let the kid be a tree that stays on.
