### User
My roommate's partner is coming over to help put together a bookshelf. We've never met. I've been here a month. I keep picturing myself either hiding in headphones or giving a tour of my entire childhood. I almost know what "be nice" means. I want kind and specific, not a sitcom.

### Assistant
Kind is the tone. Specific is the content. You don't need a persona. You need a few concrete moves so you're not inventing manners while holding an Allen key.

Start with your name and one true detail, then stop. "Hey, I'm Jordan. I have the room by the fire escape." Let them have a turn. Nervous people fill air with extra jokes. You don't have to.

Ask one question about them as a person, not about the relationship: whether they eat here often, if they have a food thing in the shared fridge, whether they want water before the furniture fight. "Do you want a glass of water before we start?" is kinder than "So how serious is this?" The second one is not your business on shelf night.

I don't know the layout yet. Are you expected to help build, or just exist in the living room while they work?

### User
I offered to help because it felt rude not to. Living room is shared. Their bedroom is theirs. Roommate said "just be yourself," which is not a plan. I don't want to vanish, and I don't want to host a welcome mixer.

### Assistant
"Be yourself" is roommate-speak for "please don't make this my problem." Translate it into actions.

Stay in the shared space for a bounded stretch. Helping with the shelf is a gift if you actually help: hold the side, pass the screws, don't narrate. Twenty to thirty minutes of overlapping presence shows you're not hiding. Then you can say, "I'm going to let you two finish this — trash is Thursday, Wi-Fi is on the router." That's an exit with a logistics fact, which is warmer than "mi casa" and cleaner than a slam.

If they offer a hug and you're not a hug person, a wave plus "I'm more of a handshake person" is kind and specific. Don't fake a hug to prove you're friendly.

Avoid teasing the couple, ranking the apartment, or asking when they're moving in. That conversation can arrive without your probe.

If the partner seems shy, match shy. Quiet is not rude. If they seem chatty, keep answers short and real: where you work in one sentence, whether you mind noise after ten. Pick one. Don't deliver a biography.

You almost had this. The missing inch is the bound: help, one question, one fact, then room.

### User
What if my roommate takes a call and I'm alone with the partner holding a wobbly side panel. That's the scene.

### Assistant
Have a small, true topic that isn't the relationship. The weird light switch. The laundry card. "Does this cam lock go this way?" Tasks rescue you better than speeches. You can also stand up: "I'm grabbing water — want one?" Then you come back. You don't have to perform fascination.

If the silence gets heavy, name it without making it their fault: "I'm a little rusty at first-meet talk. I'm glad you're here. I'm just not a host." That's kind, specific, and it gives them permission to be ordinary too.

If they ask something too personal — rent, your dating life, why you moved — you can decline without frost: "I'm still settling. I'd rather keep that for later." Later can mean never. That's allowed.

When your roommate comes back, you don't recap like a report. A look that says you're fine is enough. You're not being graded.

If the shelf is actually going badly and you're not useful, it's kind to say so: "I think I'm making this worse. I'll get out of the way." Specific. Not a joke about being a disaster.

### User
Name, one detail, water, hold the panel, trash-day fact, then my room. If I'm stuck, laundry card or water, not an interview. No couple-teasing.

### Assistant
Yes. You are a roommate, not a mascot. Do those few things and you will have been decent, which is the actual assignment, even if nobody says good job when the last screw goes in. You almost had the plan. Now it's small enough to use.
