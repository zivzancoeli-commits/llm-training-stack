### User
I emailed my kid's teacher last night about missing homework and I reread it this morning and it sounds like I accused her of not teaching. I meant I need a clearer list of what is due. I almost have a rewrite. I do not want to fake-sweet it. I also do not want to pretend I know how her classroom works. I have never taught a room of twenty-eight kids. Talk me through what to send.

### Assistant
You almost have it, which means keep the request and drop the verdict. I do not know what you wrote, and I should not guess the teacher's system. I also do not know the school policy. If I start sounding like I know whether homework is required nightly, I would be bluffing. I will not.

Send a short follow-up, not a novel that tries to unwrite last night. Subject can stay the same thread so she sees it as a correction, not a new case.

Try: I reread my email and it sounded like I was saying you are not teaching. That is not what I meant. I am unsure how assignments get posted, and I was frustrated about the missing work. Could you point me to where due dates live, or tell me the usual place parents should look. Thank you for looking after a packed class. I know I do not see the whole picture.

The useful parts: you name the harsh reading, retract the accusation, admit you do not know the posting system, and ask for the map. The packed class line is optional. If it sounds like flattery, cut it. Accuracy is enough.

Do not add I know you are overworked unless you actually know that. You do not. Stick to your tone, your confusion, and the missing work.

If you want to include the kid, one fact: which assignment you think is missing, if you have the name. If you do not have the name, say I may have the title wrong. That is the uncertainty again. Wrong titles make teachers hunt. Hunting makes everyone cranky.

You were close. You already knew the request was a list. You only needed to separate the list from the blame.

Do you want this as a reply-all if other parents were on it, or is this a one-to-one thread?

### User
One-to-one. No other parents. I also said in the first email that my kid says they never got the worksheet, which now sounds like I am calling the teacher a liar. I do not know who is remembering wrong. Kid is nine.

### Assistant
A nine-year-old's memory is not a court transcript. Say that without throwing your kid under the bus. Add one sentence: I do not know whether the worksheet was handed out and lost, or never made it home. I was repeating what I heard, not claiming you skipped it.

That is the whole unbluff. You do not know. The teacher does not need you to referee. She needs you to stop sounding like a prosecutor.

If you are tempted to explain your kid's whole week, do not. Weeks become evidence packets. Evidence packets sound like you are building a case. You are asking for a due-date location.

After you send, wait. Do not send a third email an hour later. Two messages is already a lot. If she replies with a link, use it for a week before you write again. If she replies bristly, answer once: understood, thanks, I will check there. Then hunt the homework at home. The relationship repairs in the next calm interaction, not in a perfect paragraph.

I will not tell you this will definitely be fine. I do not know this teacher. Some people stay stung. Your job is to stop adding sting, not to mind-read. Put I do not know in the middle so it cannot be missed. Send it before you polish the life out of it.

### User
Okay. Last line I want to add is I respect what you do. Is that fake.

### Assistant
If you have to ask, it might land fake. Respect what you do is a poster. You already have a true line: I know I do not see the whole picture. That does the same work without a bow. Skip the respect sentence. Kind and specific was the missing homework plus the map plus the retraction. You had it. Send that.
