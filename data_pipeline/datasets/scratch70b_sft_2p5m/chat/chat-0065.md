### User
My grandma texted me a screenshot of a tweet that just said "the curtains were just blue" and then a picture of a sad cartoon dog in a hat on fire saying "this is fine." She asked, in all caps, WHETHER THIS IS ABOUT HER LIVING ROOM. She painted her living room blue last month. I started typing "it's a meme about media literacy and also the this-is-fine dog is a separate template" and I sounded like a Wikipedia page. I am the grandchild who is supposed to be good at the internet. How do I explain this without a glossary.

### Assistant
Drop the words "meme," "template," and "media literacy" unless she asks for the names later. She asked a real question: is this about her living room. Answer that first, in ordinary speech.

"No. This is not about your paint. Someone on the internet grabbed two old jokes and mashed them together. Neither joke is about your house."

Then explain the two jokes as if they were short stories, because that is what they are.

First joke, the curtains: some readers like to hunt for hidden meaning in books and movies. A writer says the curtains were blue, and a certain kind of class discussion treats that as a symbol for sadness or death. Other people push back and say: maybe the curtains were blue because they were blue. The sentence "the curtains were just blue" is that pushback. It means, "do not over-read a small detail." It is a little rude toward teachers and a little tired of symbolism. It is not a review of her paint job.

Second joke, the dog: it is a cartoon of a dog sitting in a room that is on fire, smiling, saying that everything is fine. People send that picture when a situation is obviously bad and someone is pretending it is not, including themselves. The smile is the point. The dog is not brave. The dog is in denial.

When those two pictures show up together, the sender is usually saying something like: we are overthinking a small thing while a bigger thing is on fire, or we are insisting a detail is deep while ignoring the obvious disaster. It is sloppy humor. It is not a coded message to her.

If she still looks at the blue walls and frowns, you can add one practical line: "If a stranger on the internet posts a picture, it is almost never aimed at one person's living room. They do not know you."

### User
She is going to ask why people talk in pictures instead of sentences. Also I used "it's giving" in a different text last week and she asked what it is giving. I still have not answered that one. Do I explain both in the same call or will I fry her.

### Assistant
Do not stack them in one call. Pick the screenshot in front of her. "It's giving" can wait until a slower afternoon. Two internet dialects in ten minutes is how you get a smart person to decide the whole medium is nonsense.

If she asks why pictures: people reuse a picture the way they reuse a proverb. A proverb is a short saying everyone already knows, so you can point at a situation with it. The dog on fire is a proverb with a drawing. You do not have to invent a new paragraph every time something is going badly and someone is smiling. You hold up the known picture. That is the whole mechanism. It is not more sophisticated than "the early bird gets the worm." It is just faster to tap and send.

Stay in that register. No "viral," no "template," no "copypasta." If a word would need a definition, swap it for a sentence she already has.

When you do get to "it's giving," later: it means "this reminds me of" or "this has the vibe of." "It's giving rainy Sunday" means the thing feels like a rainy Sunday. People dropped "vibe of" and left "giving." It is fashion-talk that escaped into normal texts. It is not a gift. Nothing is being handed over.

For tonight's call, close the loop on the living room so she can stop worrying. "Your blue walls are blue. That is allowed. The picture is two old jokes glued together. You did not miss a secret." Then stop. Intern energy wants to be thorough. Thorough is how this becomes a seminar. She asked whether it was about her room. You answered. That is competence, not a lecture.
