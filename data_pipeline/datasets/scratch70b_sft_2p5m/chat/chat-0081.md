### User
I'm taking my sister's kid to a street festival this weekend. He's seven. She gave me this whole speech about "lost child protocol" and I nodded like I understood, but I think I only half got it. I know the obvious part — don't lose him — and I know I shouldn't just scream his name into a crowd if something goes wrong. I want the actual sequence, kind, specific, not a horror story and not a shrug.

### Assistant
You almost have it. The protocol is not a magic spell. It is a few decisions you make before you are scared, so that if you get separated you do not invent a plan while your heart is doing something unhelpful.

Start before you leave the house. Take a photo of him that morning in the clothes he is wearing. Same shirt, same hat, same shoes. Put your phone number in his pocket on a card, and put his grown-up's number too, not only yours. Seven is old enough to keep a card in a zipper pocket if you make it a job, not a lecture.

Agree on a meeting spot the minute you arrive, while everyone is still calm. Pick something that does not move: a specific food stall with a sign, a statue, the info tent, the ticket arch. "Near the music" is not a spot. Walk him to the spot. Have him point at it. Say, "If we get split, you go here and stay. You do not go looking for me in the crowd." A lost child hunting for an adult is how two people keep missing each other.

Tell him what a helper looks like in this place. I will not invent a law about who is allowed to help. The practical version is often: a person in a staff shirt, a person at an information booth, a police officer if one is visible. The instruction for him can be: "Find a worker in a matching shirt or stand at the info tent. You can say, I am lost and my aunt's name is ___." Give him your actual name, the one on your ID.

Your job in the first minute of a split is smaller than the movies. Stop. Look around at kid-height. He may be four meters away behind a stroller. Call his name at a normal volume once or twice. If you do not see him, you go to the meeting spot. You do not sprint in a random direction.

If he is not at the meeting spot, you go to staff. Kind and specific: his name, his age, the photo, the color of the shirt, where you last stood, the time. Ask them where lost children are taken. Many events have a quiet tent for that. I do not know this festival's layout, so I will not fake a map.

While staff are looking, you stay reachable. Phone unsilenced. You do not wander into a second neighborhood "just in case." You also do not post his full name, school, and your address on a public page in the first five minutes. A photo and a description to staff is the job.

If you find him, the reunion is not a scolding. Crouch. You can say you were scared. You can also say he did the right thing if he went to the spot. Shame makes the next festival harder.

Prevention that is specific: he holds a strap on your bag in the densest blocks. Bathroom trips together. You do not send him to a booth "just over there" in a river of people.

You already knew "don't lose him." The protocol is: photo, card, immovable meeting point, staff with facts, you stay put enough to be found. Kind because he is seven. Specific because vague plans melt in noise.

### User
What if he starts crying and won't tell a stranger he's lost?

### Assistant
Then the meeting spot matters even more, because he can go there without making a speech. You can also practice one sentence at home until it is boring: "I am lost. Please take me to the info tent." Boring is good. If he freezes anyway, a staff person with your photo still has something to work with. You did not fail because a seven-year-old cried. You built a path that does not depend on him being brave on cue.
