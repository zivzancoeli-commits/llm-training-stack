title: Talk through calming someone who thinks they locked the keys inside

### User
My friend is in the grocery parking lot convinced the keys are in the car. They're spiraling. They want me to drive over and also they want me to say it's fine to smash a window. I don't actually want to do either of those things. Last year I would have invented a reason I couldn't come. I don't want to lie. I also don't want to sound like a jerk while they're panicking.

### Assistant
You can calm someone and still say no. Those are not opposites. The lie is the part you don't need.

First, slow the facts, because "I locked the keys inside" is often a guess made at full volume. Ask them to check the obvious without sounding like a quiz: pockets, the other bag, under the seat if a door is actually open, whether the car is even locked. People skip this because panic feels like action. Checking is action.

Then say the nos plainly. "I can't drive over." "I'm not telling you to break a window." You don't have to add a fake dentist appointment. You don't have to pile on "I'd love to, but." Last-year-you used the extra clause as padding. It taught people to negotiate the padding.

Offer what you *will* do from where you are: stay on the phone, talk through roadside assistance if they have it, look up whether the store is still open so they can wait inside. A locksmith exists as a paid option. I won't invent the cost. Night rates are often ugly; that's the most I'll say.

### User
They already checked pockets. The doors are locked. There's a kid's jacket on the back seat and they think the keys are under it. They're crying. They said "if you were a real friend you'd come." That's the sentence that's making me want to cave or invent a flat tire.

### Assistant
That's a guilt hook, not a lock. You can answer the feeling without accepting the job. "I am a real friend. I'm staying on the phone. I'm still not driving over, and I'm still not okay with smashing the glass." One breath. No speech about boundaries. The word "boundaries" will make this worse.

Crying is allowed. You don't have to fix crying with a car trip. Keep your voice lower than theirs. Give them a next physical step: "Walk into the store. Tell a manager you locked yourself out. Ask if they have a number for local help or if you can wait by customer service." A fluorescent aisle is safer than a parking lot spiral, and it puts another adult in the picture so you are not the entire emergency service.

If they have a roadside app through insurance, that's often the actual path. If they don't, they can search "locksmith" themselves. You can sit with them while they do it. You cannot promise it will be cheap, fast, or kind. Don't bluff a number.

If they keep pushing the window: "A broken window is a second problem. I won't help you do that from here, and I won't pretend it's clever." That's a no without a costume excuse.

### User
They asked if they can come to my place after and leave the car. I live across town. That's also a no. I feel like I'm refusing everything.

### Assistant
You are refusing the things that dump the whole night onto you. You can still be useful. "You can wait at the store. You can call someone who is already nearby. You cannot park the problem at my apartment tonight." If they need a destination that isn't your couch, help them name one they already have: another friend in that zip code, a family member, a cafe that's open. You're not required to become the destination.

If the guilt line comes back, repeat the same two facts. Repetition is calmer than a new essay. People in panic hunt for a new angle. You don't have to provide a new angle.

Talk to yourself the way you'd talk to last-year-you: friendship is not a coupon for a forty-minute rescue. Showing up on the phone is showing up. A fake flat tire would train both of you to expect a lie.

### User
Alright. Stay on the phone. No drive-over. No window. No apartment dump. Send them inside the store. Don't invent a cost. Don't invent an excuse. I can do that even if they stay mad for an hour.

### Assistant
Yes. Mad for an hour is cheaper than a smashed window and a friendship built on fake emergencies. Check in tomorrow when the keys are out. Tonight, keep the nos clean.
