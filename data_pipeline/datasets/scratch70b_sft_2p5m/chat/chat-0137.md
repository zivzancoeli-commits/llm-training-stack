### User
My roommate had a minor outpatient thing yesterday. There's a sheet on the fridge with a list of "contact us immediately if" — fever, spreading redness, numbness, "unusual discharge." I'm the intern brain in this apartment. I keep rereading it like I'm supposed to certify him. He says he feels "off." I don't want to bluff that I know what off means.

### Assistant
You are not supposed to certify him. I can't see the wound, I don't know the procedure, and I will not guess a diagnosis from fridge paper. Those lists are nets. They catch rare problems. They are not a schedule of what will happen.

A useful first line is: "I read the sheet. I don't actually know what half of it means. You said you feel off. Do you want to call the number, or do you want me to leave it?" That admits you don't know and gives him an exit.

One thing I shouldn't invent: is "off" a new feverish feeling, pain that's worse than yesterday, or just tired and annoyed at being home?

### User
He says tired, a bit sore at the site, no fever that we measured. The sheet says a low-grade temperature can be normal and also says fever is a reason to call. That's the part that makes me feel like I'm failing a test. I don't want to sound calm by making up an answer.

### Assistant
You're not failing a test. The sheet is badly designed for humans. "Can be normal" and "call if fever" can sit on the same page because clinics would rather print a wide net. That does not tell you his temperature, and I will not invent a cutoff number as if I were his clinician.

Talk in observations, not conclusions. "You're tired and sore. We don't have a fever reading that worried you. I don't know if that's expected day one. I'm not going to guess. We can call and ask." That is not a diagnosis. That is refusing to sit on a question you cannot answer.

If he wants you to "just tell him it's fine," you still don't. "I don't know if it's fine. Fine isn't a thing I can see from a leaflet." Intern energy is: we ask the person whose name is on the after-hours line.

I would not search the procedure name in front of him tonight. Results love the worst paragraph. If you look anything up later, treat it as vocabulary, not a verdict.

### User
What if he asks what I think the soreness *means*? I can feel myself about to say "that's normal" because it sounds like a grown-up.

### Assistant
Then say you don't know. Out loud. "I don't know what it means. I'm not going to guess. We should ask someone whose job is this." That is not a failure. "That's normal" is the bluff. It sounds calm for ten minutes and then you're stuck defending a sentence you invented.

Offer to sit next to him while he calls, or write the question so he doesn't have to perform confidence: procedure yesterday, tired, sore at the site, no fever that you measured — is that expected, and what should make us call back? Stick to what you observed. Don't upgrade "off" into a syndrome. Don't downgrade spreading redness if you actually see spreading redness — that one is on the sheet as a reason to contact them, and you still don't name a disease. You report the skin and you call.

If the clinic says they can only speak to him, you hand him the phone with the question written. You still didn't bluff.

If anything on that urgent list is actually present — spreading redness you can see, numbness that's new, discharge that wasn't described as expected, a fever he's actually measuring — you don't debate the leaflet. You get a human. Until then, unsure plus a phone call beats a made-up reassurance.

### User
So the talk is: I saw the sheet, I don't understand it, you're tired and sore, I won't call that normal, do you want to call, I'll sit here. No Google theater. No fake calm.

### Assistant
Simple is the point. Your job is not to match the leaflet's density. Your job is to not lie with confidence. If he says he's fine and you still have a pit in your stomach, you can still say you'd feel better hearing it from the number on the page. Then you stop arguing. Unsure is allowed. Pretending you passed intern rounds in the kitchen is not.
