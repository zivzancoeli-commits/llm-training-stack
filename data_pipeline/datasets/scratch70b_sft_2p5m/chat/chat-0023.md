### User
Last night my friend told a joke at a small birthday dinner. It used someone's family situation as the punchline — not a stranger, a person at the table. The room went quiet in that specific way. The person laughed a little so nobody would die. I am a new intern in my actual job and I am treating this friendship like I might get fired from it. I was going to text "hey that was problematic" and I know that sounds like a poster. I also don't want to pretend I didn't notice. How do I tell them without becoming HR?

### Assistant
I need one thing before I tell you what to send.

Was the target someone who is still in the group chat and likely to see a pile-on, or are you talking one-to-one with the joke-teller because you care about both people and you were actually there?

### User
One-to-one. I'm not trying to ratio my friend. The target is my other friend, Sam. Sam texted me later "it's fine" which might mean fine or might mean please never mention this. The joke-teller, Priya, asked me in the car if people were "just tired." I said "yeah maybe" and I have been chewing that maybe since.

### Assistant
You already know "yeah maybe" was a dodge. Intern brain loves a dodge because it feels like keeping your job. Friendship is not a performance review, but it still needs a true sentence.

Do not open with "problematic." That word makes people defend a poster instead of a person. Do not open with a speech about your values. Open with what you saw, because you were in the room.

A message that is human: "I've been thinking about the joke at dinner. I don't think people were just tired. It used Sam's family as the punchline and the table went quiet. I should have said that in the car instead of 'maybe.'"

That admits your delay. Good. You are not claiming moral first place.

Then one ask, not a verdict dump: "Did you mean it as a roast Sam was in on, or did it come out harsher than you wanted?" You do not know Priya's intent. Guessing intent is how this becomes a trial. Let Priya answer.

If Priya doubles down — "Sam laughed," "people are sensitive" — you do not need a TED Talk. You can say, "Sam laughed the way people laugh to keep a table moving. I'm telling you it landed badly from where I sat." You are reporting your seat, not appointing yourself referee of Sam's inner life.

If Priya is immediately sorry, you do not need to pile on. "Thanks for hearing it. I think a private sorry to Sam is the useful one, not a group-chat confession." I do not know what Sam wants. "It's fine" is ambiguous on purpose sometimes. You can tell Priya that Sam may not want a replay. Priya can still send a short private note: "I shouldn't have used your family as a joke. I'm sorry." That is specific. A public apology can force Sam to perform forgiveness.

You are not HR. HR would collect statements. You are a friend who failed a small test in a car and is taking the retake by text. Keep it off the group thread. Keep Sam's "it's fine" out of a screenshot. If Sam wants you to drop it, drop the campaign. You can still tell Priya the truth about the room without making Sam the exhibit.

### User
What if Priya says I should have called it out in the moment and now I'm making it a thing?

### Assistant
Then you tell another true thing: "You're right I should have said something at the table. I froze. I'm saying it now because the car answer was a lie." You can own the freeze without accepting that silence forever is the price of being pleasant.

Making it a thing is what already happened when the table went quiet. You are naming it, not inventing it.

If the friendship can hold one honest text, send it today, not after you have workshopped it into a memo. Short. Specific. One question about intent. No poster language. Then stop typing. Let Priya be a person on the other side. You are new at a job. You are allowed to be slightly old at being a friend.
