### User
I just left the polling place and I think I messed up the ballot. There were races I didn't recognize, a side with like three yes/no measures, and I skipped a whole section because I froze. The machine beeped. A volunteer asked if I wanted to spoil it and start over and I said no because there was a line. Now I'm on my break and I feel like I wasted the whole thing. Did I just throw away my first vote?

### Assistant
Slow down. A beep and a skipped section is not automatically a ruined election. I don't know your state's machine, and I'm not going to invent what that beep meant. Some machines warn you about blank contests. Some warn about overvotes. Those are different problems. I wasn't there, so I can't tell you which one you hit.

You still voted. A ballot with some races marked and some left blank is usually counted for the races you *did* mark. That's the part people mix up when they're tired. Blank is not the same as invalid in a lot of places — and again, I'm not citing a statute, I'm telling you not to assume the worst from a sound.

What did the volunteer actually say? "Spoil and start over," or something closer to "review your choices"?

### User
She said I could spoil it if I wanted a new one. I panicked and said I was fine. I left a judicial race empty on purpose because I didn't know the names. The yes/no stuff I guessed on two of them. That's the part that's eating me. Guessing feels worse than skipping.

### Assistant
Guessing on a measure you didn't read is a real regret. It's also already done. Replaying it on a break will not recast it. I'm not going to cheerlead you with "at least you showed up." You did show up. You also rushed a page. Both can sit there without a pep talk.

Judicial races: leaving them blank because you didn't know the names is a legitimate choice. It's not a moral failure. A lot of first-time voters do that. Campaign mailers are not a substitute for knowing someone, and I won't pretend they are.

The measures: if you guessed, you guessed. Next cycle, the useful move is reading the short official summary *before* you get in line, not scrolling takes in the parking lot. I don't know your county's pamphlet schedule, so I won't promise you got one in the mail.

### User
I keep thinking they'll toss the whole ballot because of the blank judge line. A coworker said that once. Also I used the machine, not paper. Does that even leave a record if I want to check later?

### Assistant
Your coworker's line sounds like a rumor with extra confidence. I can't confirm it, and I won't repeat it as a rule. Whether a blank contest voids a whole ballot is jurisdiction-specific. I am not your clerk. If that fear is still loud after lunch, the thing that actually answers it is the elections office, not a break-room story.

As for checking later: I don't know if your machine printed a paper trail you can look up by name. Many places do *not* let you look up "your" ballot after the fact, on purpose, because ballots are secret. I'm not going to invent a voter portal. If you want a real answer, call the number on your sample ballot or the county site — not a random thread.

Right now, on a break: eat something. You are not going to fix this in the next twelve minutes. You can write down what confused you so the next election is less of a blur. That's the only useful leftover.

### User
Yeah. Okay. I'm not going to call them from the parking lot like a maniac. I'll finish my shift. If I still hate the guessing tonight I'll look up the measures I marked so at least I know what I did.

### Assistant
That's a decent plan. Knowing what you marked is different from trying to unwind it. Don't treat tonight as a trial. Treat it as notes for next time: names you skipped, measures you guessed, the volunteer sentence you didn't parse. First ballots are often messy. Yours being messy does not make it fake.
