### User
We had seven people at my table. Mid-roast, my cousin started posting photos and a play-by-play: who brought the salad, a joke my dad made, a picture of my kitchen mess. I saw it because someone else liked it. It was not a public figure dinner. It was lasagna. I asked them in the hallway to stop. They said "it's just my account" and went back to typing. People are still here. I don't want a scene over dessert. I also don't want my apartment on a timeline.

### Assistant
Your table is not content by default. "Just my account" still leaves the room.

For the rest of tonight, keep it small and in the room you can control. You already asked once. Ask once more, to the phone: "Please no more photos or play-by-play of this dinner. I'm not okay with it." If they keep going, you can say, "If you need to post, step outside, but not our faces or the apartment." Then you stop bargaining during coffee.

You can also tell the other guests, quietly, that you didn't okay photos. That lets them choose their own faces. You are not required to make a speech at the table unless you want the group to hear the rule.

I will not invent platform takedown rules. You can ask them to delete the posts. They may refuse. Tonight is about stopping the next one.

Are the posts identifiable — address, last names, kids — or mostly food?

### User
Food, my messy counter, dad's joke which includes a nickname he only uses here, and the street out the window. No kids. The cousin has a couple thousand followers. Not famous. Enough that I don't know those people. Dessert is in ten minutes.

### Assistant
Ten minutes is enough for a second ask and a seating choice. Sit them away from the best angle if that helps. More useful: the sentence, then you serve dessert yourself so you are moving, not hovering over their screen.

After people leave, text: "Please take down the dinner posts from tonight, especially the street and dad's nickname. I asked in person. This was a private meal." Short. If they argue about their account, you do not need a seminar on publicity. Repeat the take-down ask once.

If they refuse, you can still tell your dad what happened so he is not surprised later. You can mute or unfollow. I will not promise a legal hammer. A cousin with a few thousand followers is still a cousin you can uninvite next time.

For the guests still in the apartment, a light line works if someone picks up a phone: "I'm keeping this one offline." That protects the rest of the night without a trial.

### User
If I uninvite them next time, the rest of the family will say I made it political. It was lasagna. I feel dramatic.

### Assistant
Asking for a private meal is not political. It is a house rule. Dramatic would be smashing the phone. You asked in a hallway.

Next invitation can wait. You do not have to announce a ban tonight. You can finish dessert, get people out, send the take-down text, and sleep. In a week, if they still treat your sink as a set, you host without them, or you host with a no-phones ask at the start. Starting the night with "please keep phones down" is easier than chasing a live blog.

Serve the dessert. Say the line if the typing continues. Do not perform a closing argument for the group. Lasagna was enough work. Your counter does not need a caption.

### User
They're typing again during the cake. I can see the glow. I asked twice. The street is still in the last post.

### Assistant
The cake does not require a third speech to the whole table. Go to their chair. Quiet: "Phone down for the rest of this, please. No more of the street." If they keep typing, you can say, "I'm done posing for that." Then you sit with the people who are actually at the table. After the door closes, the take-down text. You already asked in the hallway. Repeating at cake is not dramatic. It is the last in-person chance before the night becomes a recap they do not own. Speak to the glow. Then cut the cake for the room, not for the followers.
