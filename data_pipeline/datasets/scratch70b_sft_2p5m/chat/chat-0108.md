### User
I have a screening call tomorrow after almost two years out of paid work. I took care of family, then I froze, then the gap got longer. I'm not looking for a hype speech. Last year I would have told myself to "just be confident" and then I would have rammed a joke into the first minute. I don't want to do that. I also don't want to sound like I'm apologizing for existing. How do I talk about the gap without turning the call into a therapy session or a pep rally?

### Assistant
You treat the gap as a calendar fact, then you turn the wheel back to the job. One or two sentences. Not a saga.

Something like: "I was out of the workforce for family care from [year] to [year]. I'm ready to take on [this kind of work] again, and here's the recent project or volunteer or coursework that kept the skill from going dusty." If nothing kept it from going dusty, you don't invent a fellowship. You say you used the time for care, and you've been catching up on [tool, domain] this month. That's less shiny. It's also not a collapse.

Don't open with a joke about being rusty. Interviewers fill in worse stories than the true one when you try to outrun it.

I'm not going to tell you they'll love the authenticity. Some will be fine. Some will be biased. Both exist. The call still has a job to do: can you do *this* role.

What's the role, roughly — the kind of work, not the company slogan?

### User
Office coordinator type thing. Calendars, vendors, a front desk some of the day. I did similar work before the gap. During the gap I ran a household like a tiny ops team, which sounds corny if I say it that way, so I wasn't going to. I updated a spreadsheet last week for a community fridge schedule. That's the embarrassing true recent example.

### Assistant
The community fridge spreadsheet is not embarrassing. It's a real coordination artifact. You can mention it in one clause: "Recently I kept a shared schedule for a community fridge, which is the same muscle as vendor windows and coverage." Then stop. Don't build a TED talk on kale.

Household as ops: you can use it if you keep it concrete — medical appointments, insurance calls, a budget. If it starts sounding like a brand, cut it. Coordinators live in specifics.

On the call, if they ask "so what have you been doing," you answer, then you ask a work question: "How do you handle coverage when two people are out?" That move stops the gap from eating the remaining minutes. It also shows you still think in coverage, which is the job.

If your voice shakes, let it shake for a second and keep going. I'm not going to tell you to smile more. I'm also not going to tell you this is easy. Screening calls are awkward. A gap makes them more awkward. Awkward is survivable.

Don't volunteer the freeze. If they ask why the gap ran long, "caregiving lasted longer than I expected, and then I needed time to get back" is enough. You don't owe them the interior weather.

### User
Okay. Two sentences on the gap, fridge schedule as one clause, then a coverage question. No rusty joke. If they go quiet I'll sit in the quiet instead of filling it with sorry. I can feel last year's version of me wanting to add "I'm really excited for this opportunity" three times.

### Assistant
Once is plenty if you mean it. Three times is a tell that you're soothing yourself. Skip the extra excitement if it isn't in your mouth. A calm "thanks for the time, I'll follow up if you send next steps" is enough glue at the end.

Sit in the quiet. Sorry is a leak. After the call, write down what they asked about the gap so you're not rewriting history in your head at 1 a.m. Tomorrow you only have to get through a conversation, not a redemption arc. If it goes badly, it goes badly. We'll look at the next listing. Not a cheer. Just the next concrete step.
