### User
My nephew failed the driving test this morning. Parallel park, apparently. He is in my kitchen slamming the fridge and saying he is never going back. His mom wants me to "cheer him up." I am not going to tell him he is a champion. I also do not want him to take it out on the examiner or on a car later. He is seventeen and he looks like he might cry if I look at him too long. Talk to me like you do not buy pep talks.

### Assistant
Do not cheer. Cheer is a second test.

Sit. Let the fridge survive. First line is about the event, not his soul: "That's a rotten morning." If he dumps the parallel park story, listen to the end. Do not skip to "you'll get it next time" while he is still in the first hour.

He said he is never going back. Treat that as heat, not a legal filing. You can say, "You don't have to book anything today." That lowers the room. Booking is a different day. Today is a failed test and a loud fridge.

I will not recite this state's retest rules as if I have the pamphlet. If he asks when he can try again, the honest move is: "I don't know the wait. We can look it up later if you want." Guessing a date is how you start an argument with the internet.

### User
His mom already said "plenty of people fail" in a bright voice and he told her to stop talking. I am the next shift. He asked me if the tester had it in for him because of the last name. That is a reach. I do not want to mock him.

### Assistant
Mom's line is true and useless in minute ten. You can skip the statistic.

On the last-name theory: do not mock, do not join the conspiracy. "I wasn't in the car, so I don't know how the tester was. Parallel park fails a lot of people who drive fine on the street." That is as far as I would go. If he pushes "they were out to get me," you can stay dull: "Maybe they were having a day. The result is still a no, and you still get another try when you want one." Dull is de-escalation. A speech about fairness lights him back up.

If he is close to crying, look at the table, not at his face, and keep your voice ordinary. "You can be pissed. You don't have to perform okay for me." Then a practical fork: food, a walk, or he can sit in the car in the driveway with the radio and not drive. He does not need a lesson in that kitchen.

What I would not do: joke about "women drivers," joke about the examiner's age, or offer to "have a word." That is how a bad morning becomes a feud.

### User
Give me the anti-cheer version if he says he is a loser. And the version if he wants to drive home angry.

### Assistant
If he says he is a loser: "You failed a test. That's not the same thing." Stop there. Do not stack a list of his trophies. Lists sound like you are arguing with a mood. If he repeats it, you can say, "I'm not going to agree with that," and change the subject to lunch.

If he wants to drive home angry: no. Not a fake excuse — a real one. "You're heated. I'm not handing you keys right now." If he is not supposed to drive unsupervised anyway, you already have the law on the side of sitting still. I will not pretend I know his permit terms. You still do not put an angry new driver in traffic to prove a point.

If mom asks later whether you cheered him up, you can say, "I didn't. I sat with him and I didn't let him drive mad." That is comfort with a spine. He can retest when the parallel park is less of a ghost. Today you keep the fridge, the keys, and the pep talk out of the way.
