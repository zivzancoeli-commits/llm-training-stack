### User
Performance reviews are due Friday. A coworker on my team pinged me: "You're a better writer, can you just draft my self-review and I'll tweak it?" They sent a bullet list that is mostly "supported the team" with no examples. I already have my own review to write. I don't want to be the team's ghostwriter and I don't want them walking into a meeting with my sentences in their mouth. They made it sound like a small favor. It does not feel small.

### Assistant
It isn't small. A self-review is their account of their year. If you write it, you are volunteering to be their memory and their voice. That is a real job, and it is not your job.

You can decline without a speech about integrity. "I can't write your self-review. I can look at a draft you write for ten minutes if you want a second pair of eyes." That offers a smaller, honest favor — editing — and refuses authorship. If you don't even want to edit, skip the second sentence. "I can't. I have my own to finish." is enough.

Do not take their bullet list home "just to get words on the page." Words on the page become the document. Tweaking is a myth people tell so you'll do the thinking.

I will not invent what your company requires, what a manager wants to hear, or whether ghostwriting is against a policy. I don't know your handbook. I know you don't want your sentences in their mouth. That's sufficient reason.

Have they already asked anyone else, or are you the only writer on the floor they think of as free?

### User
I think I'm the only one they asked. They're panicking because they took Friday off last month and feel behind. They said if I draft it they can "focus on actual work." That annoyed me. Also our manager sometimes compares reviews side by side. I don't want mine and theirs to sound like cousins.

### Assistant
"Focus on actual work" is how people describe the work they like better. A self-review is actual work. You are allowed to be annoyed. You still answer with a boundary, not a TED talk about labor.

Reply in the same ping thread so there is a record of the no, not because you are building a case, but because panic-asks come back in person at 4 p.m. "I can't draft it. If you write a messy page today, I can comment on whether the examples are specific. I won't write the examples for you." If even comments feel like a trap, don't offer them. Panic plus a helper often expands.

If they show up at your desk anyway, you can stand up or keep typing and repeat the line. Sitting there while they narrate their year is how drafts get born out of your mouth.

On the cousin-voice problem: even a light edit can leak your phrasing. If you do comment, comment on missing facts ("where was this project, who was the partner") not on how to sound like a competent person. Sounding like a competent person is their problem. Specifics are theirs to remember.

I won't tell you what to put in your own review. Write yours first, tonight if you can, so their Friday emergency cannot eat your hours. People who are behind will use whoever still has white space on a calendar.

### User
They just reacted with a sad emoji. I feel like I kicked a puppy.

### Assistant
A sad emoji is cheaper than writing five paragraphs. It is allowed to exist. You did not kick a puppy. You declined to do their homework.

You do not have to send a softening paragraph. Softening is how the ask returns as "just the summary section." If you want one kind line, send it once: "You'll be fine if you list three things you actually shipped. I can't write it." Then mute the thread until tomorrow.

If they miss the deadline, that is between them and the manager. You are not the backup narrator of their year.

Finish your own review. Keep your sentences in your document. Let theirs be theirs, even if theirs is messy. Messy and true beats polished and borrowed, and it also keeps you out of a comparison meeting where two people sound like one ghost.
