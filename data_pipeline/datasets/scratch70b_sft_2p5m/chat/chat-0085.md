### User
I printed the agenda for Tuesday's meeting about the bike lane on Maple. Half of it looks like a different language. There's "ROW reallocation," "protected facility," "LOS drop," "complete streets," and a slide titled "modal split targets." I want to actually understand what people are arguing about instead of nodding along. Can you walk me through this like I'm smart but I don't work for the city?

### Assistant
Yeah. Most of that is three ordinary fights wearing fancy hats: who gets the street space, how scary the ride would be, and whether cars will move slower.

ROW is "right of way": the strip the city already owns between the property lines. Sidewalk, curb, parking, travel lanes, median. "ROW reallocation" means they are not buying extra land. They are slicing that sandwich differently. Take a parking lane or shrink two travel lanes: that is the whole trick.

A "protected facility" is a bike lane with something between you and moving cars: a curb, posts, parked cars used as a wall. "Painted" usually means a white line and a hope. People argue because a line does not stop a door or a turning van. Protection costs more and eats width.

LOS is "level of service." Engineers grade car delay like a report card, often A through F. "LOS drop" means the car grade got worse, not that the street failed as a place to live. If someone waves it like a fire alarm, ask: worse for whom, at what hour, compared to what.

"Complete streets" means the street should work for walking, biking, buses, and driving, not only fastest car throughput. "Modal split" is the pie chart of how people move. "Targets" means they want that pie to look different later. It is a wish plus a measuring stick, not a spell.

If you want one question that cuts the jargon, ask: "Which slice of the existing street are you taking, and what sits in that slice today?" That forces a picture instead of a slogan.

### User
Okay, so if they take the parking on the north side, that's the reallocation. The flyer also says "peak-hour LOS falls from C to D on the Maple/3rd approach" and someone in the neighborhood group is calling that gridlock. Is D actually gridlock, or is that the scare word?

### Assistant
Scare word, usually. I am not looking at their model, so I will not pretend I know Tuesday's numbers. In a lot of city reports, C is "busy but moving" and D is "you wait more at the worst minute." F is the one that starts to sound like a parking lot. People still call D "gridlock" because it sounds like collapse.

Ask them to translate the grade into minutes. "How many extra seconds at 5:15, and is that the worst fifteen minutes or the whole afternoon?" If they cannot say it in seconds, they are using the letter as a mood.

Same move with "capacity." That is how many vehicles the lane can theoretically swallow in an hour under their assumptions, not a moral fact. "Over capacity" means the model thinks more cars want through than the lane can process at the speed they assumed. True at 5:20, maybe false at 2:00.

A "conflict point" is a spot where two paths cross and someone can get hit: a right-turning car across a bike lane, a driveway. Protected designs try to slow those or make them more visible. Painted lanes often leave the conflict sitting there with a bike symbol on it.

### User
There's also "quick-build" versus "capital project" on the last page. And "tactical" in parentheses. I think they want to try posts and paint first. Does that mean it's fake, or is that a real lane?

### Assistant
Real enough to change the street. Not fake. "Quick-build" or "tactical" usually means cheap, fast, and reversible: plastic posts, paint, planters, maybe a rubber curb. "Capital" means construction money, concrete, drainage, years of design. Cities use quick-build when they want to test the idea before pouring a curb, or when the big money is years away.

The honest risk is maintenance. Posts get hit. Paint fades. If nobody replaces them, the "protection" becomes a rumor. Ask who inspects this after month three, and what happens when a post is gone.

If you speak at the meeting, skip the acronyms. Say: I get that you are moving space from parking or a car lane into a bike lane with a physical buffer. I want the extra wait said in minutes, not letters. I want to know which conflict points you are actually closing. And I want to know whether this is posts-and-paint with a maintenance plan, or a drawing of a future curb. That is the whole meeting, in English.
