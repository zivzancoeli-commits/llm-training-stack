### User
My friend told a joke at lunch and the table just... died. It wasn't a slur or anything, but it was mean about someone's body in that "I'm kidding" way. I laughed a little because I panicked. Now I keep thinking I should say something to them later, but I don't want to be the hall monitor. They're my actual friend, not a random. How do I bring it up without making it a TED talk?

### Assistant
Before I give you a script: are you trying to tell them because the target was sitting there, or because the target wasn't there and you still hated it? Those are different talks. One is "you hurt someone in the room." The other is "that joke is not who I think you are, and I don't want to laugh at it."

Also, did they notice the table die, or are they still riding the joke like it worked?

### User
The person it was about was at the table. They smiled that fake smile and changed the subject. My friend did a little "anyway" and moved on. I don't think they think it was a disaster. They've done softer versions of this before. I don't want a fight. I just don't want to keep being the audience.

### Assistant
Then you're not the hall monitor. You're the friend who doesn't want a repeat. That's allowed.

Don't open with a lecture about comedy. Don't open with "you always." Open with the one lunch, the one line, and how it landed. Private, not in the group chat. Walking somewhere is better than a text, because texts turn into exhibits.

You can say: "Hey, at lunch, the joke about [their] body sat wrong. The table went quiet. I even did a panic laugh, which I regret. I don't think it landed as a joke." Then you stop. Let them answer. If they get defensive, you don't have to win the theory of humor. You can repeat the fact: it was about a person's body, that person was there, it went quiet.

If they say "they know I didn't mean it," you can say, "I believe you didn't mean harm. It still landed mean." Meaning and landing are different. Sharp fourteen-year-olds get that faster than adults who hide behind "it's just jokes."

If they ask what they should do, that's a separate step. Don't assign an apology speech unless they want one. You can say the target might not want a big scene, and a short private "hey, that was a dumb joke, sorry" is usually cleaner than a public encore. I don't know this friend group. I'm not going to script a grovel.

Your job in the first talk is the mirror, not the punishment.

### User
What if they say I need to lighten up, or they bring up a joke I told last month that was also kind of sharp?

### Assistant
If they say lighten up, you don't have to prove you're fun. You can say, "I'm fine with jokes. I'm not fine with that one about a body at the table." That's a boundary, not a personality.

If they bring up your old joke, don't fake amnesia. If it was also sharp, own it in one line: "Yeah, that one was also too much. I'm trying not to do that." Then return to lunch. Two wrongs is not a group pass. If their example is actually different — a roast you both agreed to — you can say so without a trial. "That was us going at each other. Today was about someone who didn't sign up."

Don't pile on a history of every time they've done it unless they ask. One incident keeps the talk small enough to survive. If this is a pattern you can't sit with, that's a later decision about how much lunch you share. You don't have to announce a breakup with a friend over one sandwich.

After you talk, let it be awkward for a day. Awkward is the point landing. If they adjust, you don't need to grade them. If they double down next week, you already said the true thing once. You can leave the table, or you can repeat it shorter. You don't owe a TED talk either time.

Ask the question, say the lunch, stop. That's the whole move.
