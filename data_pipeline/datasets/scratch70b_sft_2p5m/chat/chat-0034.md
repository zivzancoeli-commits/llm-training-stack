### User
Power just died. Laptop has some battery. Router is dead, so the internet is dead. I was in a meeting. Now I'm in the dark with a glowing rectangle like a raccoon. Tell me what you'd actually do in the next ten minutes. Don't give me a preparedness essay.

### Assistant
Minute one: stop poking the light switch like it owes you money. Check whether it's just your place or the block. Look out the window. If the street is dark, it's not your fuse. If the neighbor's kitchen is still on, then it's you — breaker box, if you know where it is and you can do it in the dark without being a hero. I won't pretend I know your panel. If you don't know, skip it and treat it as a cut.

Minute two: your meeting. You vanished. That's worse than a short message. If your phone still has data, you send one line to the host or the chat: "Power cut, dropped. I'll rejoin if I get a connection." Don't write a speech. If your phone has no data either, you don't sit there composing in a notes app like that helps them. You've done what you can for the meeting.

Are you on a laptop with a decent charge, or are we talking twenty minutes of panic-light?

### User
Maybe an hour if I dim it. Phone has some battery. Data is slow but it's there. I can hear the fridge dying. There's a standup in forty minutes I cannot miss because I'm presenting.

### Assistant
Next minutes, then, are not about the fridge. The fridge will be what it will be. You're buying a path to standup.

Minute three to five: kill anything on the laptop that isn't the deck or the notes you need. Screen dim. Keyboard light off. Don't open new tabs to "see if the outage is on a map." Your phone can do that later. The laptop is now a presenting device, not a browser.

Hotspot from the phone if you know how and if it still has data. That's your meeting pipe. Test it for thirty seconds: can you load the call link. If yes, you stay on phone data and you save the laptop for slides. If hotspot is hopeless, you present from the phone if the slides are in email or a doc you can open there. Ugly is fine. Missing is not.

Minute six: tell the people who need you. Slack, text, whatever already works on the phone: "Outage here. I have phone data. I'll be on at [standup time] from backup. If I drop, here is the one-sentence version of my update: [write it now]." That sentence is the whole insurance policy. Skeptical-uncle version: if the lights stay out, you still delivered the content.

Minute seven: find a place you can actually sit. Don't work in a hallway because it feels dramatic. Window light if it's day. Don't light candles next to the laptop like a movie. If it's night and you need a light, use the phone flashlight briefly, then put it down so you don't murder that battery too.

Minute eight to nine: plug nothing into a dead wall like you're testing reality. If you have a power bank, use it on the phone first, because the phone is the network. If you don't, you don't rummage for one. You already know.

Minute ten: you either have a hotspot path or you have a written update. That's the fork. You stop optimizing. You do not start emptying the freezer "just in case" during these ten minutes. That's a later problem. You also don't call the utility to perform anxiety. If you want a status later, the phone can do it after standup is set.

### User
Hotspot connected. Call audio is crunchy. I feel like I should apologize for five minutes at the start of standup.

### Assistant
Don't. One sentence: "I'm on phone data because the power's out. If I drop, I already pasted my update." Then you present. People can hear crunchy. They cannot use an apology.

If you drop, you don't keep reconnecting as a personality. You send the paste. That's the job. Ten minutes from the cut, you should be a person with a pipe and a backup sentence, not a person writing a memoir about the fridge. You've still got half an hour. Sit still. Don't spend it refreshing the lights.
