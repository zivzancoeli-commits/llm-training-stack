### User
Lights just died. Desktop is dead. I was in a shared doc with Dana editing a client deck. Phone is on the charger that no longer charges. I'm on a break I was already using to stare at the wall. Tell me what you'd actually do in the next ten minutes. No "build a go-bag" lecture.

### Assistant
Minute one: look outside. If the block is dark, it's not your fuse. If the neighbor's lamp is on, then it might be you — only open a breaker if you already know the box and you can do it without becoming a story. I don't know your panel. If you don't either, skip it.

Minute two: Dana. You vanished mid-doc. That's worse than a one-liner. If the phone still has battery, you send: "Power cut. Desktop dead. I'll pick up if I get a connection." Don't narrate the fridge.

What's left on the phone — twenty minutes, or enough to be useful?

### User
Maybe forty if I stop playing with brightness. Data is slow. There's a client call in thirty-five minutes. I'm supposed to share the deck. Dana has the file too but I was the one talking.

### Assistant
The next minutes are the call, not the ice cream melting.

Minute three: dim the phone. Kill the podcast you had paused. The phone is now the office.

Minute four and five: hotspot is irrelevant if the computer is a brick. You're presenting from the phone. Open the deck in email or the cloud copy if it loaded before the cut. If it won't load, text Dana: "Send the latest as a phone-friendly link or PDF. I'm presenting from mobile." Ugly is allowed. Missing is not.

Minute six: write the one-sentence version of your part of the call. Paste it to Dana and, if you have the client thread, there too: "If I drop, the decision we need is [whatever it actually is]." That's the insurance. Tired-coworker version: you still delivered the content even if the video dies.

Minute seven: sit somewhere with daylight if it's day. Don't light a candle next to a charging brick like a movie. Don't keep flipping the wall switch. It already answered.

Minute eight: if you have a power bank, it goes on the phone, not into a fantasy about the desktop. If you don't, you already know. Don't rummage.

Minute nine: test the call link for ten seconds. Camera off is fine. Audio is the job.

Minute ten: you either have a path into the call or you have a pasted sentence. You stop optimizing. You do not start a utility-company performance. You do not empty the freezer. That's later. You also don't write a paragraph in Notes titled "outage thoughts."

### User
Deck opened on the phone. Audio will be whatever. I feel like I should start the call with a long apology so they know I'm not flaky.

### Assistant
Don't. One line: "Power's out, I'm on phone, camera off. If I drop, Dana has the paste." Then you talk about the slides. People can hear tired. They cannot use a speech about your building.

If you drop, you don't spend the rest of the hour reconnecting as a personality. You send the paste. That's the job.

If Dana offers to take the talking, let them. Pride about being "the one who presents" is a bad generator.

You've got about twenty-five minutes left. Sit. Don't refresh the lights. Don't open a news outage map every thirty seconds; it burns battery and changes nothing. If you want a status, check once after the call.

For the fridge: after the call, not during these ten. Same for "should I go to a cafe." A cafe is a maybe later if the phone is dying and the call still matters. Right now you have a deck and a sentence. That's enough for a break-room brain.

### User
Alright. Phone is the office. Dana has the backup line. No apology tour. After the call I can think about cafes.

### Assistant
Ten minutes from the cut, you should be a person with a pipe and a backup sentence, not a person narrating the dark. You've still got time. Stay sitting. Do the call. Then you can be tired on purpose.
