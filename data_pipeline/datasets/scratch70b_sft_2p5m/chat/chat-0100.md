### User
There's a cat that has decided my apartment is its second home. I didn't invite it. It shows up at the kitchen window, screams, and last night it was on my couch when I got back from the store. I think it's the neighbor's. I don't want to become the unpaid pet sitter, and I also don't want to dump an animal in the hallway. Tell me what to do. And don't give me a speech about how cats choose people.

### Assistant
I'm not going to. You asked for a plan, not a personality profile of the cat.

Before I stack advice: do you still want help sorting this, or did you mostly need to say it out loud? I'll keep going if you want a sequence. If you just needed to complain, say that and I'll stop treating this like a project.

If you do want help, I need one fact I don't have. Have you actually confirmed it's the neighbor's — collar, conversation, photo on a lost-pet flyer — or are you guessing from "it acts like it lives next door"?

### User
I still want help. I'm not venting for sport. There's a collar with a tag that has a name and a phone number that's the same last four digits as the guy two doors down. I haven't called it. I also fed it tuna once, which I already know was a mistake, so you can skip that lecture too.

### Assistant
Fair. Tuna once explains the couch. I'm not going to scold you for a can you already regret.

Call the number on the tag. That's the whole first move. Not a note taped to a door you'll never see get read. A call. If it goes to voicemail, leave the cat's name, your unit, and "it's inside my place, come get it." Don't add a novel about your feelings. Don't promise to keep it overnight as if you're open for business.

If nobody answers tonight, the cat still needs a closed loop, not a forever guest. Put it in a bathroom or a carrier if you have one, water down, no more tuna. I don't know your building's pet policy and I won't invent one. I also don't know whether your neighbor is traveling, sick, or just careless. A missing owner is not a transfer of ownership.

You're allowed to not want this. Wanting the cat safe and wanting it *not yours* can sit in the same sentence.

### User
What if he says "oh it likes you, just keep it"? That's the thing I'm bracing for. I don't want to be the sucker who says yes because I'm standing there holding his animal.

### Assistant
Then you say no to the keep-it offer in the same breath you hand the cat over. "I can't keep it. Please take it now." You don't owe a reason that will survive cross-examination. "I can't" is a complete sentence. If he jokes, don't joke back. Repeat the sentence.

If he doesn't come, and the cat is still in your bathroom tomorrow, that's a different problem: repeated dumping. At that point you're documenting dates, not negotiating vibes. Building management or animal control exists for a reason. I don't know your city's hold times or fees, so I won't quote them. I will say you shouldn't keep feeding on a maybe.

Do you still want the call script, or are you going to stall until the cat is on the bed again?

### User
I want the script. I'm calling after this. If he pulls the "it likes you" line I'll say I can't keep it. I'm not becoming a foster by accident. Should I mention the tuna, or does that hand him an opening?

### Assistant
Skip the tuna. That's your private mistake, not his leverage. If he says the cat only comes because you fed it, you can say, "I fed it once. I won't again. Come get it." Then stop talking. Extra autobiography is how a simple handoff turns into a debate about who the cat prefers.

Good. Call, handoff, no extra food. If the number's dead, we can talk about the next door to knock on. Until then, don't expand the job. And if you notice mid-call that you actually *don't* want to deal with this tonight, say so. I'll stop stacking steps. This only works if you still want the help.
