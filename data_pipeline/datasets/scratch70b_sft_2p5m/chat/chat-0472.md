### User
I ordered a oat latte. I said my name is Ben. The barista grinned and wrote "Big Ben" and a clock. The person behind me laughed. I have to stand at the pickup counter and listen for a joke. This is the first time I want to say don't do that, without becoming a bit myself. They're steaming milk. I can still see the cup. It's not too late if I'm fast. What do I say that isn't a TED talk?

### Assistant
You treat it like a wrong order, because it is.

"Hey — can you put Ben, just Ben? I don't want the nickname on the cup." Point. If they have to remake the writing, they can tape a new cup or scratch it. I won't invent whether they charge you. You don't need a new latte. You need a name you can hear.

Don't grin first. Don't say "it's fine, it's funny" while asking them not to. That's two messages. Last year's you would have taken Big Ben and performed a laugh. This year's you can have four letters.

If they say it's already in the system, "Call Ben when it's ready, please." The shout is the part that matters. The Sharpie is second.

Don't lecture the line. Don't explain why nicknames land wrong. You don't owe the cafe your history.

Are they still holding the cup, or is it already in the queue of five?

### User
Still in their hand. They said "it's just a joke, lighten up" and the clock is still there. Milk is loud. I have three seconds of eye contact.

### Assistant
"I heard you. Still just Ben." Then you look at the pastry case so this doesn't become a staredown. If they write over it, good. If they set it down with the clock, you can say at pickup, "Which one is Ben?" and not respond to Big Ben if they shout it. You can also say, "I'm Ben," pick it up, leave. You asked. That's the first time.

"Lighten up" is a dismissal. You don't have to lighten. You also don't have to win the joke in front of the laugher. Three seconds is enough for the sentence. Then you're a customer again.

If they remake it with a flourish of more jokes, you don't have to thank them for the comedy. "Thanks" for the drink is enough.

### User
They crossed out Big and left Ben. They looked annoyed. I feel like I ruined their shift for a Sharpie. Was it worth it?

### Assistant
A crossed-out cup is a success, not a ruined shift. Baristas write names all day. You asked for yours. That's allowed. If they stay annoyed, they can be annoyed. You still drink the latte.

Pickup: listen for Ben. If they shout the old joke anyway, you take the cup and you go. You don't do a second education.

You didn't TED-talk. You asked for a name. Worth it if you didn't want to be a clock in a line. Get the drink. The person behind you can laugh at something else. You get a cup that says Ben. That's the whole invented name, without a bit. Leave with the latte. The steam can be loud. Your name can be small. That's fine.

### User
If they write Big Ben next time before I can speak, do I send it back or just take it?

### Assistant
You can take it if you're late. You can also say, "It's Ben," pick it up, and not send anything back. A remake is only worth it if you have time and you still want the correction. The first-time job was the ask while the cup was in their hand. You already did that.

If it happens again as a pattern, you can skip the grin and use the same sentence at the register before the marker: "Ben, no nicknames." That's a pre-ask. You're not a difficult customer for wanting four letters.

Drink the latte. Don't stand at the counter rehearsing a TED talk you already didn't give. The clock drawing can stay crossed out. You got Ben. Leave. Next time you order, you say the name first, then the drink, if that helps the marker land. That's a habit, not a war with a Sharpie. The oat milk can be ordinary. Your name can be too.
