### User
My roommate sent a paragraph that says we need to "realign domestic labor," "reset the shared living contract," and "avoid chore-blindness after high-traffic social hosting." Translation: we had people over Friday and Saturday, the sink looks like a science fair, and I left a wet towel on a chair. I think I know what they want. I also think if I answer in the same language we'll still be mad on Tuesday. How do I talk about this in regular words without sounding like I didn't read the message?

### Assistant
You read it. You just refuse to speak PowerPoint in a kitchen.

Plain speech for their three phrases:

"Realign domestic labor" means: who does dishes, trash, bathroom, and floors, and is that split still fair after a weekend of extra mess.

"Reset the shared living contract" means: say the rules out loud again, because the weekend quietly broke them. Not a lawyer thing. A "we both live here" thing.

"Chore-blindness" means: you stopped seeing the mess because you helped make it, or because you walked past it until it felt normal. "High-traffic social hosting" means: we had a lot of guests and the apartment took the hit.

You already almost have it. Answer in that translation, not theirs.

A reply that works: "Yeah, the weekend left the place wrecked. I'm in for a reset. Can we divide tonight's mess and then say who owns what for the week so this doesn't sit until Tuesday?"

That shows you understood without copying their vocabulary. Copying it can sound sarcastic even when you are not trying to be.

### User
They also said I have "task initiation issues" around the compost and that the bathroom is in "a deficit." I did the bathroom last Thursday. Compost I keep forgetting because the bin is under a board. I don't want a chart. I also don't want to do a fake apology for existing.

### Assistant
More translations, then you can throw the original email in the recycling.

"Task initiation issues" is therapist-adjacent for: you do not start the compost trip even when you know it is full. It is not a medical diagnosis. It is "you stall." If that is true, say so without a TED Talk. "I forget the compost because I have to move the board. I can take it out tonight and we'll keep the bin where I actually see it." That is a fix, not a personality trial.

"A deficit" in the bathroom means: they think it is dirtier than it should be, or they think you have done it less than your share. You have a Thursday data point. Use it. "I cleaned the bathroom Thursday. If it still looks bad after the weekend guests, let's both do a pass tonight instead of arguing who is behind." Guests wreck bathrooms. That is not mysterious.

Skip the chart if you both hate charts. A chart is just a list with lines. You can do a list once: dishes tonight you, trash and compost you because you admitted the stall, bathroom both because of guests, floors whoever finishes first. Write it in a note on the fridge for seven days. Then stop. Living contracts that need weekly branding are roommates avoiding a ten-minute conversation.

Do not apologize for existing. Apologize for the wet towel on the chair, because that one is yours and specific. "Sorry about the towel. I'll hang it. I'll do compost after this text." Specific beats "sorry I am a person who has friends."

If they keep sending jargon, you can say, kindly: "I get it. Can we talk in chores and days? I lose the plot when it's abstract." That is not anti-intellectual. That is how two people share a sink.

Tonight's order, in human: hang the towel, run the dishwasher or wash the stack, take compost out with the board left off or moved, wipe the bathroom surfaces you can see, text "started, bathroom next" so they are not staring at a silent paragraph. You already knew the plot. You needed the subtitles. Now you have them. Use regular words and a wet sponge. The essay can wait.
