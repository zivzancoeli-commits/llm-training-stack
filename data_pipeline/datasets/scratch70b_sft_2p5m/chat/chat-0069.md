### User
My sister is thirty-one. She could ride as a kid, then she stopped, then she had one bad fall on a rental at twenty-two and she has not been on a bike since. She asked me to help her try again before a family weekend where everyone is going on a paved trail. We have maybe two evenings and a Saturday morning. She does not want to spend money on a class or a new bike. I have an old hybrid in the garage that is probably her size if I lower the seat. I keep almost making this a whole training program in my head. She is close. She just does not believe she is close. How do we do this without turning it into a gear shop.

### Assistant
You already have the right instinct: she is close, and a program would get in the way. Keep the budget at "garage bike, free parking lot, no new helmet unless hers is cracked," and treat the rest as setup, not shopping.

Evening one is not the trail. Evening one is the empty lot behind the grocery store after it closes, or a quiet dead-end, or an unused church lot. Lower the seat until both feet are flat on the ground when she sits. That feels babyish. It is how you steal the fear of tipping. Do not start with pedals as the main event. Have her walk the bike, then scoot it like a strider, pushing with her feet, looking forward, braking with her hands until the stop is boring. If she can scoot, glide a few feet, and stop without panic, you already did the expensive class's first hour for the price of whatever air the tires still hold.

Check the tires and the brakes before you leave the garage. If a tire is soft, a pump you already own is the spend. If the brakes feel like mush, that is a safety stop, not a character issue. I will not quote you a shop price. If you cannot make the brakes trustworthy with an adjustment you already know how to do, you postpone. A free plan that includes a bad brake is not a plan.

Evening two, if the scoot-glide is there, put the seat up a thumb or two so the knees can turn, and add pedals in a straight line only. You jog beside her without holding the bars unless she asks. Holding the bars teaches her your balance, not hers. Agree on a word that means stop. Not a yell. A word.

Saturday morning, only if those two evenings were calm: the paved trail at the empty hour, same bike, same helmet if she has one that still fits. Short out-and-back. You ride slightly behind, not in front like a tour guide. The goal is "I rode and I stopped on purpose," not miles.

She almost has it. Your job is to keep the evening small enough that her body can remember, and cheap enough that a bad day does not also include a receipt.

### User
The helmet in the garage is mine and it is too big for her. She asked if we should buy one. Also she wants me to hold the seat the whole time because that is how dad taught us. I already know that is the wrong instinct and I can feel myself giving in so she will get on the bike at all.

### Assistant
You almost have this part too. Do not buy a helmet tonight. Ask one person you already know and borrow for a week. Used from a thrift shop or a buy-nothing group beats a new boxed one. I will not invent a price. If you cannot borrow or find used before Saturday, skip the trail. She can still scoot in the parking lot. A tight budget includes delaying the trail, not skipping a helmet around other riders.

Hold the seat for the first two scoot-glides so she gets on. Then take your hand off out loud: "Letting go in three, two, one." If you never let go, she learns your knuckles, not the bike. If she freezes, go back to feet-on-the-ground scooting, not a death-grip. Keep the garage bike, a borrowed helmet if you can, the empty lot, and your hand off after two tries. That is the whole program.
