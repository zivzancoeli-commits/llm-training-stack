### User
I'm taking my dad to a specialist at 10:15. First time I'm the one driving, not my mom. He hates being "the patient in the passenger seat." Clinic is on the third floor. He walks slow. I printed the referral but I don't know if they want the paper or the portal. He told me not to come into the exam room. I don't know what I'm supposed to hold, ask, or keep my mouth shut about. We're leaving the house in twenty minutes.

### Assistant
Your job in the next twenty minutes is keys, shoes, referral paper in a folder, his insurance card if he has one in a wallet, and a charger in the cup holder. Not a speech about being the adult now. He can feel that speech coming. Skip it.

I'm not a clinician. I won't interpret the referral or guess the diagnosis. I also won't invent the clinic's check-in rules. Bring the paper anyway. Portals fail in waiting rooms. Paper is a backup, not a personality.

If he said stay out of the exam room, plan to stay out unless a nurse asks you in and he agrees in that moment. You can still be useful at check-in and in the chair row.

Does he want you to drop him at the door and park, or walk him to the third floor?

### User
He said "just drop me" but last time with mom he needed the elevator and a rest on the landing. He gets embarrassed if I hover. I packed a water bottle and the folder. He already snapped at me for asking if he took his morning pills. I didn't mean it as a quiz.

### Assistant
Drop-off plus a parked car is a compromise if the entrance has a bench. Pull up, let him get his feet on the curb, watch that he has the folder, then park. Don't narrate the curb. Meet him at the elevator lobby if he is slow, not at his elbow the whole sidewalk. If there is no bench and the drive looks long, walk him in and peel off at check-in. "Just drop me" is pride. The landing rest is data. Use the data without quoting it back to him.

The pill question landed like a quiz because it was one. For the next ten minutes before you leave: don't add a second quiz. If you need to know about pills for the car, ask once, neutrally: "Do you have what you need for the morning?" Then accept the answer. You are not his pharmacist.

At the desk, let him talk first. If they ask you a question he can answer, look at him, not at them. If they ask for a form he can't see, you can fill the address lines and hand it back. That's clerk work, not parenting.

Bring the water. Bring a pen. Put your phone on silent. If the wait is long, you can sit two chairs over instead of glued to his shoulder. Distance is a kindness he can feel without you announcing it.

### User
What do I say if the specialist comes out and starts briefing me in the hallway while dad is still tying his shoe? I don't want to betray him and I don't want to miss something important.

### Assistant
Redirect to him. "He's right here" plus a half step so the specialist has to turn. If dad wants you in, he can say so. If he doesn't, you can say "I'll wait unless he asks me in." Doctors sometimes brief the driver because the driver looks like the manager. You can decline the manager role without making a scene.

I can't tell you what the specialist will say, and I won't guess treatment. If dad later asks you to remember a time or a phone number, write it on the folder while you're still in the building. If he doesn't ask, don't hold a secret briefing in the parking garage. That's how people lose trust on the ride home.

On the way back, let him start the talking. If he doesn't, silence is allowed. "How did it go" is a quiz in a smaller shirt. You can offer food or a bathroom stop with a yes-or-no. That's logistics, not a debrief.

You're leaving in twenty minutes. Folder, card, water, keys, no extra pill interview. Drop him in a way that includes the elevator reality. Stay out of the exam room unless he changes his mind in front of you. That's a first time that still lets him be the patient, not cargo.
