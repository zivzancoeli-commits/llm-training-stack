### User
There's a drip under my kitchen sink. Not a flood. A drip. Cabinet floor is wet, sour smell, I opened the doors and immediately regretted it. I don't know if it's the trap, the supply line, or the dishwasher hose. My uncle is going to ask what I did in the first ten minutes like it's a test. Walk me through those ten minutes like you're the one kneeling there, not a blog.

### Assistant
Minute one: I pull everything out of the cabinet. Bottles, trash bag, the mystery pan. I want a clear floor so I can see the drip, not a sponge sitting in soup. I put a dry towel or a baking tray under the pipes.

Minute two: I find the shutoff valves — two of them, hot and cold, on the wall or coming through the floor. Clockwise until they stop. If they won't budge, I don't muscle them until they snap. I go to the house shutoff instead. I am not guessing which pipe is "probably fine."

Minute three: I dry the area so I can see a new drop. A wet cabinet lies. A dry one tells you where the next bead forms.

You still have the water on right now, yeah? Don't shop for parts until the valves have actually moved.

### User
Valves turned. Stiff, but they went. Drip slowed, then a last few drops. There's a U-shaped pipe with white crust, and a skinny dishwasher hose with a clamp older than me. No puddle on the supply lines that I can see. Uncle would say I don't know what I'm looking at. Fine. What do I look at.

### Assistant
You look at the U — that's the P-trap — and at that dishwasher hose. Crust usually means it's been seeping, not that it exploded today. The clamp is a suspect because clamps loosen and dishwashers dump a lot of water at once.

Minutes four and five: I run my fingers along the trap joints, the slip nuts, and the dishwasher connection. Feeling for slime, not staring from two feet away. Wet at a nut is a joint leak. Wet along the hose is the hose or the clamp.

I don't unscrew the trap in minute six unless I have a bucket and I accept that trap water is disgusting. Tonight, a bucket and a dry cabinet might be the whole play.

Minutes six and seven: if the leak showed up after a dishwasher cycle, I leave the dishwasher off. I don't run a "test cycle." That's how you refill the cabinet.

Minutes eight through ten: I photograph the wet spots and the clamp for the hardware store or a plumber. I leave dry towels in there so I can see if it rewets. I toss soaked stuff that will mold, leave the doors open, and I don't "just use the sink until Monday."

### User
If the drip was the trap nut, do I crank it tighter? Uncle swears that's all it ever is.

### Assistant
I try snug, not hero-tight. Plastic nuts crack. Metal ones strip. A quarter turn past finger-tight is a test. If it still weeps, I stop. Teflon tape on a slip-joint trap is often the wrong instinct; those joints usually seal with a washer, not wrap.

I'd rather buy a cheap trap kit than round off the nut "because uncle." If the clamp is rusted, I replace the clamp. If I don't know which way the washer faces, I admit that at the store instead of assembling it backwards.

I'm not inventing a diagnosis from here. Crusty trap plus crusty clamp is two suspects. Ten minutes is for stopping water and seeing the next drop, not for becoming a plumber.

If it rewets overnight with the valves off, I stop playing. If it stays dry until I crack a valve, I keep the sink off, use the bathroom, and deal with parts in daylight.

That's the ten minutes I'd actually spend on my knees: empty, shut, dry, feel, photo, don't run the dishwasher, don't strip the nut. Your uncle can grade the photos. He doesn't get to grade your panic.
