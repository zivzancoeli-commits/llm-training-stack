### User
A distant cousin died. I wasn't close, but I was closer than my mother was. She wants me not to go. She says the person was cruel years ago, that showing up "makes a statement," that I should come to brunch instead so she isn't alone with her feelings. The funeral is Saturday. Brunch is her rewrite. I think I'm going. I have never gone against her on a death. Help me say that without a documentary about who was right in 1998.

### Assistant
You can go. She can dislike the dead person. Those can both be true.

"I'm going to the funeral. I can see you another time this weekend." Brunch is not a moral substitute. If she needs company with her feelings, that can be Friday or Sunday. Saturday is spoken for if you decide it is.

You do not have to litigate 1998. You weren't appointed. If she starts the case, "I'm not going to retry that. I'm going to the service." Repeat. Cases want exhibits. You don't bring any.

Making a statement is her frame. Your frame can be smaller: you knew them enough to sit there. Sitting is not a verdict on the whole life.

I will not invent funeral etiquette law. Wear what the family asked if they asked. Be brief with anyone who wants gossip in the parking lot.

Are you going alone, or is someone else from your side going who isn't your mother?

### User
Alone, unless I fold. She already told my aunt that I'm "considering brunch." So now if I go, my aunt will think I snubbed my mother. The story is already moving. I didn't even pack a dark shirt yet.

### Assistant
Correct the story in a text, short, to mother and to aunt if aunt is in it: "I'm going to the funeral Saturday. I'll see Mom another time." You cannot control the remix. You can stop feeding the brunch version.

Pack the shirt. Logistics make the decision real. People fold in the gap where there's no parking plan and no ironed sleeve.

If mother says you're choosing the cousin over her, answer the feeling without accepting the math. "I'm not ranking you. I'm going to a funeral. I'll call you after." Then you actually call after, or you don't promise a call you won't make. Empty after-calls are how she proves you vanished.

Aunt can think what she thinks. You don't owe a three-way call. One factual text is plenty.

Folding would be brunch as obedience. You already said you think you're going. Treat that as the decision, not as a mood.

### User
What do I say at the funeral if someone from that side asks why my mother isn't there.

### Assistant
"She couldn't make it" or "She's not here today" is enough. You don't volunteer the feud. You don't defend the dead with a counter-feud. If they push, "I came to pay respects" and you look at the program. Parking-lot court is optional. Skip it.

Saturday you go, you sit, you leave when it's over. Mother gets a different hour. No 1998 documentary. No brunch as a stand-in death rite. Shirt in the bag tonight so the story has to catch up to a person who already decided.

### User
I sent the text. Mother replied that brunch is "really for me, not against them." Aunt hearted mother's message. The funeral is still Saturday. I packed the shirt. I feel like I already attended a different ceremony in the family chat.

### Assistant
You did attend a chat ceremony. Saturday is still a different room. You don't have to unheart anything. You don't have to write a second manifesto. Shirt in the bag is the vote that counts.

If brunch really is for her feelings, you can offer Sunday coffee or a walk, if you will actually go. Don't offer Saturday morning. Don't let "for me" swallow the only hour the funeral occupies. Two needs can share a weekend without sharing a timestamp.

At the service, you sit, you sign the book if there is one, you leave. You call after if you promised. Chat hearts are not a seating chart. You are going. That was the decision. The 1998 documentary can stay unproduced. Distant cousin, dark shirt, your own reason, small enough to say and large enough to keep.
