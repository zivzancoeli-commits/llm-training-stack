### User
I start next week. Bus is eating my time. I found a bike on a listing: "barely used, great for commute." Seller wants to meet in a parking lot tonight. I have a tight budget and I don't want to get a lemon or get robbed. Walk me through this like I have never bought anything from a stranger except a sandwich.

### Assistant
We're going to make a plan that fits a small budget, not a fantasy bike. I'm not going to invent a "fair price." I don't know your city, the bike, or what similar ones sell for this week. Treat any listing number as an ask, not a fact.

Budget means three pots, not one: the bike, a lock and lights if it doesn't include them, and a little left so a no doesn't empty you. If buying this would zero you out, it's too expensive even if the photo is shiny. A cheap bike you can't lock is a donation to the next person.

Tonight in a parking lot is only okay if it's public, lit, and you can leave. If it's behind a building at night, reschedule to daylight in front of a cafe or a busy lot. Bring a person if you can. Tell someone where you're going. That's intern-level basic, and it's the right basic.

What number is actually in your head for bike plus lock, and is that number rent-safe?

### User
I can do about the same as two weeks of bus fare, and I still have to eat. The listing doesn't show a price in the message you saw because I didn't paste it. They said "firm" in the title. I don't even know if firm means I shouldn't talk.

### Assistant
"Firm" is seller talk for "I don't want to haggle." It is not a law. You can still say no, walk away, and ask questions. Don't treat firm as a dare. On a tight budget, walking away is the skill, not winning a five-dollar argument.

Don't send money, a deposit, or a code before you see the bike. If they ask you to pay to "hold it," that's a no. Meet, look, decide, pay. If they can't do that, it's not your bike.

At the meeting, check that it can get you to work. Ask them to ride it in a circle. You ride it too, if it fits. Brakes should stop you. Gears should change without grinding. Tires shouldn't be dry rot. Wheels shouldn't wobble. Seat and bars should clamp, not slide. If you don't know part names, say "Show me the brakes" and squeeze them.

Look at the serial number if there is one, and whether the story matches the wear. "Barely used" with a rusted chain is a story. You are not accusing them in the lot. You are deciding not to buy a mismatch.

I won't quote what a used bike "should" cost. Search local listings for the same kind and see the range. If this one is an outlier, ask why, or skip it. Your budget doesn't have room to fund someone else's optimism.

### User
What do I pay with. Cash feels stupid. An app feels like I can get scammed. Also what if it dies on day three.

### Assistant
Cash in a public place, for the amount you agreed after you saw it, is common because it ends the deal on the spot. Don't flash a wad. Count what you need before you go and leave the rest home. If you hate cash, some people use a payment app in person after the test ride. I'm not going to tell you which app is safe. Confirm the name in front of you, don't follow a QR from a text, and don't "send extra for a hold." If payment gets weird, you leave.

There is no honest guarantee a stranger's bike lasts. That's the trade for paying less than a shop. Reduce the chance: ride it, check brakes, don't buy the first listing out of panic. If you have a little money left, a lock and lights matter more than a prettier frame.

If it's a maybe, ask whether a local shop will do a safety check, and treat that cost as part of the budget. I won't name a shop fee. If that check would blow the budget, pick a simpler bike or keep the bus another week.

Tonight, in order: public daylight if you can change it; a person with you; no money ahead; test ride; walk away if it feels off or if paying would empty you; lock and lights after you have a working bike. You are not rude for saying "I'm going to pass." You are someone with rent. That's the whole plan.
