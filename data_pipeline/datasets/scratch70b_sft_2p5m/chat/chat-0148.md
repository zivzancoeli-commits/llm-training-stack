### User
The kid's tree costume split up the back twenty minutes before curtain. Glue, felt, a paper crown. I am not going to do the "this is a fun adventure" voice. They're crying, the teacher is hovering, and if another adult says "it's fine!" I might actually walk out. Talk to me like a skeptical uncle, not a camp counselor.

### Assistant
It's not fine. The costume failed. That's a real, small disaster, not a lesson about resilience in a newsletter.

Don't cheerlead. Don't lecture. Do the next physical thing.

Turn them away from the hallway audience. Sit or kneel so you're not talking down from adult height. Say, "The back ripped. We're going to close it enough that you can go on. You don't have to smile about it." Then stop talking. Crying can finish a lap.

I won't pretend I know the school's costume rules or whether glue is allowed backstage. I also won't diagnose anyone's feelings as "stage fright" like it's a stamp.

What is the rip, exactly — seam, felt panel, or the whole trunk hanging off?

### User
Felt panel, from the neck to about the waist. Safety pins in my bag. The kid wants to quit. Teacher said "the show must go on" which made it worse.

### Assistant
"The show must go on" is adult poetry. Kids hear: your feelings are in the way of our night. Don't repeat it.

Safety pins: good. Overlap the felt so the gap isn't a stripe of shirt, pin from the inside if you can so the metal isn't a weapon, three or four pins, not one heroic pin that will pop on stage. If the shirt underneath is dark, even better. If it's a bright tee, pin anyway and let it be a slightly ugly tree. Ugly tree still reads as tree from row six.

Ask the kid one question that isn't a pep talk: "Do you want to go on with pins, or do you want to sit this one out?" That's a real fork. If they sit out, that's allowed unless the school has some contract I can't see from here. Don't threaten them with wasting everyone's time. Don't bribe them with ice cream in the same breath as the pins. Bribes turn into a second argument.

If they go on: "I'll pin it. You don't have to be happy. You just have to not yank the back." Practical. Skeptical-uncle tone is respect for the problem.

Teacher: you can say, "We'll be a minute. Please don't say must-go-on to them right now." You don't need a fight in the wings. You need space.

### User
There's another parent filming the meltdown. And I keep wanting to joke "well, that's theater."

### Assistant
Don't joke "that's theater" while they're crying. That's you performing for the filming parent. Joke later in the car if you must, without an audience of a kid who just lost a costume.

Filming: "Please don't record this." If they keep going, step your body between the camera and the kid. You don't need a legal speech. I won't invent recording laws for a school hallway. You can still say no with your body and a short sentence.

After the pins, give them a job that isn't "be brave": hold the crown, find their mark, wait for their entrance. Jobs beat slogans.

If they go on and the pins show, you don't clap extra-loud as compensation. Clap like the other kids. After, "The pins held. You did the scene." Not "see, it was fine." It wasn't fine. They did it anyway, or they didn't. Both are information, not a moral.

If they refuse: take the costume off their shoulders so they're not standing there as a failed tree. Go to the lobby or the car. Tell the teacher they're out. You can be sorry for the cast without making your kid pay in public.

Tonight is glue and felt. Tomorrow you can talk about a sturdier close — snaps, a second layer, a backup shirt in the same color — if they still want the part. Not now. Now is pins or exit.
