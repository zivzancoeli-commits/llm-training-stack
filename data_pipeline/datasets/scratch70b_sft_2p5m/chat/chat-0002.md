### User
First day in this house-share kitchen and someone already asked me to "just finish the sink" because they have a call. It's 8:40 p.m. I cooked one pan of eggs. The sink is a geology exhibit. I don't want to start a war, but I also don't want to become the new person who does everyone else's dishes. How do I say no without inventing a meeting or a migraine?

### Assistant
Say no to the pile, not to the person. You can be tired and still be clear.

A sentence that works: "I'm not going to do the sink tonight. I'll wash what I used." Then you wash your pan, your fork, your plate, and you leave. You do not add "I would, but..." and you do not invent a call of your own. Fake excuses train people to wait until your excuse expires.

If they push with "it'll only take a minute," repeat the same line once. Repeating is not rude. Inventing a reason is what makes you sound like you're bargaining.

Do you know whose dishes those are, or is it a mixed pile?

### User
Mixed pile. Three mugs I didn't touch, a crusted pan, someone's blender base. The person who asked is apparently the one who "usually keeps things moving." They smiled when they said it. I'm on a break from unpacking and I just want tea.

### Assistant
The smile is the trap. It makes refusal feel like you failed a vibe check. You did not. A shared kitchen is not a volunteer shift you inherited by walking in.

Make the tea. While the kettle boils, wash your egg pan. Put it in the rack. If they are still in the doorway, you can add one practical boundary: "I'll label a shelf tomorrow. Tonight I'm only cleaning my stuff." That is information, not an apology.

Do not pre-agree to a chore wheel at 8:40 p.m. on day one while you are hungry and half-unpacked. Chore wheels decided under social pressure become your job plus their job. Tomorrow, in daylight, you can ask who owns which shelf and whether there is already a written rotation. Tonight you protect the precedent: new person does not equal sink staff.

If they look disappointed, let them. Disappointment is allowed. You are not required to fix their face.

### User
What if they tell the group chat I'm not a team player? I haven't even met two of the roommates.

### Assistant
Then you still washed your pan. Group chats love a story. Your story can be boring and accurate: you arrived, you cooked, you cleaned your dishes, you went to bed. If someone later asks in person, use the same sentence you used at the sink. Consistency is what makes "not a team player" sound thin.

You can even offer a real yes later, on your terms. "I'll take a weeknight of counters once I know the system" is a yes with a date. "I'll do this mountain so you can take a call" is a yes with no end.

Tired-coworker version, because that's the energy you have: you would not stay late to finish a stranger's inbox on your first day. Dishes are the same politics with more grease.

When you walk out, leave the kettle in a neutral spot, not as a peace offering of extra mugs washed. Extra mugs are how the pile learns your name.

Tomorrow morning, take a photo of the empty rack after you wash breakfast. Not to post. To remind yourself what "your share" looks like. If someone wants a house meeting, go. Listen. Still do not confess to a debt you never took on.

No is a complete answer. You do not owe a medical subplot or a calendar lie. Wash what you used. Drink the tea. Unpack. The sink was already a geology exhibit before you fried the eggs. It can wait for the people who built it, or for a written plan that includes you as one vote, not as the mop.
