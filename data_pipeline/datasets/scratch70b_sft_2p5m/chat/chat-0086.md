### User
I honked at someone in the left-turn lane this morning, then I rolled down the window and called them an idiot because they didn't go on the first gap. It was my partner in the other car. We were heading to the same appointment. They already knew it was me by the time I realized. I got to the lot first. They sat in the car for a minute before coming in. We made it through the appointment without a scene, and now I'm in the work bathroom trying not to replay it. I need to apologize tonight and I don't want to do the fake "sorry you felt that way" thing, and I also don't want a speech. What do I actually say?

### Assistant
Two workable shapes, then I'll pick one.

Option A is short and specific, in person, before you start dinner or whatever the evening's first task is. You name the behavior, you don't explain the traffic, you ask what they need. Something like: "I honked and I yelled at you this morning. That was me losing it, not you failing a turn. I'm sorry. I don't want to debate the gap. I want to know if you're still angry, and what would help tonight." Then you stop. You do not add the story about running late unless they ask.

Option B is a pause plus a written line, then the in-person part. If they went quiet after the appointment, they may not want a full conversation the second you walk in. You send one message on the way home: "I owe you an apology for this morning. I'd rather say it in person than type a paragraph. Tell me if tonight is okay or if you want space." Then you follow whatever they answer. The spoken apology is still the same as A, just later.

I would do A if you will see them within a couple of hours and they are not in the middle of putting a kid to bed or on a work call. I would do B if the house is already tense or you tend to overtalk when you feel guilty. You sounded like you want this done tonight, so my recommendation is A, with a hard stop after the question.

The part that usually wrecks these is the because-clause. "Sorry I snapped, because you didn't take the gap and we were late" is not an apology. It is a verdict with a sorry glued on. If they want to talk about the turn later, they can. Tonight you own the honk and the insult.

### User
They texted "we can talk later." That's it. No emoji, no period even, just those four words. Does that mean I should still do A when I get home, or did they just pick B for me?

### Assistant
They picked the timing. "Later" is not "never," and it is not "please start the speech at the door." Don't ambush them in the kitchen at 6:02. Don't send a second text explaining the first text.

When you get home, keep it to one sentence you can say while putting your bag down: "I got your text. I'm here when later is later." Then you do the evening. If they open it, you use the A script. If they don't, you don't fill the silence with a TED talk about your stress.

If "later" still hasn't happened by the time you're both actually off-duty, you can ask once, not as a demand: "Is later tonight, or do you want this tomorrow?" One ask. If they say tomorrow, you wait.

Don't promise "I'll never honk again" unless you have a plan you can keep. A keepable plan is boring: phone in the bag, leave ten minutes earlier. Say that only if it's true and only after they want to talk. Tonight is the insult. The calendar is a different conversation.

### User
I'm worried they'll say I humiliated them in public. There were other cars. I don't think anyone heard the window thing except them, but I don't know. Should I lead with that?

### Assistant
Don't lead with a courtroom about who overheard. You don't know, and guessing makes it about your embarrassment instead of theirs. If they say they felt exposed, believe that sentence. You can say, "I didn't think anyone else heard, and that doesn't make yelling at you okay." Then stop.

Skip "you know how I get when I'm late." Skip humor. A tired person can hear a joke as you trying to skip the hard part.

You still want help tonight, or do you want me to drop this until you're home?
