### User
I have to call a new doctor's office in like ten minutes to set up as a patient. I hate phone stuff. I don't even know if I should tell them why I want to be seen or if that's a later thing. I have a list in my notes app that's too long. I'm tired and I keep putting this off. Walk me through what I actually do when they pick up.

### Assistant
When they pick up, you are not presenting a case. You are booking a first visit and answering intake. That's smaller than the list in your notes.

I am not going to guess a diagnosis. I'm also not going to invent this clinic's rules. Offices differ. Be accurate and short, then write down what they tell you.

For the next ten minutes, do this in order. First, sit down with the phone, a pen, and your insurance card if you have one. Circle one reason for the visit: "I need a new primary care doctor" or "I have a thing I want a clinician to look at." The rest can wait for the appointment.

Second, if they put you on hold, stay on hold. Don't start washing dishes.

Third, when a human is there, lead with: "Hi, I'd like to become a new patient and schedule a first appointment." Then let them run their script. They will ask date of birth, insurance, maybe a referring name. If you don't know an answer, say you don't know. Don't bluff a member ID.

Do you already know the clinic name and whether you need a referral, or is this a cold call from a list?

### User
It's a clinic a coworker named. No referral that I know of. Insurance is through work, card is in my wallet. The reason is I've been more tired than usual for a couple months and I don't have a regular doctor. I don't want them to think I'm dramatic. Also I hate saying "tired" because it sounds fake.

### Assistant
Tired for a couple of months is a reason people call clinics. You don't have to diagnose yourself on the phone. "I've been unusually tired for a couple of months and I need a primary care visit" is enough. If they ask whether it's urgent, say you need an appointment, not the emergency room. I can't tell you whether something is urgent from a chat. If you have chest pain, fainting, or something that scares you right now, that's a different call.

If they offer a date six weeks out, take it unless you already know you need sooner, in which case you ask, "Is there a cancellation list?" That's a normal question. Don't argue with the calendar.

Write down: date, time, address, what to bring, whether you need to arrive early for paperwork. Repeat the time back once. People lose first appointments because they trusted their memory on a tired afternoon.

If they ask for a list of medications, read the bottles or the pharmacy app. If you don't take any, say none. If they ask about allergies, same. Don't invent a complete medical history because you feel unprepared. Incomplete and true beats a story.

You have about six minutes before you said you'd call. Card out, one sentence reason, pen. That's the prep.

### User
What if they ask a bunch of questions I don't know, like last physical or family history, and I freeze?

### Assistant
Then you say, "I don't know that off the top of my head. I can bring it to the visit." Reception is not the exam. Some intake forms are long on purpose. You are allowed to have gaps. Freezing is usually you trying to sound like a perfect patient. Perfect patients don't exist. Tired coworkers exist, and they still get appointments.

If the questions feel clinical and you're not talking to a nurse, you can ask, "Is this for scheduling or should I wait for the clinician?" That's not rude. That's keeping the call in its lane.

After you hang up, send yourself the appointment details in a text. Put it on the calendar. Drink water. You don't have to process the tiredness on this call. You only have to get a time on a book.

Ten minutes from now: sit, card, one sentence, listen, write, repeat the time. The rest of the list stays in the notes app for the visit. Call.
