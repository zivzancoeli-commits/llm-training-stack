### User
First day in a house with three other people. I went to make toast and someone had left a note on the toaster that said "community, help yourself" next to a loaf that is definitely not mine. Then one of them asked if I could take their dish shift tonight because they have a date. I don't want to start a war. I also don't want to be the new person who does everyone else's chores. How do I say no without inventing a fake plan?

### Assistant
You say no without a fake plan by using a real sentence: "I can't take your shift tonight." That's the whole thing. You don't owe a story about a dentist, a headache, or a friend who may or may not exist.

The toast note is a separate pile. Don't eat the loaf. "Community" written by one person is not a house vote. If you're hungry, use your own food or ask out loud, "Is this actually shared?" Asking is ruder in your head than in the room.

Which part is happening in the next hour — the dish shift ask, or the bread?

### User
The dish shift is tonight. The bread is sitting there looking at me. I already said "I'll think about it" to the dishes, which I know is a maybe they heard as yes. I hate that I did the thing I used to do last year, the freeze-and-agree thing.

### Assistant
Then unfreeze it before dinner. Find the person and say, "I thought about it. I can't cover tonight." If they look disappointed, let them look disappointed. You are not required to fill the silence with "unless you really need me." That's a second yes wearing a costume.

You don't need an excuse. "I just moved in and I'm not picking up extra chores on day one" is a reason if you want one. It is still not required. A no can stand without a footnote.

On the bread: leave it. Make your toast from your bag. If someone later says you were antisocial for not eating their sourdough, that's a strange complaint and you can ignore it.

Shared kitchens run on two boring rules you can adopt today without a meeting: your name on your shelf, and you wash what you use. You don't have to announce either rule like a manifesto. You just do them.

### User
What if they say "come on, it's your first night, be cool"? I can already hear it. I will want to laugh and fold.

### Assistant
If they say be cool, you can say, "I'm good. I can't do the dishes tonight." Same no. You can even smile. A smile is not a contract.

Do not offer a replacement night in the same breath unless you actually want that replacement night. "Not tonight, but Thursday" is a different deal. Only say Thursday if Thursday is real.

You're talking to me like last year's you, the one who collected other people's inconvenience. This year's you can decline a date-night dish shift on day one. That's not a war. That's a boundary with toast crumbs on it.

Put your food on one shelf. Take a picture of the shelf if you're the type who forgets which one. When the dish person asks again in person, shorter is kinder than a speech. Speeches sound like you're asking permission.

### User
I'll tell them after this. "I can't take your shift tonight." No dentist story. I'm making my own toast. If they ask why, I was going to say I have a call. I don't have a call.

### Assistant
Then don't invent the call. If they ask why, you can say "I don't want to take extra chores on my first night" or you can say nothing but the no. Both are honest. A fake call is last year's move, and it trains them to negotiate around your calendar instead of hearing the no.

Go eat. If they push a second time, repeat the same sentence and walk the plate to your room. You don't have to win the vibe. You have to keep your night. Tomorrow you can talk about a real chore chart if the house even has one. Tonight is toast that is yours and a sink that isn't your date-night gift.
