### User
My team keeps asking me on this cabin trip. I don't want to go. I also don't want to invent a "family obligation" or a fake wedding. People at work talk in this coded way — bandwidth, optics, protecting my energy — and I sound like a LinkedIn post when I try. I'm new. Translate it into speech a human would send.

### Assistant
Plain speech first, jargon second so you can hear the swap.

"I don't have bandwidth" means: I don't want to spend Saturday and Sunday on this.
"I need to protect my energy" means: I'm already tired and a cabin will make that worse.
"It might look like I'm not a team player" means: I'm scared you'll think I'm cold.
"I'll circle back" means: I'm stalling so I don't have to say no.

You don't need any of those. You need one true sentence and one boundary. True sentence: you don't want to go. Boundary: you're not going to fake a conflict.

Intern-plain: "Thanks for asking. I'm going to skip the cabin. I don't want to do a weekend trip. Have a good time — I'll see you Monday."

That's the whole note. No sick parent. No mysterious car trouble. No "maybe next time" unless you actually want next time. "Maybe next time" is a polite lie if you already know you hate group cabins.

If they push with "come on, it's bonding," translate that too. Bonding here means they want you in the photos. You can answer, "I get that it's a bonding thing. I'm still not going." You're not failing orientation. You're declining a social extra.

If someone more senior says they "really want full participation," ask what they actually mean. Participation at work is the job. A cabin is optional unless your offer letter said otherwise, which it almost certainly didn't. I won't invent a labor rule for your country. If you're scared this is secretly required, ask your manager in one line: "Is the cabin expected, or optional?" That's clarifying the assignment, which is an intern's actual job.

### User
What if they ask why, and "I don't want to" feels rude?

### Assistant
"I don't want to" is blunt. You can keep it true and add a little clothing without adding fiction.

True clothing: "Weekends I keep for rest and my own stuff. A cabin isn't rest for me." Still true, still not a fake wedding.

What you don't do: list three reasons. Three reasons sound like you're negotiating. One reason is a decision.

If they keep going — "you'll change your mind," "everyone's coming," "we already bought food" — you repeat the decision, not the essay. "I'm not going. I hope the food gets eaten." Then you stop answering in the thread. Repeat-once is polite. Repeat-four-times is a debate club.

If you do want a smaller yes, make it specific and real. "I can do Friday drinks after work, not the overnight." Only say that if you'll actually go. A fake Friday is the same class of lie as a fake wedding, just shorter. "Just come for Saturday" is still a trip; you can say no to that too.

You're new, so you might worry the no will follow you. Some groups are cliquey. That's a real social cost. The honest trade is: you might be slightly outside the in-jokes for a week, and you keep your weekend. I won't pretend there's zero cost. I also won't tell you to buy belonging with a trip you resent. Resentment leaks.

### User
Draft the Slack message. No "circling back." No "so sorry!" parade.

### Assistant
"Hey — thanks for including me. I'm not going to the cabin this weekend. Not a maybe; I'm out. Have fun. See you Monday."

"Thanks for including me" is already the warmer word. Don't add a paragraph about mental health unless you want that to be the story.

If a person DMs "why though," answer in the DM, still plain: "I like you all at work. I don't want to do a weekend trip. That's the whole reason."

No excuse file. No fake calendar block. If you already blocked the calendar with a fake event, delete it. A fake block is a lie that can collide with someone tagging you.

The translation of a clean no is: you say it, you don't decorate it, you show up Monday and do your work.

### Assistant
