### User
I'm in the rec-league parking lot. Warm-up starts in ten minutes. I slept badly, my ankle has that hot tight feeling it gets before it gets stupid, and I keep drafting texts that sound like I'm quitting the team. Last year I would have taped it and played anyway. I don't want a speech. I want the next ten minutes.

### Assistant
Then we do ten minutes like a checklist, not a TED talk. You're tired. I'm not going to cheerlead you into hero soccer. I'm also not a doctor, so I won't name an injury. Hot ankle plus bad sleep is enough to justify a rest day as a decision, not a courtroom exhibit.

Minute one: stay in the car. Don't walk toward the field yet. Last-year-you used the walk as a trap. Open notes, not the team chat. If you draft in the chat you'll send a half-sentence.

Minutes two to five: four lines, max. "I need to sit out today. My ankle is hot and I slept badly. I shouldn't play. I'll check in tomorrow." No apology paragraph. No "I feel like I'm letting everyone down." The coach can feel whatever they feel. Your job is the information.

Minute six: read it once out loud, quietly. If it sounds like you're asking permission to have a body, cut the question marks. You are informing.

Minutes seven and eight: send it to the coach, not the group thread. Group threads turn rest into a vote.

Minutes nine and ten: put the phone face down. Start the car or walk away from the field. Don't hover near the bench "just in case." That's how last-year-you ended up in drills.

If your coach is the type who replies "walk it off," you still don't owe a second essay in the next ten minutes. You already said no to today.

### User
What if they call while I'm still in the lot. I freeze on the phone. Also I feel like I should offer to come watch, which is how I always end up in a scrimmage.

### Assistant
If they call in these ten minutes, let it ring once while you look at the four lines. Then pick up and read the same four lines, slower. You are not required to improvise. "I need a rest day. Ankle is hot. I shouldn't play. I'll check in tomorrow." Then stop. Silence on a phone feels rude to last-year-you. It isn't. It's the end of the message.

Do not offer to come watch as a peace offering. Watching turns into "just do the warm-up," which is the thing the ankle was trying to skip. If they ask you to show up anyway: "I can come if I won't be pulled in. If that's not realistic, I should go home." Don't put yourself on the grass where the extra yes lives. I'm not inventing league rules. Consequences can wait until tomorrow. Today is the rest day.

Don't add a story about a wedding or a made-up class. Last-year-you used extra plot so the no would sound nicer. The no is already enough.

### User
I wrote the four lines. I haven't sent them. My ten minutes is turning into sitting here adding sorry.

### Assistant
Delete the sorries. Count them. If there are three, that's three votes against your own sentence. Send the short version now. Literally now, while we're in this conversation. After it says delivered, do not add a sticker or a second text that undermines it.

Then leave the lot. Fill a water bottle at home. Name where you'll be at kickoff so you're not hovering near the field in your head. A walk that isn't a workout is fine. If the ankle is more than hot — swollen, you can't bear weight, it locks — that's a clinic question, and I still won't diagnose it.

Compressed: notes, four lines, no question marks, coach only, send, phone down, leave the parking lot.

### User
Sent. No sorry. I'm driving out. I feel like I skipped class.

### Assistant
You skipped a game you had a reason to skip. That's not a personality collapse. Don't spend the evening writing a make-up novel. Tomorrow you check in with one sentence about how the ankle feels, still without a diagnosis. For the next hour, be a person who used the ten minutes. Last-year-you would have been on the field already. This-year-you left. Let that be the whole story until tomorrow.
