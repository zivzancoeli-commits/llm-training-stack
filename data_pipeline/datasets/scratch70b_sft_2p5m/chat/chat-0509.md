### User
I complained about a bar-level bass from the unit next door after 11. The landlord said the previous tenant in my apartment "loved the noise" and used to go over and hang out. That is not a policy. That is a ghost with good taste, apparently. I work mornings. I don't want to hang out. I want sleep. They made me feel like I'm the defective tenant for needing a wall. What do I send that isn't a fake housing-law memo?

### Assistant
You don't need the previous tenant's personality. They're not on the lease. You are.

Email, short, same thread if there was one: "I need quieter nights after 11 on weeknights. I work mornings. I'm asking you to talk to the neighboring unit about volume, or tell me what you will do. The previous tenant's habits aren't a plan I can use." You don't argue whether anyone loved bass. You don't accept a social history as a repair.

I will not quote a noise ordinance, a decibel number, or a habitability deadline. Those depend on where you live. If you want the real rule, use your city's published noise or tenant pages, or a local clinic. I am not those pages.

Keep a log: dates, times, how it sounds in your bedroom, whether you asked the neighbor directly. Logs are dull. Dull is useful later if you need a third party. Don't turn the log into a novel.

Have you spoken to the noisy unit yourself, or only to the landlord?

### User
I knocked once. They said they'd "try" and then the bass came back on Friday. The landlord manages several buildings and likes being buddy-buddy. I think "loved the noise" is code for they don't want to police a tenant who pays on time.

### Assistant
Then you keep your writing about the bass, not about the landlord's social life. You can notice the buddy pattern without putting it in the email. Emails that psychoanalyze landlords get treated as personality. Emails that list Friday 12:40 a.m., bass through the bedroom wall, work at 7, get treated as a request.

Second knock is allowed if you felt safe the first time. Same one sentence: "It's coming through again after 11. Please keep it down on weeknights." If you didn't feel safe, don't knock. Use the landlord and the log.

If the landlord repeats the ghost tenant, you can reply once: "I'm not that tenant. Please address the noise with the other unit." Then you stop debating ghosts.

I will not tell you to withhold rent or break a lease. I will not predict a court. If nothing changes, your next step is whatever local path actually exists — a noise complaint number, an inspector, mediation — and you follow their instructions, not a script from me that pretends to be the law.

Meanwhile: earplugs, a fan, rearranging the bed if that helps even a little. That's coping, not conceding that bass is a feature you ordered.

Don't start a campaign in the building group chat unless you like extra enemies. One-to-one with other people who already complained is different from a pile-on.

### User
Should I record video of the wall throbbing for the landlord?

### Assistant
A short clip with a timestamp can help a skeptical manager more than adjectives. Don't film people through their windows. Film your wall, your clock, the sound as it exists in your space. Don't post it publicly. Send it in the email thread if you send it at all.

You are not defective for needing a wall that behaves like a wall. Previous tenants can love whatever they loved. You need mornings. Ask for that in writing, log the Fridays, skip the legal cosplay. Let the ghost stay out of your bedroom. If the landlord sends another story about hanging out next door, you don't have to audition as the fun tenant. Fun is optional. Sleep is the request. Repeat the request. Leave the ghost in the past tense where they belong.
