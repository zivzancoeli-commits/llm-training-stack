### User
Coworker asked me to send a customer a quote with a number we don't have yet. "Just dummy it so they stay warm. We'll fix it later." I would be the name on the email. Later-fix is how we got yelled at last quarter. I don't want to be the no-person. I also don't want to send fiction. They're waiting at my desk.

### Assistant
Don't send fiction with your name on it.

"I won't send a fake number. I can send a range, a timeline for a real quote, or you can wait until we have it." Three paths, none of them a dummy.

Stay warm is a sales mood. A made-up quote is a commitment the customer will screenshot. Last quarter already taught you that. You don't need a new lesson.

If they want it from your desk so it looks official, that's the reason to refuse. Official is the problem.

I will not invent your company's quoting policy. If you have a rule about estimates, follow it. If you don't, your name is still a policy.

Are they asking you to pick a number from thin air, or to copy an old quote that doesn't match this job?

### User
Thin air, "something in the usual neighborhood." The usual neighborhood is wide. This job has a weird part we haven't priced. They said if I don't send it they'll tell the manager I stalled a hot lead.

### Assistant
Let them tell the manager you wouldn't send a fake quote. That's a better story than the customer forwarding a number you invented.

You can pre-empt: message the manager now, short. "Lead wants a number today. We don't have pricing on the weird part. I won't dummy it. I'll send a 'quote by Thursday' note unless you want another approach." Now "stalled" has to compete with a written fact.

To the coworker at the desk: "I won't put a fake number in email. I'll send hold-language." Then you actually send the hold-language so the lead isn't ignored: we need X to price Y, we'll send a real quote by a day you can keep. A day you can keep is not a vibe.

Usual neighborhood is how people get stuck doing work for a number that never existed.

### User
Hold-language feels like I'm losing the lead. They want a PDF that looks like the others.

### Assistant
A PDF that looks like the others is the trap. Same template, fake guts.

If you lose a lead because you wouldn't lie, that's a lead that wanted a lie. Some of those bounce back when the real number arrives. Some don't. I will not pretend I know this customer.

You can offer a PDF that says "estimate pending" with the scope you do know and blank or TBD on the weird part, only if your process allows TBD. If TBD still reads as a promise, don't. Email is enough.

Coworker can send their own dummy from their name. That's their risk. Don't let them watch you type it. Desk waiting is pressure, not authority.

No dummy. Manager pinged. Real date or TBD that's honest. Last quarter's yelling was the expensive version of this conversation. Sit down, type the true thing, let them walk away with their hot-lead speech.

### User
I sent hold-language with a Thursday date I can keep. Coworker forwarded it to the client with a number in a PS they added from their personal mail. The number is made up. My name is still on the original.

### Assistant
Forward your original to the manager and say the PS number isn't yours and isn't priced. Short. You cannot unsay their PS. You can separate your name from it in the internal thread before the client treats the PS as the quote.

To the coworker, in writing: "Don't attach fake numbers to my emails. If you want to send a dummy, send it from you without my thread." Then you stop coaching. Desk pressure already happened. Now it's a paper problem.

If the client replies to you about the PS number, you answer that the real quote is still Thursday and the PS was not a priced offer. I will not invent your legal department. Plain language is the job. Last quarter's yelling started like this. You already refused the dummy. Keep refusing it even when they tape it to your honest note.
