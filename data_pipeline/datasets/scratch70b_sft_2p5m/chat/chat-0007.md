### User
I borrowed my roommate's car to grab groceries. I parked in a spot that looked like street parking. There's a ticket on the windshield now. Street cleaning? Permit? I honestly cannot tell from the code on the slip. They're going to be home in an hour. I don't want to lie and I also don't want to pretend I know the law. How do I say this without turning it into a courtroom?

### Assistant
You start with the fact you do know: you drove, you parked, there is a ticket, you have not paid it yet. You do not start with a theory.

I will not tell you what the code "really means" or who is legally on the hook. Ticket liability depends on the city, the car's registration, and what the slip actually says. If you do not know, say you do not know. That is sharper than a fake expert voice.

Bring the physical ticket to the conversation. Put it on the table. "I used the car for groceries. This was on it when I got back. I can read the time and the location. I cannot honestly tell if this is street cleaning or a permit zone. I didn't want to guess." Then you ask what they want to do about paying and about the car.

If they get loud, stay on the sequence: you borrowed, you parked, you found this, you are telling them in the first hour. Volume is not evidence.

Did you take a photo of the curb signs, or only of the ticket?

### User
Only the ticket. I was embarrassed and I just drove home. The sign was one of those metal ones with three overlapping stickers. I thought the bottom sticker was expired. I'm realizing that was a story I told myself.

### Assistant
Say that part too. "I saw a messy sign and I told myself the restriction was dead. That was me hoping, not me reading." A sharp fourteen-year-old can smell a cover story. Honesty about the hope is more useful than a lecture on municipal codes you do not have.

Do not promise that you will "definitely win an appeal." Appeals exist in some places and not others, and I am not going to invent a win rate. You can offer to look up the citation number on the city's payment site together, tonight, with the ticket in hand. Together is the point. Solo research at 1 a.m. so you can present a brief is how this becomes a debate team.

Money: do not invent a price. The slip should list a fine or a website. If the amount is on the paper, read it aloud. If it is not, say "I don't know the amount until we look it up." Guessing high or low to soften the blow will backfire when the screen loads.

If they want you to pay it because you were driving, that may be fair as a house rule even if the legal owner of the ticket is a different question. You can agree to pay what the site shows without claiming you understand the statute. If they want to split, fine. If they want to fight the city, they can, and you can help with the photo you failed to take by writing down the block name while it is still in your head.

What you should not do is hide the slip in a drawer until "a good moment." The good moment is before they find it themselves.

If you still have the grocery receipt, it only proves you bought milk. It does not prove the sign was confusing. Leave the receipt out of the opening speech.

One hour is enough time to put the ticket on the counter, charge your phone, and write three sentences so you do not ramble: I borrowed the car. There is a ticket. I do not know the exact rule I broke. Then you listen. Listening is the part that keeps it from becoming a courtroom. Judges talk first. Roommates who want to stay roommates do not.
