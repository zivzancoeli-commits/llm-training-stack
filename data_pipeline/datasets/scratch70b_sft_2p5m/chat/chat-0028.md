### User
Landlord texted "quick look at the kitchen sink, ten minutes." That was forty minutes ago. He's now opening kitchen cabinets and asking when I last ran the bathroom fan. I am standing here like a hostage with a polite smile and I hate it. How do I end this without starting a war.

### Assistant
Drop the polite smile. You don't need a war, and you also don't need to play host. Ten minutes was the agreement. Forty minutes is him shopping for extra chores.

Say the time out loud, not as a joke. "You said ten minutes. We're past that. I need you to wrap up the sink." Short. Flat. You are ending the extra tour, not thanking him for his time.

Don't narrate your feelings. "I feel uncomfortable" invites him to reassure you and keep looking. "We're done after the sink" is a closer. If he starts on the fan: "That's not why you came. If you want a longer visit, text me a time." Scheduling is the adult version of no. Fake cheer keeps the cabinets opening.

Is he actually fixing the sink, or just inspecting?

### User
He looked at it, said the trap is probably fine, then started the cabinet tour. There's no tool bag. This is nosiness with a work order costume.

### Assistant
Then you don't owe him a workshop. He's off-script. You can be civil and still usher him out.

Walk toward the door while you talk. People linger when you plant yourself in the kitchen. "Okay. Sink's fine, you said. I'll walk you out." If he keeps opening things, repeat his reason: "You came for the sink. That's done." Repeating his words is a receipt, not an argument.

If he says he's "just making sure," answer: "If you want an inspection, put it in writing with a time." I'm not going to quote your local notice rules like I'm a lawyer. Those depend on where you live and what your lease says. What you can do today is stop volunteering extra access.

Don't apologize for having a life. "Sorry to rush you" hands him the power again. He overstayed a ten-minute visit.

### User
If I get blunt he's going to remember it at renewal. That's the trap. I also don't want him deciding the apartment is "messy" because my dishes are in the rack.

### Assistant
Renewal anxiety is real. So is training him that "ten minutes" means an hour. You can be direct without scoring points.

Tone: bored, not sarcastic. Sarcasm reads as a fight. Bored reads as a person with a clock. "I wasn't expecting a full walkthrough. Let's stop here." If he comments on dishes, don't defend the dishes. "Those aren't the sink. We're good." You already let him in. That's the courtesy.

If he keeps going, pick up your keys and stand by the door. Silence plus a door is clearer than a paragraph. One more line if needed: "I need you to leave now. Text me if the sink still needs a plumber." Then wait.

If he refuses to leave, that's not awkwardness anymore. Tell him to go, and if he doesn't, get someone else involved — a roommate, a neighbor, or, if you feel unsafe, treat it as someone who will not exit your home. I'm not going to pretend I know your city hotline. Overstaying after you've asked him to go is not "just being thorough."

For today, assume he'll leave if you stop entertaining the extra tour. Take the minutes back.

### User
Okay. I'm going to say the ten-minute line. I can feel myself about to add "whenever you want to come back just let me know!" which is how I got here.

### Assistant
Don't add that. That's an open invitation with no end time. If you want a future visit, make it specific later, in a text, for a stated job. Not as a parting gift while he's still in your cabinets.

Say the closer. Walk him out. After the door shuts, text a one-liner so there's a record: "Sink visit finished. Please schedule if you need more than a quick look next time." No smiley. No "thanks so much for stopping by!!" He had a reason to be there. He used it up. You don't have to gild it.

You're allowed to want him out. That's not a personality flaw. That's a person who said ten minutes and meant it.
