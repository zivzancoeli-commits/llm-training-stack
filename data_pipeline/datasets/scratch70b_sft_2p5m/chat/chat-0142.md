### User
My cousin is at the rec center parking lot convinced the keys are in the Civic. They're spiraling. They want me to leave work and they want me to say it's fine to pop a window with a rock. I don't want to do either. I also don't want a fake meeting as an excuse. Talk to me like I can handle a clean no.

### Assistant
You can calm someone and still say no. Those aren't opposites. The costume excuse is the part you don't need.

First, slow the facts, because "keys are inside" is often a guess at full volume. Pockets, the other jacket, whether a door is actually locked, whether the spare is at home. People skip this because panic feels like action. Checking is action.

Then the nos, plain. "I can't leave work." "I'm not telling you to break a window." You don't add a fake dentist. You don't pile on "I'd love to, but." Extra clauses are what people negotiate.

Offer what you will do from where you are: stay on the phone, talk through roadside help if they have it, ask if the rec desk is still open so they can wait inside. A locksmith exists as a paid option. I won't invent the cost. Night and weekend rates are often ugly. That's the most I'll say.

### User
They checked pockets. Doors are locked. There's a gym bag on the passenger seat and they think the keys slid under it. They're crying. They said if I actually cared I'd already be in the car. That's the sentence that makes me want to cave or invent a flat tire.

### Assistant
That's a guilt hook, not a lock. You can answer the feeling without taking the job. "I do care. I'm staying on the phone. I'm still not leaving work, and I'm still not okay with smashing the glass." One breath. No speech about boundaries. The word "boundaries" will make this worse.

Crying is allowed. You don't have to fix crying with a commute. Keep your voice lower than theirs. Next physical step: "Walk into the rec desk. Tell them you locked yourself out. Ask if they have a number for local help or if you can wait inside." Fluorescent lobby beats a parking-lot spiral, and it puts another adult in the picture so you are not the entire emergency service.

If they have a roadside app through insurance, that's often the actual path. If they don't, they can search locksmith themselves. You can sit with them while they do it. You cannot promise cheap, fast, or kind. Don't bluff a number.

If they keep pushing the window: "A broken window is a second problem. I won't help you do that from here, and I won't pretend it's clever." That's a no without a costume.

Skeptical version, because you asked for it: most "if you cared" lines are panic hunting for a chauffeur. You are allowed to care and still keep your shift.

### User
They asked if they can come to my job and wait in the lobby until I'm off. That's also a no. I feel like I'm refusing everything.

### Assistant
You are refusing the things that dump the whole afternoon onto you. You can still be useful. "You can wait at the rec desk. You can call someone who is already nearby. You cannot park the problem in my work lobby." If they need a destination that isn't your building, help them name one they already have: another cousin, a parent, a cafe that's open. You're not required to become the destination.

If the guilt line comes back, repeat the same two facts. Repetition is calmer than a new essay. People in panic hunt for a new angle. You don't have to provide a new angle.

Friendship is not a coupon for a forty-minute rescue. Showing up on the phone is showing up. A fake meeting would train both of you to expect a lie. Don't do it.

### User
Stay on the phone. No leaving work. No rock. No lobby dump. Send them inside. Don't invent a cost. Don't invent an excuse. I can do that even if they stay mad until dinner.

### Assistant
Yes. Mad until dinner is cheaper than a smashed window and a family story built on fake emergencies. Check in later when the keys are out. Right now, keep the nos clean. You don't owe a reason that isn't true. "No" is already a complete sentence. The rest is just you staying human on the line.
