### User
Friends want me on a weekend trip. The group chat is using words like "low-key getaway" and "just a chill house." I don't want to go. I also don't want to invent a family emergency. Last time I said "I might be busy" they kept the plan half-open around me and it was worse. How do I decline in plain language without sounding like I hate them?

### Assistant
"Low-key getaway" usually still means travel, money, a shared house, and a weekend you don't get back. If you don't want that, you decline the trip, not the friendship. Those are different sentences.

Don't hide behind "might be busy." That's the jargon version of no, and they heard it as maybe. Plain speech is: "I'm not going to make this trip." Then one true reason you're willing to share. True can be "I need a quiet weekend" or "I can't spend that money" or "group houses aren't my thing." It does not have to be a dead relative.

I won't invent what the house costs. If money is the reason, you can say money without a spreadsheet.

Have they already booked something with your name on it, or is this still the idea stage?

### User
Still the idea stage. Someone sent a link to a house. No deposits. The chat is moving fast though. One person said "RSVP by tonight so we can lock it." I like these people. I just know I'll be the tired one in the corner while they stay up.

### Assistant
Then tonight's RSVP is actually helpful. You can close the loop while it's still cheap for everyone.

In the group, keep it short: "I'm out for this weekend. Have a good time. Don't hold a bed for me." That's the translation of all the soft language. "I'm out" means no. "Don't hold a bed" means they can lock the house without you. "Have a good time" is the part that says you don't hate them.

If you want one reason, add it in the same message so they don't hunt you in DMs for the real story. Pick the true one. "I need a home weekend" is complete. "I don't want a group house" is complete. Don't stack three reasons. Stacking sounds like you're trying to win a case.

If someone replies "come for Saturday only" or "just the first night," that's a new ask. You can decline that too: "Still a no, but thanks for trying to make it work." You don't have to attend a slice of a trip you already refused.

Avoid "I wish I could." If you don't wish you could, don't say it. Wishy sentences are how "no" turns back into "maybe" in a group chat.

### User
There's one friend who will take it personally. They always do. Also I don't want them to stop inviting me to everything.

### Assistant
Taking it personally is their habit, not a verdict on your weekend. You can send them a separate message that's still true: "I'm skipping this trip. I still want to hang around town another time." That's the plain version of "this isn't a breakup." You are not required to soothe them for ten texts. One extra line is plenty.

On future invites: people who want you at dinners will keep asking if you show up to the things you actually say yes to. A clean no on a trip is better for that than a miserable yes. If they stop inviting you to everything because you skipped one house, that's information about the group, and you still don't have to lie to keep a slot.

If they use more jargon — "it would mean a lot," "we're counting on the numbers" — translate it in your head. Meaning a lot is their feeling. Counting on numbers is the house math. Neither one books your body into a bunk. You can acknowledge the feeling ("I know you wanted a full house") and still stay home.

Send the group no tonight. Send the one-friend line if you want. Then mute the planning thread so you don't get worn down by photos of the porch. You almost had this already: you knew you didn't want to go. The missing piece was a sentence without a fake emergency. Use the real one. Stay home on purpose.
