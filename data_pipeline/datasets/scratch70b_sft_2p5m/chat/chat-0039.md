### User
My brother wants the car Saturday. That's the third weekend in a row. I said maybe last time and then I sat at home. Gas is already a problem. I don't hate him. I just can't keep treating the tank like a shared myth. I almost have a rule in my head and I keep not saying it. Help me make a plan that doesn't require money I don't have.

### Assistant
You almost have it, so let's not dress this up as a family therapy session. The car is a budget object. Borrowing it is not free even when nobody hands over cash, because fuel, wear, and your Saturday are all real.

Before you write the rule, I need one practical fact: is this *your* car, a parent car you're the usual driver of, or a car you both chip into? The plan changes if you are the only person who fills it versus if the household treats it as a pot. Also: Saturday for what — a shift, a date, a ride for someone else? You don't have to justify every trip, but the plan should match the kind of use.

A tight-budget plan is usually three pieces, not a speech: when he can take it, how the tank comes back, and what happens if he blows it. Keep numbers out of fantasy. Don't invent a "fair" hourly rate. Talk in fuel and time.

### User
It's in my name. I pay insurance. He chips in sometimes when he remembers, which is not a system. Saturday is a shift at the warehouse, early, and he'd be back by mid-afternoon. I was going to grocery shop in the morning because the bus plus bags is miserable, but I could shop Sunday if I have to. I'm trying not to be the villain.

### Assistant
You're not the villain for noticing the tank. He's not a villain for needing a ride to work either. The mess is the missing system.

Here's a plan that fits a tight week. He can take it Saturday for the shift. You shop Sunday. In exchange, he returns it with at least the same fuel you handed him, measured the cheap way: he texts you a photo of the gauge before he leaves and after he gets back. If the needle dropped, he puts fuel in before he hands you the keys, even if it's a small amount. You do not need a fancy app. You need a photo and a habit.

If he truly has no cash this week, don't pretend fuel appears. Then he takes the bus or carpools, or he swaps one chore that actually saves you money — grocery run with you on Sunday, not "I'll get you back." Pick one swap. Not five vague promises.

Say it like someone who almost had the sentence: "Warehouse shift yes. Photo of the gauge both ways. Same fuel when it comes back. If you can't do fuel this week, we need a different ride. I can't float another empty tank."

### User
What if he says he'll fill it and then he doesn't, and I'm standing there Sunday with a near-empty car and a grocery list?

### Assistant
Then you shrink access, not invent a punishment. Next time: "Last time the gauge came back lower and I covered it. I can't do that this month. Fuel happens before the keys, or you don't take it." Up front is the fix for the Sunday surprise.

If he feels treated like a kid, stay flat: "I'm treating the car like a bill. I'm not doing the maybe." You need a tank that gets you to the store. A scheduled ride swap later in the week is a plan. "I owe you" is not.

### User
Okay. Saturday shift yes, photos, same fuel, or he doesn't take it. I shop Sunday. If he flakes, next time fuel happens before the keys. I can say that without a fight voice.

### Assistant
Say it once, in the kitchen, not in a paragraph text you'll regret. If he agrees, stick to it even when it's slightly inconvenient. The plan only works if you don't undo it with another "maybe." You already knew the rule. You just needed to hear it out loud so it counts as a decision instead of a mood.
