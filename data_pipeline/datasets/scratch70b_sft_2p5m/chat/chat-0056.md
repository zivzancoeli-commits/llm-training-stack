### User
New job, week two. There is a fancy espresso machine and an unwritten list of who is allowed to touch it. I used it once, made a perfectly fine coffee, and Dana from accounting gave me a look like I had keyed a car. Then Raj whispered that the machine is Ken's baby and Ken gets in at seven. I just want caffeine. I am on break. I am too tired for a feudal system around beans. What do I actually do tomorrow morning.

### Assistant
Two options, then I will pick one.

Option A: do not touch the espresso machine for a while. Use the drip pot or the kettle. You get caffeine. You stay off Ken's radar. Cost: worse coffee, and you are obeying a look from Dana, which is annoying.

Option B: use the machine, but learn the local rules in ten minutes so you are not the person who left the portafilter dirty. Ask Raj, not Dana. Ask what Ken actually cares about. Usually it is milk skin in the steam wand, a puck left in, or an empty hopper. Wipe what people can see. Looks are not write-ups.

Recommendation, tired-coworker version: Option A until Friday, then Option B once with Raj standing there so you have a witness. Week two is early to die on espresso hill. Caffeine is the goal. Status is not.

Do not send a Slack about coffee equity. That turns beans into a values debate. Do not apologize to Dana. You did not key a car. A look is not a policy. If Ken wants a policy, he can put a sticky note on the machine. Until then it is a pet, not a law.

If you need the good stuff for a morning meeting, buy a cheap drink on the way in twice this week. I will not quote cafe prices. You know what you can spend. If you cannot spend, drip pot. Pride is expensive.

What time do you get in, and is Ken already there. That changes whether Option B is a greeting or a crime scene.

### User
I get in at 8:30. Ken is there at seven and gone to a standing meeting by 8:15, so I never meet him, I only meet the look. Dana is the look. Raj is useful but he talks like everything is a secret.

### Assistant
Then Dana is the politics, not Ken. Ken may be a story people tell so they do not have to say Dana likes control of the kitchen. Possible. Not proven. Do not confront her with that theory. Week two is early to write a novel.

Still: drip until Friday. Tomorrow, greet Dana like a person, not a defendant. If she comments, say I used it yesterday, I wiped it, happy to follow the house rule. If she names a rule, follow it. If she only does the look, you have learned the look is the product. You cannot comply with a face.

Ask Raj one concrete question: what do I clean so nobody gets mad. If he stays secretive, say I need two steps. Secret-talkers can handle a list of two.

Friday, use the machine once, clean the wand, knock the puck, wipe the tray. Do not photograph coworkers. If Dana looks: I cleaned it. Then walk. Negotiating foam is how break ends and resentment starts.

If Friday still feels hot, skip the machine for a month. That is spending political capital on work, not foam. Revisit when you have a name besides the new person who touched Ken's baby.

Get your caffeine. Keep energy for actual work. If Ken ever appears at 8:30, ask him directly. Direct beats folklore. Until a human with a calendar talks to you, folklore is just Dana's look with extra characters.

### User
Fine. Drip tomorrow. If Dana says you can use it if you do it right, do I let her teach me or is that a trap.

### Assistant
Let her teach you once, five minutes, then thank her and stop. Teaching is how some people put the look down. A trap is her hovering every morning. If she hovers, go back to drip and do not narrate why. You need coffee and a seat, not a closing argument. Five minutes is cheap. A weekly seminar is not. Take the lesson. Do the two steps. Leave the feudalism in the kitchen.
