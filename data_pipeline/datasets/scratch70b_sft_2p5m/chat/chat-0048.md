### User
The dress rehearsal is tonight. The costume — a cardboard armor thing with hot glue — split down the side in the car. My kid is trying not to cry and I'm trying not to do the "this will be so funny someday" voice because I remember hating that voice. I don't have a sewing machine in the back seat. I need to talk them through the next two hours without fake sunshine.

### Assistant
Skip the silver lining. The armor split. That's a materials problem, not a character test. You can be calm without being cheerful.

First, name what's true: "The glue failed. We can still get you on stage. It just won't look like the photo." If they cry, let them cry for a minute in the parking lot. Crying is not the costume getting worse. Talking over it with jokes is.

Then look at the actual tear. Packing tape on the inside beats a glitter patch unless the character is already chaotic. A popped strap can live on a safety pin for one night. I am not a costume designer. School-play armor is cardboard and hope. Hope failed. Tape is what you have.

Ask what they need: "Do you want me to fix it while you breathe, or help tape so it feels like yours?" Don't guess in a peppy way.

### User
They want it to look "real." The split is obvious. There's silver paint flaking. They said they can't go on. The teacher texted "come anyway." I almost snapped "we'll figure it out!" which is the fake-cheerful cousin of a plan.

### Assistant
"We'll figure it out" is a fog machine. Replace it with one visible step. "I'm taping the inside. You'll still see a line. The audience is far. The teacher still wants you there. You don't have to like it." That's de-escalation without a smile campaign.

"Real" is a trap at this hour. Nothing about hot-glue cardboard was real. What they mean is "I don't want to be the kid whose costume died." You can agree: "Yeah. This sucks. You won't be invisible. You'll also not be the only crooked armor in a school play, even if it feels like it." Don't point at other kids as a gotcha. Just don't lie that the tear is a charming choice.

If they refuse to go in, don't drag. Give a boundary that's still kind: "I'll walk in with you and talk to the teacher. If the armor can't be worn, we ask for a plain shirt and the same lines. The play is the lines, not the glue." I don't know their teacher's style. Don't promise a standing ovation. Promise an adult conversation in the hallway so the kid isn't the one announcing the disaster to a gym.

Safety: if the costume now has sharp cardboard edges, tape those too. A costume that cuts is worse than a costume that's ugly. If they can't move their arms, it's not wearable. Function over "real."

### User
They asked if people will laugh. I wanted to say no. I also remember they *did* laugh when someone's hat fell off last year, including my kid. I don't want to lie.

### Assistant
Don't lie. "Some people might laugh because it's a gym and kids are wired. If they do, it's about the prop, and it will be thirty seconds. I'll be in the seats. You can look at the back wall." That's not fake-cheerful. It's a forecast with a place to put their eyes.

If laughter happens, they don't have to pretend it's fine. They can finish the scene anyway. That's the skill, not "loving the spotlight." You can say that without a poster about resilience.

Tonight's script for you, in the car, tired-self version: no someday, no "adventure," tape the inside, walk in together, ask the teacher for a backup shirt, keep the edges safe, tell the truth about laughing. After the show, skip the recap that turns this into a lesson. Offer food. If they want to throw the armor out, let them. You don't have to preserve a cardboard failure as a souvenir of growth.

### User
Tape inside, don't call it real, hallway with the teacher, truth about laughing, food after, no lesson speech. I can do that. I already feel less like I have to host a mood.

### Assistant
You're not the host. You're the person with tape. That's enough. The play will be imperfect, which it was going to be even with intact glue. Get them through the door without selling them a feeling they don't have.
