### User
Priya from two desks over asked if I wanted to be "accountability partners" for a diet. She wants daily Slack weigh-ins, photos of lunch, and a shared spreadsheet. I said I was "trying to eat better," once, in the kitchen, about a leftover. I am not on a diet. I do not want to send her my lunch. I also sit eight feet from her. If I say no, I will still hear about her macros. If I say yes, I will resent her by Wednesday. How do I decline this without becoming the office project?

### Assistant
Decline the partnership. Keep it about the format, not about her body or yours.

"I'm not doing weigh-ins or food photos. I'll skip the spreadsheet. Good luck with your plan" is a complete answer. You do not owe a counter-offer. A counter-offer is how you end up walking at lunch every day as a consolation prize you also do not want.

If you want one soft landing, you can add, "I was talking about leftovers, not a program." Then stop. Extra autobiography is bait.

I will not give you diet advice, targets, or a medical take. This is a workplace boundary around Slack and a shared sheet.

She may keep talking about macros in the kitchen. You can listen, you can say "mm," you can change the subject to the printer. You do not have to join a ledger to be polite.

Did she already make the spreadsheet and add you, or was this only a verbal ask?

### User
Verbal, in the kitchen, this morning. She said she'd "set it up tonight" and that it would be fun. Two other people heard. I kind of nodded. I think she heard the nod as a yes.

### Assistant
A nod is not a contract, but she will treat it like one if you go quiet until the invite arrives.

Message her before tonight. Slack, not a kitchen sequel with an audience. "I should be clear — I'm not joining an accountability sheet or lunch photos. Hope the plan works for you." Short. No joke about "I'm too weak." Jokes invite coaching.

If the spreadsheet invite still lands, decline the share or leave the doc. Do not enter a starter weight "just to see." Data is how this becomes a daily ping.

If she looks disappointed at the desk, you can survive disappointment. You do not have to fill the silence with a mini-plan. "Not for me" is allowed at work.

The two people who heard do not need a press release. If someone teases you into joining, repeat the same line. Consistency is what makes it boring, and boring is what makes it stop.

### User
She just sent a message: "What's your starting number so I can make the graph?" I haven't opened it on the big monitor. I don't want the row visible behind me.

### Assistant
Reply from your phone, not on the monitor. Do not type a number.

"I'm not doing the graph — I'm not in. Thanks for asking." Then mute that thread if you can. If she follows up with "just try for a week," you can repeat once: "Not in." Second time you stop explaining.

You can still eat your leftover in the kitchen. You can still say the food is good. You did not volunteer to be a datapoint. Accountability partners are optional even when they are cheerful. Be cheerful about something else. The spreadsheet can exist without your row. Let it.

### User
If she prints the sheet and leaves it on the kitchen table with a blank line for me, do I write "n/a" or just leave it?

### Assistant
Leave it blank. "N/a" is still a participation. A blank line is a line that is not yours.

If she asks at the table, same sentence as Slack: "I'm not in." Then talk about the printer jam or the weather. You do not need a speech about body talk at work. You already declined the format.

If the paper sits there all week, you can throw your own leftover container away and still not pick up a pen. Paper is not a trap unless you write in it. Don't. Eat your lunch. Walk back to the eight feet of space that is your desk. The graph can miss a row forever. That is a successful decline, not a missing homework. You were talking about leftovers. Keep it that small.
