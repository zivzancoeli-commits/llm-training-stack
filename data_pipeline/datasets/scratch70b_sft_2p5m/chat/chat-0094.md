### User
A friend texted me twenty minutes ago: "Can you spot me $400 until Friday? I'll explain later." No context. We are close enough that this isn't a stranger scam, and not close enough that I pay their rent without a conversation. I have $400. I also have a habit of saying yes and then being angry. I don't want a TED talk about boundaries. I want to know what you would actually do in the next ten minutes, like a person who has been burned and is not impressed.

### Assistant
I would not send $400 in the next ten minutes. I would not ignore the text for three days either. Here is the clock.

Minute one: I read the message twice and I don't tap a payment app. "Later" as the explanation is a smell. I don't need a novel. I need a sentence about what the money is for and when Friday actually means, because Friday-the-deposit and Friday-I-meant-next-week are different animals.

Minutes two to four: I write a reply that asks for those two things and puts my own answer on hold. Something like: "I need to know what it's for and when you would pay it back, including a date, not 'soon.' I'm not sending anything until I have that." No apology for asking. No joke. No "love you." A skeptical person can live without the frosting.

Minutes five and six: I look at my own numbers, privately. Not a vibe. The actual checking balance after rent, food, gas, and whatever I already promised this week. If $400 makes me short, the answer is no, even if the story is sad. I will not pretend I know your bills. You said you have $400. Having it is not the same as it being spare.

Minutes seven to nine: I decide my rule before they reply, so their explanation doesn't hypnotize me. My rule would be one of these, chosen in advance: no cash gifts this month; a smaller amount I can lose without a friendship autopsy; or a yes only if the payback date is on a calendar and I can survive being wrong. I pick the rule while I still have a spine.

Minute ten: I put the phone face down and I do not draft a second text that softens the first. If they call, I let it ring until I can say the same words with my mouth. Voice is how people get talked into "just this once" before the facts land.

That is ten minutes. No transfer. A question. A private look at the bank. A rule. Silence.

### User
They just replied: "It's for a car repair. I need the car for work. Friday I get paid." Still no shop name, no amount breakdown. They added "I wouldn't ask if it wasn't serious."

### Assistant
I still would not tap send.

"Serious" is not documentation. Car repair can be true. It can also be a shape people use because it is hard to argue with. In the next few minutes I would answer the new information without pretending it is a receipt.

I would text: "I need the total from the shop and a Friday date. If I lend anything, it has to be an amount I can stand not seeing again." Then I wait. If they cannot produce a number, I do not fill it in with $400 because that was the first ask. The first ask is a ceiling they chose, not a bill I verified.

If I were going to say no, I would say no in the same window, not after a long sympathetic paragraph: "I can't lend $400. I hope the car gets sorted." I would not invent a fake reason about my own landlord unless that reason is true. "I can't" is allowed. If I were going to offer less, I would name the less and the date, and I would assume it is a gift in my own head so Friday's disappointment doesn't turn into a stalking project.

I would not offer to call the mechanic. I would not drive over with cash in an envelope to feel like a movie. I would not ask our mutual friends if this person is "good for it." Gossip is not due diligence.

Ten minutes from this new text: reply with the ask for the shop total and the date, or reply with the no. Don't sit in "maybe" until they send a sad photo of a tire. Maybe is how you wake up having paid.

You already know you get angry after yes. Treat that as data. The next ten minutes are for a decision you can defend to yourself tomorrow, not for a story that would impress a nicer uncle than I am.
