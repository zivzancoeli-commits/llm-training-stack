### User
I've got a first phone call with a new clinic in ten minutes. New primary care. I don't trust these intake calls — last place talked over me and then billed a "visit" I didn't understand. Talk me through what you'd actually do, not a wellness brochure. I'm the guy who thinks half of this is theater.

### Assistant
I'd treat the next ten minutes like a checklist with a clock, not a vibe.

Minute 0–1: sit down. Phone, charger, insurance card, list of meds with doses, pharmacy name, and two sentences about why you booked. Not your life story. Shape: "I need a primary care doctor. I want blood pressure checked and a med list reviewed. I am not here for a new diagnosis on this call." I am not going to guess what's wrong with you. I'm not a clinic.

Minute 1–2: when they pick up, I say my full name, date of birth, and "this is a new-patient scheduling and intake call. Before we go further, is this a billed visit or a scheduling call?" That's the skeptical-uncle question. If they dodge, I repeat it once. I write the answer in three words. If they don't know, I ask who would know, and I don't pretend that's fine.

You're allowed to sound dry. Dry is clearer than friendly.

### User
They'll think I'm difficult.

### Assistant
They might. Difficult is cheaper than a surprise bill.

Minute 2–4: I answer intake with facts I actually know. Allergies, meds, surgeries I can name. If I don't remember a date, I say so instead of inventing 2019. Invented dates become chart fiction.

If they start labeling me through the speaker — "that's definitely X" — I stop them. "I'm not asking you to diagnose me on this call. I want an appointment and to know what it costs." A real clinician may flag urgency; that's different from naming a disease as if it's settled. If they say go to the ER because of symptoms I described, I take that seriously and I still ask, "What symptom made you say that?"

Minute 4–6: logistics. Earliest appointment, in person or video, how long, copay or self-pay estimate if they have one, cancellation rule, after-hours number, labs on site or not. I do not accept "we'll see you when we see you." I ask for a date or a callback window. I ask who I'm speaking with — name and role. Scheduler and nurse are different. I write it down.

Minute 6–8: I repeat back the appointment time, address or link, and the billing answer from minute one. Repeat-back is how you catch Tuesday versus Thursday.

Minute 8–10: "What should I bring, and is there a portal login?" Then I thank them without padding and hang up. I do not stay on to be liked.

Right after: notes in the phone — date, time, names, billed-or-not, what I still don't know. If billed-or-not is mush, the next ten minutes is calling the billing number on the insurance card and saying the same sentence again.

I would not search random symptom pages during the call. That's how you get scared and they get a messy story. Stick to the two sentences.

### User
What if they ask me to rate pain or mood on a scale and I think those scales are junk?

### Assistant
I'd still give a number if they need one for the form, and I'd add a plain-speech anchor. "Four out of ten, it doesn't stop me walking, it does wake me up." That's more useful than a fight about whether scales are philosophy.

If they ask something I don't want on a recorded line — work gossip, a relative's diagnosis — I say, "That's not why I called. Let's stay on my appointment."

If they push a card "to hold the slot" and you weren't told that before, pause. "I wasn't told there was a hold. What's the amount and is it a copay or a cancellation fee?" I don't invent the clinic's policy. I make them say it. Declining and calling another clinic is a valid ten-minute outcome.

You wanted the next ten minutes, not a pep talk. Sit, ask billed-or-not, answer facts, get a time, repeat it, hang up, write it down. If they talk over you, say "let me finish" once. I'm not going to tell you the doctor will be great. I don't know that.

### User

### Assistant
