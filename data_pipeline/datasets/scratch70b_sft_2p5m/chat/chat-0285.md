### User
Priya left last month after a fight with our director that everyone heard through the glass. She texted me today asking if I can be a "work reference, nothing dramatic, just dates and that I'm reliable." I sat next to her for a year. She was fine at the work. She also sent a all-caps Slack on the way out. I don't want to lie and I don't want to tank her. I also don't want our director finding out I'm the person they call.

### Assistant
You can decline without writing a review of her last day. A reference is not a participation trophy and it is not a punishment. It is you putting your name on a phone call.

If you cannot speak to her work without stepping on the exit, you should not be the reference. "Fine at the work" is something you can say. "Nothing dramatic" is her hoping you will skip the part everyone heard. You do not have to skip, and you do not have to volunteer it.

I will not tell you what your company policy is about references. Some places funnel everything through HR. If you have an employee handbook line about that, follow it. If you do not know, that is a reason to pause, not to invent a rule.

Were you her manager, or a peer? And did she already list you somewhere, or is this still a question?

### User
Peer. She hasn't listed me yet, she asked first, which I guess is decent. The job is at a smaller company. She said they might call this week. I keep drafting "of course" because I want to be kind.

### Assistant
Asking first is decent. "Of course" is still a contract. Kindness here is a true yes or a clean no, not a soft yes you resent.

If you say yes, define the box before she lists you. Text: "I can confirm we worked together for a year, your role, and that you delivered [two concrete things]. I won't talk about how you left, and I also won't pretend I was your manager. If they ask why you left, I'll say they should ask you." That is a usable reference. It is not a glowing novel.

If that box feels too small for what she wants, say no. "I'm not the right reference for this. I hope you land it." You do not owe a paragraph about the glass office. You do not need to cite the all-caps Slack. A no can be boring.

Do not secretly call the director to "get ahead of it." That makes you a character in her exit all over again. Do not warn the whole team. This is a two-person text.

If you are unsure whether you are allowed to speak, ask HR or your manager one dry question: "A former peer asked me to be a personal reference. Is that something we do here, or should I point them to HR?" Then follow the answer you get, not the one you wish you had.

### User
What if they ask "would you work with her again" and I hesitate out loud. I hesitate at everything.

### Assistant
Then do not say yes to the reference. A hesitant "would you work with her again" is louder than the dates. If you cannot answer that in one clean sentence, you are not ready to take the call.

A clean sentence, if it is true: "Yes, on the kind of work we did together." If that is not true, you already have your answer about being the reference.

For the next ten minutes: do not send "of course." Write which of the two texts you mean — the boxed yes or the boring no — and send only that. If it is the yes, add "please don't list me until I confirm." If it is the no, send it before she types your name into an application tonight.

You sat next to her for a year. That is real. The messy exit is also real. Your name does not have to carry both unless you choose it. Choose once, in writing, and then stop drafting kindness that is actually panic.
