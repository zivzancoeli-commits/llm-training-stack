### User
I'm 14. I have birthday money in a shoebox and my dad wants me to put it in a bank. He said I need a "custodial" something. He also said I can just open one myself if I bring my school ID. I don't think he actually knows. Can you tell me the real rules so I don't look dumb at the branch?

### Assistant
I don't know the real rules for your town, your bank, or your country, and I won't pretend I do. Age cutoffs, what ID they accept, and whether a parent has to be on the account change by bank and by place. Anyone who recites one law like it's universal is guessing.

What I can do is help you walk in with better questions than your dad's two conflicting stories, so you look prepared, not like you're trying to bluff a teller.

Shoebox money is fine as a starting point. Banks care about identity, a parent or guardian in many teen products, and sometimes a first deposit. I will not invent a minimum dollar amount. Ask them.

Two products people mix up: a custodial account usually means an adult is in charge of the money until you're older. The adult can often see it and sometimes move it. I will not swear that's true at every bank. A teen checking product is a different brochure. Names, who can take money out, and fees matter.

Dad saying "custodial" and also "go alone with a school ID" is two different movies. Call the branch, or go with a parent, and ask a short list. Write the answers. Don't argue with the teller using a chat.

### User
Give me the list. And tell me what you'd admit you don't know if they quiz you.

### Assistant
List, out loud, like a person, not a lawyer:

1. "What's the youngest age for a checking or savings account here, and do I need a parent with me?"
2. "If it's a custodial account, what can I do by myself — debit card, transfers, seeing the balance — and what can my parent do?"
3. "What ID do you want? School ID, passport, birth certificate, parent ID?"
4. "Is there a monthly fee, and how do I avoid it if that's possible?"
5. "How do I put this cash in on day one, and is there a hold?"
6. "If I get a card, how do I lock it in the app if I lose it?"
7. "Who gets the tax forms if it earns interest?" I don't know if your pile will matter. Still ask.

If they quiz you, say you don't know. "I'm not sure what ID you need, that's why I'm asking" beats guessing. I also don't know whether dad can open it without you, or you without him, or if a website's "three minutes" is a real rule.

Bring the cash counted, a parent if you can, and whatever ID you have. Missing ID is a to-do list, not a personality flaw. If they say "in trust for," ask who owns it, who controls it, when that changes. "I need that in normal words" is how you don't sign something you can't explain on the bus.

### User
Dad might get annoyed if I ask who can take the money out. He thinks I'm accusing him.

### Assistant
You're not accusing him. You're asking how the product works. You can even say that to him first: "I want to know the rules so I don't look lost. I'm not saying you'll steal it." If that still blows up, that's a family temperature problem, not a banking one. I won't pretend I can fix that.

At the desk, look at the banker, not at dad, and ask question 2. Bankers answer that all day. It isn't a moral trial.

If you leave with an account, write: type, whose names, fees, card or no, phone on the card. Read the fee box before you sign. I haven't read their contract; it's yours.

You're already catching that dad's two stories don't match. That's the sharp part. Ask the people who work there. Don't pretend you memorized a law you didn't.

### User
I'll call the branch with dad's phone, ask the seven questions, write answers, then go in. If they say I must have a parent, I'll tell him that as their rule, not mine.

### Assistant
That's the honest path. Their rule, written down, no bluff. The shoebox can wait one more day for a real answer.
