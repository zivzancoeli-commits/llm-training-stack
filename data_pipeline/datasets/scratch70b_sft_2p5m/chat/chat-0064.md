### User
It is Saturday, I have one working hour before I have to leave, and the washer just died in the middle of a load. Not dead-dead, maybe. It filled, it sat there humming like it was thinking, then it clicked and the drum did not move. Error lights I do not recognize. The floor is dry, at least. I am not calling a repair person on a Saturday morning for a lecture. Tell me what you would actually do in the next ten minutes, not the five-year maintenance plan.

### Assistant
I would not diagnose it from the couch and I would not start shopping for a new machine. Ten minutes. Clock up.

Minute one: I stop the cycle if I can, then I unplug the washer. Not just power off on the panel. Pull the plug. Water and motors are not a riddle I solve while the thing is still live.

Minutes two and three: I open the lid or door. If it is a top-loader with a full tub, I pull the wet laundry into a basket. Sitting in standing water smells like a gym bag by tonight. If it is a front-loader and the door will not unlock, I leave it. I do not pry. Forcing a locked door adds a broken latch.

Minute four: I look at the hose situation with my eyes, not with a wrench. Supply hoses in back: any spray, any damp patch, any kink obvious from the side? Drain hose: still in the standpipe, not kicked out onto the floor? Dry floor is good news. I am not flooding. I still am not yanking the machine out yet. Ten minutes does not include a full pull-out unless water is going somewhere it should not.

Minutes five and six: I check the stupid stuff that mimics a dead washer. Child lock. Lid switch. A sock sitting on the lid that keeps a top-loader from thinking it is closed. On some machines a cycle will fill and then refuse to spin if the lid switch is sulking. I press the lid down firmly. I look for a control lock icon. I do not hold random button combos I saw in a thumbnail.

Minute seven: I find the model number on the sticker, usually inside the lid or on the back edge, and I take a photo of the error lights plus that sticker. I am not Googling from memory later. I want the actual lights, the actual model.

Minutes eight and nine: if I got the clothes out, they go in a bag or toward a laundromat. I do not run the dryer on sopping clothes to "help." If the door is locked, the job is unplugged, photo taken, bucket nearby, and I leave.

Minute ten: I plug it back in only if the floor is dry and I have a reason, like I suspect a glitch and want one reset. One reset. If it fills and stalls the same way, I unplug again. I do not keep restarting a machine that already told me no. Then I leave. Saturday repair cowboy hour is how people ruin a drain pump.

### User
Front-loader. Door did unlock. Clothes are in a basket now, dripping on a towel. Error is a little lock-looking light and a number I photographed. You would really just walk out after that.

### Assistant
Yes. I would walk out with the photo, the wet basket in the tub if I have a second, and my hour intact. The lock light plus a stall after fill often means the machine decided it was unhappy about a door, a drain, or a balance, not that I personally failed laundry. I am not going to interpret that code as gospel from across the room, and I am not going to quote you a part price I do not have.

What I would not do in minute eleven: take the back panel off. That is not a ten-minute job and it is how you void things and pinch a hose.

When I get home I can look up the code against the model photo. Until then the useful work is already done: power isolated once, clothes out, no standing flood, evidence saved. That is the whole Saturday-morning win. The machine can wait in the unplugged state. Wet cotton cannot.
