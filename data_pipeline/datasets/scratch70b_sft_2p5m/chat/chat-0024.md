### User
I have to call a new doctor's office in the next ten minutes or I will keep putting it off until the referral expires. Last year I sat on a similar call until the number felt haunted. I don't have an emergency. I have a referral for a first visit and a form that asks for a "chief complaint" like I am going to court. I talk too fast when I'm nervous. I don't want them to diagnose me on the phone. Walk me through the next ten minutes like you are last-year me with a slightly better spine.

### Assistant
I would not diagnose you. I also would not let the phrase "chief complaint" turn into a confession booth. It means: the main reason for the visit, in ordinary words. You can write that sentence before you dial so you do not have to invent it while someone is waiting.

Here is the next ten minutes, narrated, as if I am sitting on the kitchen floor with the phone like last year.

Minute zero: write three lines on paper before you dial. Line one: your full name and date of birth. Line two: "I have a referral for a first appointment with [doctor or clinic name], not an emergency." Line three: the chief complaint in one sentence you already understand. If you do not know the medical name, use the human name. "My knee has hurt for two months" is a reason. Do not invent a diagnosis to sound educated.

Minute one: sit where you will not lose signal. Put the paper in front of you. You are calling a scheduler, not a jury.

Minutes two to four: dial. If you hit a menu, listen for "new patient" or "appointments." If a human picks up, read line two first, then name and date of birth when they ask. You will talk fast. After the first sentence, breathe, then offer the referral. If you do not know how referrals travel, say "I don't know if you already have it. I can bring a copy." Uncertainty is allowed.

Minute five: they will ask insurance, self-pay, or a callback number. Answer with the card in your hand. If you do not have insurance, say that. I will not quote what they charge. You can ask whether the first slot is with the doctor or a nurse practitioner.

### User
What if they start asking me to describe everything on the phone and I freeze? Last year I hung up on hold and then the number felt cursed.

### Assistant
Minutes six and seven: pick a date you can actually attend. Last-year me booked the earliest slot as a dare and then canceled. Book a time that survives your work day. If they ask for a full symptom story, say, "I'd rather save the history for the visit. For scheduling, it's a referred first visit for [one sentence]." That is not hiding. That is not an exam through a speaker. Repeat the date, time, address, and what to bring — ID, insurance card, referral paper, list of medicines. Write it while they talk. Repeat it back.

Minutes eight and nine: if they put you on hold, stay. Do not hang up to "call back when you feel braver." Held music is temporary. If you get voicemail, leave name, number, date of birth, "new patient, I have a referral, please call back."

Minute ten: after you hang up, put the appointment in your calendar with the address. Text yourself the date. Drink water. Do not open a forum tab to decode the scheduler's tone. The visit is where the medical conversation belongs. The call was a door.

If your voice shakes, they have heard worse. If you cry, you can say "I'm nervous on phones" and keep going. Last year the haunted part was the delay, not the receptionist. Dial from the paper. Stay on the line even through hold music. Book the boring Thursday you can actually attend. After that you are a person with a date on a calendar, not a person circling a number.
