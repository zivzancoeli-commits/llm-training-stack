### User
It is a heatwave and I do not have air conditioning. The apartment is on the top floor. I keep seeing posts about "thermal mass" and "cross-ventilation" and "wet bulb" and I almost understand them, then my brain melts. I just want the room to stop feeling like a dryer. Translate the useful stuff into normal speech and tell me what to actually do tonight. I am not looking for a science fair.

### Assistant
You already have the right instinct. Most of those words are fancy labels for things you can feel with your hand.

Thermal mass means heavy stuff that soaks up heat slowly and dumps it later — walls, floors, a closed oven. In a top-floor box, the ceiling soaks heat all day and hands it back at night. You cannot fire the building. You can stop adding heat from inside.

Cross-ventilation means open two openings on different sides so air has a path through, not a window sitting there like a picture. One wall of windows means less of that path. A fan in the window can push hot indoor air out.

Wet bulb is weather-people talk for "how well sweat can cool you." When air is hot and very humid, sweat just sits. That is why a fan on a sticky night can feel like a hair dryer. I will not quote a cutoff number. If you feel dizzy, confused, or stop sweating when you should be a mess, that is get-help, not a blog tip.

Tonight, plain version: stop cooking if you can. Cold food. Lights off when you do not need them. Shades closed on the sunny side. After outdoor air is cooler than indoor air — usually after dusk — open what you can and run a fan to shove trapped heat out.

### User
I have one box fan and windows on one wall. The hallway is slightly cooler. Neighbors get mad if I prop the apartment door for long. Also I keep hearing "close up during the day, open at night" which I think I get, but I opened at 6 and it felt worse.

### Assistant
You opened too early. At 6 the outside can still be hotter than your rooms. Opening then invites the oven in. Regular speech: compare. Hand out the window. If outside feels hotter, keep closed. If cooler, open. A cheap thermometer helps if your hand is unsure. I will not pretend I know your city's 6 p.m. temperature.

One wall of windows: box fan in a window facing out, exhausting. Crack a second window on that wall if you have two, so air comes in one and leaves the other. Not a perfect cross-breeze. Still better than a fan in the middle of the room stirring soup.

Hallway: a few minutes with the door open while you stand there is different from propping it all evening. If neighbors are annoyed, skip the all-night door. Use the exhaust fan and a wet cloth on your neck instead of turning the corridor into your AC.

Body, not building: sip water even when you are not thirsty. Cool shower, then sit in front of the fan while you are still damp. A spray bottle works. Skip alcohol if you can; it makes the heat feel braver than you are. Not a medical order. Just what people notice.

### User
What about ice in front of the fan? And sleeping. The bedroom is the worst room. I almost had a plan and then the jargon made me feel like I was doing it wrong.

### Assistant
Ice in front of a fan is a bowl of cold that the air passes over. It can take the edge off a small space for a while. It also makes the room more humid as the ice melts, which can undo the comfort if the night is already sticky. Try it for twenty minutes. If you feel clammy instead of cooler, stop. You did not fail a technique. You ran a test.

Sleep in the least bad room, even if that is the living room floor. The bedroom being worst usually means it has more sun or less air path. Swap. A sheet, not a duvet. Head near the airflow, not under a blanket because habit says blankets.

You were not doing it wrong. You were translating. Close when outside is hotter. Open and exhaust when outside is cooler. Fan in the window pointed out. Water. Damp skin. No extra oven from the stove. Door to hallway only if people will live with it. That is the whole useful kit without the vocabulary. The words were never the test.
