### User
My kid is fifteen and wants to stay out until midnight on weekends because "everyone else does." Current curfew is ten. They are not a disaster — grades are fine, they text when they're late by ten minutes — but they roll their eyes so hard when I say no that I start doubting myself in the driveway. I do not want a speech about trust. I want a plan that does not make me the cartoon parent. What would you actually do?

### Assistant
I would not argue with "everyone else." That sentence is a fog machine. You cannot fact-check a high school. You can decide what you are willing to live with on a Saturday night.

Two options, then I will tell you which one I would pick.

Option A: keep ten as the default, and sell a later time as a trial with a return ticket. Pick one weekend night, not both. Eleven, not midnight. They tell you the address, who is bringing them home, and they answer a check-in text at a set time. If that goes clean for a stretch — more than one lucky night — you can talk about eleven becoming the weekend number. Midnight stays off the table until you have a reason that is not peer pressure. You are testing a later hour in a box you can still see.

Option B: hold ten, and spend your energy on the ride home instead of the clock. If the real fight is "I feel babied," a rigid ten with a humiliating interrogation will keep producing driveway eye-rolls. Keep the hour and still treat them like a person: they tell you the plan before they leave, you do not cross-examine their friends in the doorway, and if they will be ten minutes late they text before ten. B is for if midnight is a hard no in your gut.

I would do A. Not because fifteen-year-olds deserve midnight. A trial at eleven takes them seriously without you surrendering the whole night. A hard ten with no path turns every conversation into a verdict on their character.

Midnight is a different animal than eleven. After eleven you are dealing with tired drivers, thinner buses, and parties that have changed shape. I am not going to pretend I know your town's streets. The jump from ten to twelve is not a small courtesy.

When they bring up everyone else, answer like a skeptical relative: "I'm not parenting everyone else. I'm parenting you, and I'm willing to try eleven on Saturdays if you handle the check-in. Midnight is not on offer because a crowd stays out." A screenshot of someone else's house is still not data about your kid.

Do not buy "if you trusted me you would say yes." Trust is whether they do the small things they already agreed to: the on-time text, the real address, coming out when you arrive. If those are solid, say so. If they are not, a later curfew is you lighting your own hair on fire.

Put the trial on the family calendar. One night. One hour later. A check-in. A review date. If they blow it, you go back to ten. That is how adults run experiments when the cost of being wrong is picking someone up in the dark.

### User
They already told their friends midnight was "probably fine." Now I look like the problem.

### Assistant
You are not obligated to honor a press release they issued without you. Tell them, once, that they jumped the gun and they will have to un-tell their friends. That sting is useful. It teaches them not to spend a yes they do not have. I would still offer A if you want a path, and I would still refuse midnight. Looking like the problem to a group chat is a bad reason to change a household rule. Let them be briefly embarrassed. Then give them a real number they can actually say out loud: eleven, one night, with a text. That is a sentence a fifteen-year-old can survive.
