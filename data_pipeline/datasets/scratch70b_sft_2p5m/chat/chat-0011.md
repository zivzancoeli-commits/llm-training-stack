### User
My dad writes passwords on a sticky note in the kitchen. He also reuses the dog's name. I want to set him up with a password manager this weekend without making him feel stupid. I'm the family intern for tech. He gets quiet when I talk too fast. I need a kind, specific sequence, not a TED talk about breaches.

### Assistant
Go slow, sit beside him, not across like a help desk. One screen. His laptop or his phone, whichever he actually uses to log into email. Do not start with a horror story. Start with the sticky note: "We're going to put these in a locked list so you don't have to remember the clever ones."

Pick one manager and stay there. I will not name a winner; use whichever you already trust and can sit through with him. Install it together. He clicks. You narrate. If you click for him, he will not know the icon next week.

First account to save is email, because that is the key to reset everything else. He types the current password once while you watch the save prompt. Then you change the email password to a long one the manager generates. He does not need to see the philosophy of entropy. He needs to see that he can tap the item and it fills.

Write the vault's master password on a new card that lives in his wallet, not on the kitchen sticky. Say why in one sentence: "This one password opens the list. The list holds the rest." If he wants a phrase he can remember, help him make a long phrase that is not the dog. Do not mock the dog.

When he goes quiet, you stop talking and you point at the next button. Quiet is overload, not attitude.

What device is the sticky note actually serving, phone or the computer by the window?

### User
Mostly the window computer for banking and email. Phone is for photos and the weather. He will not do two setups in one day. Also he is going to ask if this means I can see all his passwords.

### Assistant
Honest answer, intern-plain: "If I help you install it, I might see them today while we type. After that, I should not stay logged in on my phone. This should be your vault." Then you do not add his vault to your devices unless he asks you to be a recovery person. If he does want a recovery person, say what that means: you could open the list if he is stuck. That is a family decision, not a default. Kind is not the same as secretly retaining admin rights.

One device today: the window computer. Phone is a later Sunday. Say that out loud so he is not waiting for a second marathon.

Sequence on the computer, with pauses:

1. Install. He enters his email to create the account.
2. Master password. Phrase on the wallet card. He types it twice.
3. Save email. Change email password. Confirm he can log out and back in using the fill button.
4. Save the bank site he actually uses. Do not hunt for every leftover forum from 2011.
5. Turn on the browser bit that offers to save. Show him the popup and say "yes on sites you care about, never on a computer that is not yours."
6. Delete the kitchen sticky together. That ritual matters. If he wants a paper backup of the master phrase only, the wallet card is enough.

If a site blocks paste, show him the copy button and the slow paste. Get annoyed at the bank, not at him.

Speak like this: "Click the blue button. Now the three dots. Now Save." Not "navigate to settings." Settings is a fog. Buttons are a map.

If he fails the first autofill, you do not take the mouse. You ask him to try once more. Success has to be his hand. You are allowed to smile when it works. You are not allowed to sigh when it does not.

End after email plus one bank, even if you have energy. Interns over-teach. Parents remember the last confusing step, not your completeness. Next weekend, phone, same vault, same words.

You are not making him modern. You are replacing a sticky note with a list he can lock. Kind and specific is the click path, the wallet card, the one-device limit, and the true answer about who can see the vault. That is the whole weekend. Leave the breach TED talk in the car.
