### User
It's my partner's birthday. I booked a table. The confirmation email mentioned a "celebration plate" if we tell them. My partner hates the clapping song. Last year at a different place they did it anyway and my partner went red and we left the cake. I need to tell this restaurant not to sing, and I need my in-laws, who love a fuss, not to flag a waiter behind my back. Dinner is in ninety minutes. What's the actual ask, and who do I tell?

### Assistant
Tell the restaurant twice: once now by phone, once at the host stand.

Call: "Reservation under [name], party of [n], birthday at the table. Please no song, no clapping, no staff gathering. A dessert is fine if we order it. No announcement." Get a name or a "I'll put a note." Notes fall off. A name helps.

At the host stand, the same paragraph, shorter. When your server introduces themselves, third time, one line: "Please no birthday song." Three times is not rude. It is how restaurants actually hear.

Tell the in-laws in the car or the lobby, before menus: "We're skipping the song this year. Please don't ask the staff." You can smile. You can still be glad they came. Glad and a veto can coexist.

I will not invent this restaurant's policy. Some places attach a free dessert to the song. You can still take a dessert without the choir. Ask: "Dessert without the song, please."

Does your partner know you're handling this, or are they hoping it simply won't happen?

### User
They asked me to handle it. In-laws are meeting us there, not riding with us. There will be six people. Uncle is the one who waves at waiters.

### Assistant
Text the in-laws now, group or the organizer: "Quick ask — no birthday song tonight, partner's request. We'll do dessert. Please don't flag the staff for a serenade." Early text beats a lobby ambush. Uncle can read.

If uncle still waves, you are allowed to say, across the table, "We're not doing the song." Then you catch the server's eye and shake your head. A head shake is a tool. Use it.

Seat your partner away from the kitchen door if you can, so staff approaching with a sparkler has farther to travel and you have more time to intercept. Ask for no sparkler too if that is part of their kit. Sparkler plus song is the full package. Decline the package.

If they start singing anyway, your partner can leave for the bathroom. You can stand and say "we're good, thank you" to the staff. Stopping a song mid-clap is awkward. Awkward is better than last year's red face. Staff would rather be stopped than watch someone melt.

### User
Host just said "we love birthdays here!" when I called. I think they heard "birthday" and stopped listening.

### Assistant
Call back or say it at the door with the negative first: "No song. No staff singing. Birthday in the party, silent on the floor." Lead with no. People latch on the first noun. Make the first noun the veto.

If they love birthdays, they can love a quiet one. You do not need to convince them philosophically.

Ninety minutes: text in-laws, negative-first at the host, one line to the server, watch uncle's hand. Order the dessert yourself if that keeps control. Your partner asked you to handle it. Handling it is logistics, not a speech about trauma at the table. Get them through dinner with a cake and no choir. That is a successful birthday. The restaurant can love birthdays in someone else's section.

### User
Uncle just texted "I already told them it's a birthday!!" with a party emoji.

### Assistant
Text Uncle: "Please un-tell them. No song. We're handling dessert." Then you still do the host stand with the negative first, because Uncle's message may have booked the choir. You are not being dramatic. You are correcting a flag.

If staff already have a sparkler in the back, the server line matters more. Catch them early. One shake of the head. Your partner should not have to hear the first clap and then flee. You have ninety minutes and a text to undo. Undo it. Then sit down and let the meal be a meal. The emoji can stay in Uncle's phone. The song does not have to arrive at the table.
