### User
So I host this book club and last night was a mess. People kept saying they "didn't have bandwidth" and someone called the book "not their praxis" and then we spent forty minutes on a TV show instead. I still want the group to exist. I just do not want another night where I am the only person who did the reading and everyone is doing this vague professional-speak about why they couldn't. How do I talk about that without sounding like a teacher collecting homework?

### Assistant
You almost have the real problem named already. The group has two jobs colliding: hanging out, and talking about a specific book. When the hangout wins, people reach for workplace fog because fog sounds polite. "Bandwidth" just means "I did not have time or I did not choose this." "Praxis" in that sentence usually means "this did not match how I like to think," or "I did not finish it and I am hiding behind a smart word." You do not need to mock them. You do need to translate into regular speech so the room cannot hide.

Next meeting, skip the lecture. Start plain: "I want this to stay a book club, which for me means we talk about the book we agreed to read. If most of us did not read it, we should say that in normal words and pick a different plan for the night."

If someone says they did not have bandwidth, answer like a person: "So you didn't get to it. That's fine. Were you hoping we'd still discuss, or would you rather we do a short night and pick a smaller book next time?"

If someone says the book was not their praxis, unpack it in English: "Do you mean you disagreed, you bounced off the style, or you didn't finish?" Those are three different nights. Disagreement is a good book club. Style is useful for the next title. Not finishing is a scheduling problem. The fancy word mixed those together.

Before the meeting, a group message: "If you read it, great. If you read part, say which part. If you read none, still come, but tell me so I don't plan a close-reading of chapter nine." That lets people tell the truth before they are on your couch scrambling for jargon.

Have a fork ready. Enough people read: discuss the book. Almost nobody read: do not fake a seminar. Eat, pick a shorter next book, maybe watch a clip, and name it as a social night, not as "engaging with the text." That phrase just means "talking about the pages."

If the TV show ate forty minutes, put book talk first for thirty minutes, even if it is clumsy. Then the show. Sequence, not a personality transplant.

You are allowed to say you felt stranded: "I read it and I felt a little stranded when we jumped to the show. I don't need everyone to love the book. I do want us to admit when we didn't read it."

If the group is really a hangout with a book as an excuse, you will find out when you make the deal explicit. Some people will be relieved. Some will fade. That is the club telling the truth in plain speech.

### User
What do I say in the group chat this week without sounding salty?

### Assistant
Keep it short and concrete. Something like: "Last time a lot of us hadn't read, including some of you who were being polite about it. Next book is short. If you can't do it, just say you can't — no need for a speech. We'll still meet. We'll just call it hanging out if the reading didn't happen." That translates the jargon into a plan. It also gives people a way to be honest that does not require a performance. Skip words like bandwidth, praxis, and "engaging with the text" in the message itself. If you write the invite in office fog, they will answer in office fog. You already know what you want. You want a club that can say "I didn't read" in the same language it uses to say "I did."
