### User
Uncle is in a hospital. Aunt pulled me into the hallway and said "you tell the doctor what we mean, your English is better." I am not a medical interpreter. I speak both languages at home-table level. I already missed a word last visit and everyone treated it as the official version. A resident is coming back in ten minutes. I don't want to freeze. I also don't want to be the family mouthpiece for decisions I don't understand.

### Assistant
You can help with everyday language and still refuse to be the official interpreter.

When the resident arrives, say it in the hallway before the huddle: "I can help with simple words. I'm not a medical interpreter. Please get one if you're explaining results or decisions." Hospitals often have interpreter services. I will not invent their wait time or their policy. You ask: "Can we have an interpreter for this conversation?" That's a complete sentence in English and you can say the same idea in the other language to your aunt so she hears why you're not grabbing the job.

Last visit's missed word is the reason, not an extra shame. "I got a word wrong last time. I don't want that to be the record." Fact.

You do not sign forms as a translator. You do not summarize a diagnosis. I will not diagnose your uncle. I will not guess the legal status of family translation where you are. The safer path is the official interpreter plus you as a family member who can say "she's asking about pain at night" if they still want a bridge.

Are they asking you to translate symptoms, or to talk the uncle into a procedure?

### User
Both. Aunt wants me to make him sound cooperative. Uncle is tired and said he wants to wait. She thinks wait means he doesn't understand. I think he understands and he's scared. If I "make him cooperative" I'm picking her side inside his body.

### Assistant
Don't make him cooperative. That's not language. That's pressure.

Tell the resident, with an interpreter if you can get one: he said he wants to wait. You can add that he's tired. You don't add a sales pitch. If aunt starts speaking for him, you can say, "He said wait. I'm not going to relabel that." Then you look at uncle so he can confirm.

If you have no interpreter yet, you still don't upgrade yourself. Simple bridge: "He says he wants to wait. She wants to know why the delay." Then you stop. The clinician can explain to him in small words, or they can call the service.

Scared and understanding can coexist. You are not the person who decides which. You're the person who refuses to launder his sentence.

Ten minutes: ask for interpreter, warn aunt you won't spin, stand slightly aside so they talk to him, not through your performance.

### User
Aunt will say I'm embarrassing the family by asking for a stranger to translate.

### Assistant
A stranger whose job is this is less embarrassing than a wrong word becoming a plan. You can say, "It's so we don't mess up. I'm family, not the interpreter." If she still hates it, she can be mad in the hallway. Uncle gets accurate language.

After the conversation, don't hold a second clinic in the cafeteria where you "explain what they really meant." Direct her to ask the nurse again, with the service.

You can sit with uncle quietly. Presence isn't translation. Ten minutes, one ask for the real interpreter, no cooperative spin, no fake medical certainty. That's a useful relative, not a mouthpiece.

Ask for the interpreter when the resident's shoes appear, not after the huddle has already used you. Aunt can be embarrassed for thirty seconds. Uncle gets his own sentence in a language a professional can carry.

If they never produce an interpreter, you still don't spin wait into yes. Bridge symptoms, refuse the sales pitch, don't sign. Hallway is not a clinic you run. Ten minutes, one ask, no cooperative rewrite. That's the job you actually have.
