### User
My cousin is sixteen and wants a bank account so they can get paid for a weekend job. Their parent asked me to "help them set it up" because I have a job and a debit card. I don't actually know the rules for minors. I don't want to walk into a branch pretending I'm an expert. What can I usefully do without faking it?

### Assistant
You can be the adult who admits the gaps out loud. That's more useful than a fake expert.

I don't know your state, this bank's minor-account rules, or whether a sixteen-year-old can open something alone. Those details change, and I am not going to invent a law. The shape of the help is: gather documents, walk in with questions, let the parent sign whatever the bank requires, and teach the teen how the card works after the account exists.

Ask without shame: "What account types do you offer for a sixteen-year-old? Does a parent have to be a joint owner? What IDs do you need? Are there monthly fees? Can a paycheck go in here?" Write the answers down. If the desk person is unsure, ask them to check. You can say, "I don't know this part. Can you explain it to both of us?"

Are you going with the teen alone, or is the parent coming too?

### User
Parent might come if I tell them they have to. Teen wants it to feel like their account, not a spy setup. Parent is scared of overdrafts and venmo scams. Teen thinks I'm going to lecture. I'm the intern-energy cousin, not the parent.

### Assistant
Good. Stay the cousin. Don't upgrade yourself to regulator.

Tell the parent you don't know whether the bank will let a sixteen-year-old open solo. You'll go, you'll ask, and if a guardian signature is required, the parent needs to be there or the trip is a waste. That's logistics. If the parent still won't come, you go anyway, ask the questions, and bring home the list. You don't bluff a signature you can't give.

With the teen, skip the lecture about debt. They asked for a place to put job money. Match that. You can say, "I don't know this bank's teen rules. We'll ask together. I can help you not get surprised by fees." Then talk about the two things the parent actually fears.

Overdraft: I won't quote a fee. Some accounts let you spend more than you have and then charge you; some don't. Ask whether overdraft is on or off by default, and how to keep it off if that's what the parent wants. If you don't understand the answer, say so and ask again.

Venmo and friends-asking-for-money: that's a habit, not a bank product. You don't know every app's current teen policy. Safer move: don't send money to people they haven't met, don't share the card number in a chat, tell a parent if someone pressures them.

If the teen wants the account to feel theirs, ask the bank whether the teen can have their own login and card even if a parent is joint. I don't know the answer. That's why you ask.

### User
What do I do in the branch if they start selling us a credit card or a "starter product" I don't recognize?

### Assistant
You say, "We're here for a basic place to put a paycheck. I'm not going to decide on extra products today." Then you look at the teen so they hear that they don't have to nod along. If the banker uses words you don't know, ask for the plain version: "Does this let you spend more than is in the account? Is there a monthly fee if the balance is low?" If they can't make it plain, you don't sign.

Bring the job offer or a paystub if they have one, plus whatever ID the parent already uses. If you're missing a document, leave and come back. Opening a wrong account because you were already in the chair is how people get stuck.

After it exists, sit on a bench: how to check the balance, how a deposit works, what to do if the card is lost. If you don't know this bank's app, download it with them and learn it in real time. Saying "I haven't used this one" is allowed.

You were asked to help, not to know. Help is questions, a parent if required, and a no to extras. That's a complete job for a cousin.
