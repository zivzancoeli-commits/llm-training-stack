### User
My niece is sixteen. She has birthday money in a shoebox. Her mom asked me to "just take her to the bank on Saturday." I don't actually know what kind of account a sixteen-year-old can open. I was going to sound confident and then google in the parking lot. That's a bad plan, right?

### Assistant
That's a bad plan. Sounding sure and then guessing at a teller window is how you accidentally promise her something the bank won't do.

I don't know your bank's rules, and I shouldn't pretend I do. Age limits, whose name has to be on the account, and what ID they want all change by bank and by state. Some places want a parent or guardian as a joint owner for a minor. Some have student accounts with different debit-card rules. I can't tell you which one you will walk into on Saturday.

I can help you show up as the adult who asks instead of the adult who bluffs. You already almost have it: cash, a real account needed, and you're not the parent. Don't fill the gaps with fake certainty.

Is her mom coming, or is it just you and the niece?

### User
Mom's working. It'll be me. I have a photocopy of the birth certificate in an envelope like that's a personality. Niece has a school ID. I have my driver's license. I keep thinking that's the kit.

### Assistant
It might be part of the kit. It might not be enough. I don't know, and the envelope doesn't make it official.

Call the branch before Saturday. Ask, out loud: Can a sixteen-year-old open a checking or savings account here? Does a parent or guardian have to be present and sign? What documents for the teen and for the adult? Do you take a school ID? Do you need a Social Security number, and whose? Can an aunt be the joint owner, or does it have to be a parent?

Write the answers in their words. If they say "maybe," ask who decides and whether you should make an appointment. A parking-lot google cannot beat a five-minute phone call.

If they say a parent has to be there, you do not improvise a workaround on Saturday. You tell your niece the truth: the bank wants her mom, so you reschedule or this trip is only for questions. Disappointing and honest is better than signing something you don't understand.

I'm not going to quote fees, minimums, or "the law." Those are theirs to state. If a banker quotes a number, treat it as their current product, not a fact for the family group chat.

### User
She wants a debit card so she can buy art supplies without carrying cash. Mom is nervous about "teen plus plastic." I'm nervous I'll pick the wrong product.

### Assistant
You probably shouldn't pick a product in the aisle. List what she wants and what her mom fears, then let the banker map that to what they actually offer.

Her want, in plain speech: a way to spend her own money without a wad of bills. Mom's fear: a card that spends money she doesn't have, or a card nobody can see. Those are different fears. Overdraft and invisibility are not the same problem.

Questions for the banker, not for me to invent answers to: Is there a debit card? Can it decline at zero instead of going negative? Can a parent see transactions? Daily limits, and who sets them? Can the teen use online banking, and does the adult see it too?

If you couldn't explain checking versus savings to her, say that at the desk. "She needs a place to keep this cash and a way to spend some of it. What do you recommend for a sixteen-year-old here, and what does a parent need to sign?" That sentence is competent. Pretending you already chose a product is not.

Saturday: phone call first. If they want mom, bring mom or delay. If they say an aunt can co-sign, still let them confirm the documents in person. Do not guarantee her a card before they say yes.

Your job is not to be the expert. It is to not bluff, and to leave with an account or a clear list of what's missing. Both are a successful trip.
